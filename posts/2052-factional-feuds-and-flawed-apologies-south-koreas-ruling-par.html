Factional Feuds and Flawed Apologies: South Korea’s Ruling Party Stumbles Over Its Activist Past

November 06, 2026

A half-hearted apology by senior ruling party lawmaker Kim Min-seok over controversial ties to the Shincheonji religious sect has ignited a fierce internal rebellion within the Democratic Party of Korea, laying bare the toxic factional tactics inherited from South Korea's 1980s student activist movement.

  • The trigger: Kim Min-seok dismissed his controversial handling of allegations tied to Shincheonji—a secretive religious sect frequently embroiled in political scandals—as a routine "political misjudgment," sparking outrage among party members for dodging genuine accountability.
  • The suppression: Factional allies like lawmaker Kim Young-jin urged critics to drop the matter to focus on "public livelihoods," while leadership figures weaponized the historical self-sacrifice of former party leader Jung Chung-rae to demand absolute silence from internal dissenters.
  • The cultural roots: Dissenting voices argue the party is repeating the vintage playbook of 1980s student activist factions: smear opponents during backroom fights, refuse to apologize, and force arbitrary "unity" once power is secured.
  • The broader fallout: As the governing party behind President Lee Jae-myung, the Democratic Party risks crippling its reform agenda by substituting genuine intra-party democracy with top-down obedience.

Weaponizing "Livelihoods" and Sacrifices to Stifle Dissent

Kim’s decision to hide behind the vague label of a "political misjudgment" reveals a persistent arrogance in South Korean party politics. Rather than offering a forthright admission of error regarding dealings with Shincheonji, the party leadership pivoted immediately to calls for focus on the everyday economy. Critics rightly see this as cynical misdirection: the very figures who triggered the scandal are now using "the public livelihood" as a shield to brand legitimate internal oversight as destructive party infighting.

Even more troubling is how the leadership has appropriated the party's own history. When former party leader Jung Chung-rae was unfairly cut from the candidate roster in a past general election, he nonetheless campaigned nationwide for the ticket. Today, that noble precedent is being twisted into a cudgel: party insiders cite Jung's past submission to argue that current critics should simply swallow grievances and fall in line. Converting an act of personal sacrifice into an instrument of party coercion undermines the very ethics the progressives claim to uphold.

The 30-Year Shadow of Student Activist Politics

This dynamic is not an accidental misstep; it is the direct legacy of the 1980s democratization movement's radical student factions. During that era, rivalry between the nationalist "National Liberation" (NL) and Marxist-leaning "People's Democracy" (PD) wings was defined by vicious rumors, character assassination, and unapologetic power grabs. Crucially, the student-movement style always ended the same way: after decimating rivals, the victors demanded unconditional reconciliation under the banner of a "single team," skipping any real atonement.

Three decades later, these student-union habits are being played out on the national stage by the governing party. For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung to govern effectively, the Democratic Party needs rigorous internal debate and frank accountability, not a vertical culture of enforced silence. Clinging to activist-era factional warfare and artificial apologies will only alienate voters who expect the ruling party to govern with maturity.
