South Korea's Foreign Policy Under Fire: Navigating US Pressure and Seeking European Allies

September 10, 2026

South Korea faces a high-stakes foreign policy test as Washington ramps up pressure for military involvement in the Strait of Hormuz and pushes aggressive semiconductor tariffs, forcing Seoul to weigh traditional US alliance demands against its own economic and security interests.

  • The US Push: President Donald Trump has publicly criticized South Korea for declining to join US-led efforts in the Strait of Hormuz regarding Iran, mirroring the contentious dynamics of the 2003 Iraq War deployment.
  • Semiconductor Tariffs: US officials have signaled that non-US chip producers face severe tariff walls unless they shift manufacturing to the US, threatening South Korea's core export engine.
  • The European Pivot: President Lee Jae-myung's recent state visit to France aims to diversify diplomatic, space, defense, and cultural ties away from absolute US dependency.
  • Domestic Reform Pressures: Progressive factions and lawmakers are pushing for electoral law revisions to curb prosecutorial overreach while managing internal political fragmentation.

The convergence of Washington's demands on Iran and microchips presents Seoul with a stark reminder of the limits of unconditional alignment. Iran has warned that South Korean naval participation in the Strait of Hormuz would make Seoul a direct participant in hostilities, risking diplomatic and economic ties across the Middle East. Meanwhile, trade rhetoric from US officials evokes memories of the 1980s US-Japan trade wars, threatening to subordinate South Korea's high-tech industries to American industrial policy.

President Lee's state visit to France offers a blueprint for navigating this squeeze. By coordinating with European powers seeking strategic autonomy, South Korea is exploring joint space-defense drills and cultural alliances—such as film industry cooperation—to build leverage. While these diplomatic openings do not instantly neutralize American pressure, they expand Seoul's strategic breathing room, proving that national security and economic survival do not require blind obedience.

Domestic Reforms and the Path Forward

Beyond geopolitics, domestic political battles are intensifying over judicial reform. Critics argue that vague legal definitions within South Korea's Public Official Election Act have long allowed prosecutors outsized political leverage. Lawmakers and reform advocates are pushing for legislative overhauls—such as removing ambiguous clauses and addressing past prosecutorial overreach—to prevent the justice system from being weaponized in partisan fights.

Ultimately, South Korea's success through these compounding crises depends on resisting short-term appeasement. Whether dealing with Middle Eastern military entanglements, semiconductor protectionism, or domestic legal reform, Seoul must prioritize long-term institutional independence and diversified global partnerships to safeguard its democratic future.
