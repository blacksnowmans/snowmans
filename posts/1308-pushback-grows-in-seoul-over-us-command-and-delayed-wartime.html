Pushback Grows in Seoul Over US Command and Delayed Wartime Military Control

October 19, 2026

Controversial remarks by US Forces Korea (USFK) commander Gen. Xavier Brunson have sparked sharp pushback in Seoul, supercharging demands for the government of President Lee Jae-myung to set a strict 2027 deadline to reclaim wartime operational command from Washington.

  • The territorial dispute: Speaking at a Washington think-tank seminar, Gen. Brunson remarked from the UN Command perspective that South Korea "starts at the southern fence"—contradicting Article 3 of the South Korean Constitution, which defines the nation’s sovereign territory as the entire Korean Peninsula and its adjacent islands.
  • The 2027 OPCON deadline: Critics argue the US-led "conditions-based" wartime operational control (OPCON) transition is an indefinite trap, demanding that President Lee Jae-myung's administration bypass subjective military assessments and declare complete OPCON recovery by late 2027.
  • Invoking the SOFA removal clause: Security analysts and former diplomats are urging Seoul to deploy Article 8, Clause 6 of the US–South Korea Status of Forces Agreement (SOFA), a binding provision that obligates the US to withdraw personnel at Seoul's request without proving cause.
  • Entanglement in regional proxy fights: Skeptics warn that US proposals for South Korea to lead "sensing" operations under the US Army's Multi-Domain Task Force (MDTF) are a cover to deploy "Dark Eagle" hypersonic missiles aimed at China, while cautioning against expanding the Cheonghae naval unit's missions in Middle Eastern flashpoints.

Breaking the Endless Transition Cycle

South Korea remains the only major US ally whose armed forces revert to American command during wartime—an exceptional status dating back to the 1950 Korean War. Under the current framework, handing back OPCON requires meeting three core criteria: certified combined defense capabilities, early counterstrike capabilities against North Korean nuclear assets, and a stable regional security environment. Because the Pentagon alone evaluates whether these conditions are met, security hawks in Seoul view the mechanism as a moving goalpost designed to keep the South dependent on American weapons systems and strategic oversight.

For President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, shaking off the deferential security posture that defined bilateral diplomacy during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term has become an urgent mandate. Gen. Brunson’s scheduled tenure runs through late 2027, offering a natural geopolitical deadline. Reclaiming operational command would place a South Korean four-star general at the head of the Combined Forces Command, recalibrating the USFK commander's position into a administrative support role similar to the US commander in Japan.

Strategic Forward Base or Sovereign Partner?

The dispute extends into broader regional alignments. Washington's vision for integrated deterrence increasingly pushes South Korea onto the front lines of containment against China. While the Pentagon packages the Multi-Domain Task Force around advanced intelligence and sensing capabilities, its operational core includes long-range hypersonic strike systems. Hosting such assets risks turning the Korean Peninsula into an immediate target in a superpower conflict, with Seoul bearing the full economic and military retaliation.

Similar risks surround calls to fold South Korea’s anti-piracy Cheonghae naval unit into US-led patrols targeting Houthi forces in the Red Sea and Persian Gulf. While securing maritime shipping lanes is critical for trade, joining an offensive US coalition invites asymmetric retaliation from regional powers like Iran. Navigating these pressures requires the Lee administration to assert decisive sovereign limits rather than rubber-stamping Pentagon doctrine.
