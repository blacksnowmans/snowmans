Internal Civil War: Why the Democratic Party is Attacking Its Own Governor

October 14, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has descended into a bitter internal feud as lawmakers loyal to President Lee Jae-myung turned a routine government audit into a public firing squad against Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae.

  • Internal Sabotage: During the National Assembly’s audit of Gyeonggi Province—the country’s most populous region—DPK lawmakers launched more aggressive attacks on Governor Choo than the conservative opposition did.
  • The Nominee Conflict: The friction stems from Choo’s vocal opposition to Kim Ji-yong, the President’s nominee to lead the newly formed Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO), a centerpiece of judicial reform.
  • Betrayal of Principles: Choo argues that Kim is unfit to lead a reformist agency because he previously supported the now-impeached Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during his controversial tenure as prosecutor general.
  • Electoral Fallout: Recent polling shows the DPK’s approval rating has plummeted to 35.5%, falling behind the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) at 41.3% amid accusations of party arrogance.

The MCIO Paradox and the Loyalty Test

The Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO) was designed to be the final step in stripping the state prosecution of its unchecked investigative powers. For the DPK base, the agency’s first head must be a reformer. However, President Lee Jae-myung’s pick, Kim Ji-yong, has a history of resisting these very reforms. During the 2020 disciplinary proceedings against then-Prosecutor General Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, Kim was among the high-ranking prosecutors who signed a collective statement in support of Mr. Yoon.

Governor Choo Mi-ae, a former Justice Minister who led the initial charge against Mr. Yoon, has called the appointment a "historical mistake." In response, the DPK leadership has labeled her dissent as "internal sniping." During the audit, DPK lawmakers ignored provincial policy to berate Choo for "challenging the President’s authority." This suggests a party culture where blind loyalty to the Blue House now outweighs the ideological consistency of judicial reform. By trying to silence Choo, the party is effectively signaling that the "pro-Lee" faction’s personnel choices are beyond public scrutiny.

The Price of Political Arrogance

The DPK’s internal crackdown comes at a moment of extreme vulnerability. The party’s approval ratings are in freefall, yet the leadership remains dismissive. Veteran DPK figures have been quoted on popular podcasts suggesting that even if voters are disappointed in the DPK, they "cannot possibly vote for the People Power Party." This "hostage-taking" strategy—assuming the liberal base has nowhere else to go—is clearly backfiring as young voters move toward the "unaffiliated" column or stay home entirely.

Choo Mi-ae remains a heavyweight who won her governorship with 55% of the vote (roughly 3.76 million votes) and holds particularly strong support among women in their 20s and 30s. By treating her as a rogue element for defending the original goals of the "Candlelight Revolution," the DPK risks alienating the very demographic that put them in power. If the party continues to prioritize a "loyalty-first" culture over the substantive reforms they promised, they may find that the "lesser of two evils" argument will no longer save them at the ballot box.
