South Korea’s Local Leaders Press Seoul for Greater Autonomy and Budget Freedom

November 20, 2026

On November 19, local leaders across South Korea launched a coordinated legislative push, urging the National Assembly and Prime Minister Han Seong-suk to grant municipal councils independent legal authority and devolve central government powers to regional cities.

  • What happened: Provincial council leaders lobbied parliamentary leaders in Seoul for the swift passage of the Local Council Act, while Gyeonggi Province mayors petitioned Prime Minister Han Seong-suk for local redevelopment authority and transit infrastructure support.
  • What changes: Enacting the Local Council Act would grant local legislative bodies independent personnel management and budgeting rights, severing their administrative reliance on municipal executives.
  • Who is affected: Local councils and municipal governments nationwide, including industrial hubs like Yongin, satellite cities like Ansan and Bucheon, and the administrative capital of Sejong.

Demands for Autonomy Reach the National Assembly

For years, South Korea's local councils have operated under a structural handicap: despite acting as watchdog bodies over city mayors and provincial governors, their staffing and operational budgets remain under the control of the executive branch. On November 19, Kim Hee-soo, chairman of the Gyeongsangbuk-do Provincial Council, and Park Jun, chairman of the Gyeongsangnam-do Provincial Council, traveled to the National Assembly in Seoul. Kim met with Jung Jeom-sik, floor leader of the People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition party, to demand the immediate passage of the pending Local Council Act before the year ends.

The bill, which recently cleared the subcommittee of the National Assembly's Public Administration and Security Committee, aims to provide local legislative bodies with self-governing administrative power. On the same day, during a meeting hosted by Prime Minister Han Seong-suk, mayors from major cities in Gyeonggi Province voiced frustration over central control. Yongin Mayor Lee Sang-il requested three new railway lines to support a global semiconductor cluster, while leaders from Ansan, Suwon, and Bucheon urged Seoul to delegate urban renewal authority so municipalities can overhaul aging housing developments without drawn-out central government approvals.

Bipartisan Friction and Financial Battles in the Regions

The push for regional autonomy comes as municipal leaders fight for fiscal survival amid shrinking local revenues. Sejong City gained a temporary reprieve when a parliamentary subcommittee approved a three-year extension of its special financial grant status. However, most local governments remain dependent on central budget allocations, forcing mayors and council heads to travel to Seoul annually to lobby for basic transit and development funding.

Political gridlock at the local level continues to complicate these self-governance efforts. In Gangwon Province, council members from the opposition People Power Party staged a collective walkout during an executive policy speech, prompting strong criticism from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. In contrast, the Busan Municipal Council held its second bipartisan cooperation meeting, attempting to resolve regional policy issues through negotiation. As South Korea's local councils demand greater legal independence, their success will depend not only on legislative reform in Seoul, but also on their ability to overcome partisan conflict at home.
