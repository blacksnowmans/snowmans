The Perilous Path of South Korean Prosecutor Reform

September 22, 2026

  • The Core Issue: Despite President Lee Jae-myung’s push for systemic reform, critics argue that the administration remains dominated by former prosecutors who have effectively captured key state institutions.
  • The "Choco Pie" Problem: Reformers argue the presidential office responsible for civil affairs (Minjeong) has lost its "heart"—the ability to empathize with public sentiment—becoming instead a shield for an entrenched insider circle.
  • Institutional Capture: Observers point to the appointment of former prosecutors to major financial and intelligence agencies, such as the Financial Supervisory Service, as evidence that the state is operating more like a "prosecutor-led" apparatus than a balanced democracy.
  • The Litmus Test: The nomination of Kim Ji-yong as head of the newly proposed Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA) is viewed as a critical test of the administration's true commitment to dismantling the prosecutor-led status quo.

The recent discourse surrounding South Korean politics, highlighted by discussions on the Hong Sa-hun Show featuring politician Shin Jang-sik and former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook, underscores a profound public exhaustion. The central frustration is that while the administration promises change, the personnel currently filling the halls of power—those designing policy, those inspecting it, and those verifying it—are almost exclusively from the prosecution service. This "closed-loop" structure has left many citizens feeling betrayed, viewing the state as an asset belonging to a specific group rather than the public.

President Lee’s recent 90-minute press conference was an attempt to shift this narrative, particularly through his signaled focus on fact-finding in special investigations rather than pursuing controversial indictments. While this shift away from the weaponization of the law is welcomed by reformists, skepticism remains high. The political reality is that reform cannot be achieved through rhetoric alone; it requires irreversible institutional changes, such as forcing the return of the hundreds of prosecutors currently seconded to non-prosecution agencies like financial regulators and labor offices.

The Trap of Palace Politics

A recurring theme in the critique is the danger of "palace politics"—the influence of unelected figures who whisper in the President's ear. Observers warn that if the administration relies on these unofficial power brokers rather than transparent, systemic governance, it will inevitably repeat the failures of its predecessors. True reform demands a clean break from this culture of private influence, which has historically sabotaged even the most well-intentioned policy agendas.

Ultimately, the upcoming confirmation process for the MCIA leadership will serve as a definitive indicator. If the administration forces through a candidate who is perceived as part of the old guard or who refuses to account for past actions—such as signing "loyalty oaths" (yepan-jang) common within the prosecution—the public's remaining trust will likely evaporate. For the President, the path forward is narrow: he must stop the infighting between the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party, cut ties with the "palace courtiers" surrounding him, and translate his words into tangible, permanent institutional reform.
