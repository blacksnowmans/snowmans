Prosecution Leaks and Street Extremism: South Korea’s Democratic Test

October 20, 2026

South Korea’s state prosecution is facing renewed scrutiny over politically targeted leak investigations, following a broadcast report suggesting former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon could face legal liability over selectively released prosecution records.

  • The leak inquiry: A recent report by broadcaster MBC highlighted allegations that internal records and indictments from the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office were leaked selectively, exposing the agency to charges of unlawful disclosure of public secrets.
  • Targeting political figures: The controversy directly implicates Han Dong-hoon, the former justice minister under the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was removed from office via impeachment and remains imprisoned—raising questions about selective enforcement by law enforcement bodies.
  • Street-level tensions: Alongside battles in the legal system, recent clashes near Seoul’s Olympic Park, where hardline demonstrators attempted unauthorized searches and vigilante-style intimidation against passersby, have sparked alarm over democratic order.
  • The government’s stance: The administration of President Lee Jae-myung, who leads the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, faces the dual challenge of reforming entrenched prosecutorial practices while cracking down on fringe street violence without eroding assembly rights.

Selective Justice and the Shadow of Authoritarian Practices

The dispute over leaked indictments at the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office cuts to the core of institutional trust in South Korea’s judicial system. Under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, prosecutors routinely faced accusations of leaking investigative details to friendly media outlets while aggressively pursuing critics under the guise of protecting official secrets. Commentators have drawn parallels between this selective application of secrecy laws and the authoritarian-era charge of "defamation of the head of state" used during the Park Chung-hee dictatorship to stifle dissent.

The current legal debate tests whether the justice system can apply a single standard to powerful insiders. When state organs leak investigative materials for political leverage and shield their own officials from scrutiny, the rule of law collapses into partisan warfare. Addressing past abuses from the Yoon era requires establishing clear statutory boundaries for official secrets, preventing prosecutors from weaponizing access to information against political opponents.

Defending Korea's Tradition of Peaceful Assembly

Meanwhile, the emergence of aggressive demonstrations near Olympic Park signals an unsettling departure from South Korea's established protest culture. Since the late 1990s, when the Kim Dae-jung administration ended the use of tear gas and protesters abandoned firebombs, Korean civic demonstrations—such as the massive candlelight vigils that defined modern civic resistance—have maintained strict nonviolence, avoiding the looting and property destruction seen in recent French unrest.

Private vigilante actions, such as demonstrators demanding to search citizens' belongings or staging near-lynchings of ideological opponents, cross the line from protected speech into criminal intimidation. For the Lee administration, upholding democratic integrity requires a firm balance: safeguarding the constitutional right to protest while strictly holding extremists and abusive state institutions accountable under the law.
