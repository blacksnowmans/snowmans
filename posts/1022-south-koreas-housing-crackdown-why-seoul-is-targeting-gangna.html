South Korea’s Housing Crackdown: Why Seoul is Targeting Gangnam

September 23, 2026

South Korea's conservative Yoon Suk Yeol administration is doubling down on aggressive real estate regulations aimed squarely at dismantling the "Gangnam-centric" property market, risking severe side effects for lower-income renters and non-apartment sectors.

  • The Target: Recent policy packages—including tough property tax adjustments and capital gains tax changes—are explicitly designed to break the "one quality home" trend by targeting high-end real estate in Gangnam, Seoul's wealthiest district.
  • The Side Effect: While Gangnam prices face downward pressure, a classic "balloon effect" is driving up prices in non-Gangnam districts like Nowon-gu, while the non-apartment market (villas and row houses) faces systemic collapse following the fallout from rental fraud scandals.
  • Who is Affected: Everyday renters and lower-income families are bearing the brunt of surging rental costs and a severe supply squeeze, even as the government funnels massive liquidity—such as a reported 40 trillion won (about $29 billion) intervention in 2023—to prevent a domino collapse in primary developments.

The Illusion of Artificial Equalization

The core philosophy of the current administration's housing strategy relies on a top-down equalization of property values. By squeezing luxury homeowners in wealthy enclaves like Gangnam and Banpo, policymakers hope to level the playing field across the Seoul metropolitan area. However, market economics routinely override political intentions. When high-end properties are artificially suppressed through heavy tax burdens and transaction permits, capital simply migrates to unregulated adjacent districts, sending secondary prices soaring by over 20% in some areas.

This dynamic exposes a fundamental disconnect between state intervention and market reality. While government studies point to short-term success in holding down prices in designated regulated zones, the long-term impact follows an inverted U-curve where pent-up demand eventually breaks through. More critically, the policy tunnel vision—obsessed with puncturing high-end asset bubbles—largely ignores the fracturing rental and construction pipelines that shelter lower-income households.

The Forgotten Crisis in Non-Apartments

While the market for luxury Seoul apartments remains relatively resilient, the non-apartment sector—villas, multi-family units, and townhouses—is experiencing a structural depression. South Korea's unique jeonse (lump-sum housing deposit) system acted as the primary financing engine for these smaller properties. Following widespread rental scams, tenant trust in the system collapsed, drying up the developer financing that kept non-apartment construction alive.

Government supply targets for non-apartments fall woefully short of actual urban demand, setting the stage for a steep surge in rental prices for the city's poorest residents. Simply put, punishing speculative luxury buyers does nothing to solve the basic shelter needs of a young worker living in a semi-basement or rooftop unit. Until policymakers shift focus from political score-settling over elite real estate to stabilizing foundational rental and construction markets, housing stability will remain out of reach for ordinary South Koreans.
