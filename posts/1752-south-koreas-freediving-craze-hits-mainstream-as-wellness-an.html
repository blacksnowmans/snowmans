South Korea’s Freediving Craze Hits Mainstream as Wellness and Controversy Reshape the Sport

October 31, 2026

Freediving is rapidly transitioning from an extreme niche sport into a mainstream mental wellness movement in South Korea, driven by candid celebrity disclosures and a growing emphasis on underwater meditation over risky competition.

  • What happened: South Korean actress Yoo In-young sparked widespread media coverage after publicly documenting her training and candidly admitting her failure to pass the entry-level AIDA2 (International Association for the Development of Apnea) certification due to equalizing issues.
  • What it changes: Mainstream media and elite Korean athletes are refocusing the domestic freediving narrative away from depth chasing toward sensory recovery, stress relief, and strict safety limits.
  • Global context: International headlines—including a record 29-minute oxygen-assisted breath-hold by a Croatian diver and labor abuse allegations against American influencer Ocean Ramsey—are sparking deeper debates on ethics and safety within local diver networks.
  • Who is affected: Recreational divers, fitness enthusiasts, lifestyle brands, and training academies catering to Koreans seeking relief from hyper-competitive urban stress.

Mindfulness Meets Modern Burnout

In South Korea, where relentless workplace competition and high stress levels are routine, freediving has found an unlikely second life as a mental reset. The sport requires participants to slow their heartbeat, suppress panic, and embrace complete stillness underwater. Actress Yoo In-young’s candid social media posts detailing her frustration with equalizing pressure and her ultimate failure to earn her AIDA2 credential struck a deep chord with fans. By breaking away from the typical curated perfection of Korean celebrity culture, her open experience reframed freediving as an exercise in confronting personal limits and practicing self-acceptance.

This shift toward psychological well-being is increasingly reflected in domestic media. Profiles of local freediving professionals in publications like GQ Korea emphasize that rising back to the surface safely is far more important than reaching arbitrary depth markers. For many Korean enthusiasts, submersing themselves into silent water offers a rare refuge from digital overload—making the practice less about athletic bravado and more about quiet internal discipline.

Ethics, Safety, and Global Community Insights

As freediving gains traction across East Asia, domestic communities are keeping a close eye on international standards and safety ethics. Allegations reported by media outlet Dexerto against American shark conservationist Ocean Ramsey—accusing her of forcing staff into unsafe diving conditions and demanding unpaid labor—have served as a cautionary tale for South Korea’s expanding dive school market, reinforcing that buddy safety and strict protocols must override social media performance.

At the grass-roots level, online communities on platforms like Reddit are helping beginners troubleshoot technical barriers, such as throat closure during ascents and selecting low-volume masks. Whether through viral clips of diving alongside marine life or technical gear discussions, the focus among Korean freedivers remains clear: long-term enjoyment of the ocean requires patience, proper gear, and respect for physiological boundaries.
