South Korea Appeals Court Cuts Kim Keon-hee's Sentence to Five Years, Clears Her of Luxury Watch Bribery

September 24, 2026

South Korea’s appellate court has reduced the prison sentence of Kim Keon-hee—wife of former President Yoon Suk Yeol—from seven years to five, overturning a key bribery conviction regarding an expensive luxury watch.

  • Sentence reduced: The Seoul High Court cut Kim's prison term for aggravated mediation bribery from seven to five years.
  • Watch charge dropped: A charge involving a Vacheron Constantin watch valued at about $30,000 (39.9 million won) was overturned after the court accepted her claim that it was a "purchase proxy" rather than a bribe tied to state favors.
  • Dangerous precedent: Lawmakers and critics warn the ruling creates a loophole, suggesting defendants can evade bribery charges simply by reimbursing costs just before trial deadlines or claiming ignorance of corrupt intentions.
  • Public backlash: Opposition politicians, including Hwang Un-ha of the Rebuilding Korea Party and Park Joo-min of the Democratic Party, slammed the ruling as a double standard that shields political elites while eroding judicial credibility.

A Dangerous Precedent for White-Collar Crime

The appellate court's logic hinges on treating the luxury timepiece as a commissioned purchase rather than illicit quid pro quo. Despite evidence that payment was only made right before the first trial's closing arguments—a timeline that prosecutors argued pointed to evidence tampering or a cover-up—the court gave weight to the defendant's narrative. By ruling that prosecutors failed to definitively prove Kim recognized the bribe's corrupt intent, the bench effectively placed an unreasonable burden of proof on the state while handing public figures a blueprint to dodge accountability.

Critics from opposition parties were quick to point out the glaring disparity between this lenient interpretation and the harsh penalties routinely handed down to ordinary citizens or political opponents over lesser offenses. When courts bend over backwards to validate improbable excuses for the powerful, it shatters the foundational principle that the law applies equally to all. This ruling does not just cut a prison sentence; it signals to future wrongdoers that clever legal maneuvering and last-minute repayments can sanitize corrupt transactions.
