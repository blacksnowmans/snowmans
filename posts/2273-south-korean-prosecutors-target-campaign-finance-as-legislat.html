South Korean Prosecutors Target Campaign Finance as Legislative Battles Intensify

November 13, 2026

Prosecutors have indicted six officials from the 2021 presidential primary campaign of Hong Joon-pyo, the conservative Mayor of Daegu, over allegations of illegal fund management.

  • Six members of Hong’s camp face charges for illegally spending approximately 200 million won (about $143,000) during the primary race.
  • The ruling Democratic Party (DP) is pushing a new "Equality Act" that replaces criminal jail time with financial penalties to bypass religious opposition.
  • Lawmakers from both sides have tentatively agreed to postpone the long-delayed taxation on cryptocurrency gains for an additional two years.
  • The People Power Party (PPP), the main opposition, is pressuring the government to abolish a controversial tax on reconstruction profits to revive the sluggish housing market.

Accountability Reaches Back to the 2021 Primaries

The indictment of Hong Joon-pyo’s staff serves as a stark reminder that South Korea’s Political Funds Act—the law governing how campaigns raise and spend money—remains a minefield for high-profile politicians. Prosecutors allege that the camp bypassed legal spending limits through improper accounting, a charge that challenges the transparency of internal party elections. This follows the finalized suspended sentence for former lawmaker Lee Sang-heon on similar corruption charges, signaling a broader judicial cleanup of "money politics." In a political landscape where public trust is thin, these cases emphasize that financial integrity in elections is no longer a mere formality but a potential career-ending liability for both established veterans and their staffers.

Legislative Deadlocks in the Lee Jae-myung Administration

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the National Assembly is grappling with bills that pit administrative efficiency against civil liberties. The proposed Equality Act, led by DP Representative Park Ju-min, attempts to shield marginalized groups from discrimination. However, it faces fierce resistance from religious organizations who argue that the threat of "performance bonds"—essentially heavy fines—will stifle freedom of expression. Similarly, a plan to grant the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), the state-run insurer, special police powers to investigate illegal "office-run" hospitals has unified the medical community in protest against what they call state overreach. While the government aims to stop the drain on public funds, critics fear these special investigative powers could be abused without proper judicial oversight.

The Lee administration is also navigating a complex economic agenda. The two-year delay in cryptocurrency taxation offers a temporary reprieve for the country’s estimated six million retail investors, but it highlights the government's struggle to create a stable regulatory framework. Similarly, the debate over the "Reconstruction Excess Profit Restitution Act"—a tax designed to curb property speculation by reclaiming development gains—remains a flashpoint. While the ruling DP is wary of "tax cuts for the wealthy," the opposition PPP argues that the levy is stalling necessary urban development. For the current government, balancing these populist economic pressures with structural reform remains the defining challenge of the legislative session.
