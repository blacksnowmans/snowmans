South Korea’s Nationwide Farmland Crackdown Sparks Panic and Reform Debate

September 27, 2026

South Korea's first-ever nationwide survey of agricultural land has triggered widespread anxiety among elderly farmers and heirs fearing government land grabs, forcing the Ministry of Agriculture to push back against a wave of viral misinformation.

  • The catalyst:
    •  Following public outrage over real estate speculation by public officials, the government launched a comprehensive database survey covering 1.36 million hectares (about 3.4 million acres) of farmland with changed ownership since 1996.
  • The flashpoint:
    •  Initial findings flagged 27% of surveyed plots for potential violations—such as illegal leasing or unapproved fallow periods—sparking panic that landowners would be forcibly stripped of their property.
  • The reality:
    •  Agriculture Minister Song Mi-ryung clarified that the flagged figures are targets for in-depth review, not immediate penalties, while the state moves to protect legitimate heirs and elderly farmers.
  • The policy shift:
    •  Authorities are cracking down on real estate speculators and corporate loopholes while expanding the state-run Farmland Bank’s budget by 25% to about $1.6 billion (2.26 trillion won) to buy up unmanaged land for young farmers.

Untangling the 27% Alarm: Rules vs. Rural Reality

The core friction stems from South Korea's aging countryside crashing against the country's 30-year-old Farmland Act. Rooted in the constitutional principle of gyeongjajuyun—the idea that only those who farm should own farmland—the legal framework assumes able-bodied owners are actively working their plots. With nearly 70% of farmers now aged 65 or older and a massive surge in inherited land, applying this rule strictly has become nearly impossible.

When the initial data showed 27% of ownership-transferred plots flagged for potential violations—mostly unconfirmed leases or idle land—it created a public relations disaster. Farmers braced for immediate expropriation. However, the ministry emphasizes that the checks are meant to build an accurate database and catch deliberate abuse, not punish minor administrative slip-ups. To ease tensions, the government allows inherited plots up to 1 hectare (about 2,500 to 3,000 pyeong) to be legally leased out, shielding ordinary families from harsh penalties.

Targeting Speculators While Rethinking the Future of Farming

While ordinary landowners are being offered breathing room, the government is drawing a hard line against actual speculators. Officials are targeting three main abuses: fake farming plans submitted inside land-trade permitted zones, agricultural corporations operating real estate or storage businesses instead of farming, and speculative syndicates carving up massive plots into tiny fractional shares. These offenders face strict correction orders and heavy fines.

At the same time, the broader debate points to a necessary evolution away from rigid ownership dogmas toward practical land use—often dubbed gyeongjayongjeon (those who use the land manage it). As rural depopulation accelerates, policymakers face the urgent task of transforming stagnant plots into viable hubs for younger generations without letting farmland devolve into a playground for speculators.
