How Generative AI is Breaking South Korea's Traditional Education Model

October 09, 2026

As generative artificial intelligence handles everything from writing essays to summarizing complex research, South Korea's education system is facing a profound identity crisis over what students actually need to learn.

  • The core issue: Elite universities like Seoul National University and Yonsei University have seen cheating scandals involving AI, while professors warn that automated tools are robbing students of critical thinking and foundational problem-solving skills.
  • The expert take: In a recent discussion, University of Cambridge professor of science history Hasok Chang argued that schools must move beyond rote memorization and information delivery, which AI now handles effortlessly.
  • The risk of automation: Relying entirely on AI-generated results deprives learners of the messy, difficult "experiences" and trial-and-error processes where true understanding actually happens.
  • Global divergence: While some institutions revert to hand-written exams and low-tech classrooms, others—like the National University of Singapore—fully permit AI use as long as students transparently build upon it.

The core problem with generative AI in education isn't just cheating; it is the outsourcing of human struggle. Large language models are fundamentally statistical imitation tools that calculate the "average" of vast amounts of human data. When students let AI handle the heavy lifting of reasoning, they bypass the painful, necessary labor of wrestling with difficult texts and failed experiments. Chang likens using AI to having a brilliant assistant: an automated tool can draft a paper in seconds, but the human using it must still carry the ultimate responsibility for the final work.

South Korea's broader public education system, hyper-focused on intense college entrance examinations, makes this transition even more difficult. The government pumps billions into AI talent initiatives to secure a technological edge, yet treats education primarily as an information-delivery pipeline. If schools continue to measure success by whether a student can pick the right multiple-choice answer—something an algorithm now does instantly—they risk producing a generation incapable of asking original questions or enduring failure.

Ultimately, the rise of AI offers a rare chance to dismantle rigid educational frameworks. Instead of treating AI as an enemy to be banned or a substitute for thought, classrooms must return to slow, deliberate reading and open-ended scientific exploration. An algorithm can mimic human creativity and generate smooth prose, but it cannot replace the personal value of stumbling through a difficult problem and discovering meaning through human effort.
