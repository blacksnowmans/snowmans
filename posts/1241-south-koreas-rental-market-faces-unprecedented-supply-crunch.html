South Korea’s Rental Market Faces Unprecedented Supply Crunch as Jeonse Listings Evaporate

October 16, 2026

South Korea’s rental housing market is entering a severe crisis as jeonse (lump-sum deposit) listings plummet by up to 80% in parts of Seoul, pushing renewal costs far beyond the financial reach of working-class families.

  • Jeonse inventory disappears: Rental listings dropped by 40% to 50% nationwide over the past year, with Seoul’s Jungnang-gu losing 74.3% of its available units and Gyeonggi Province's Gwangmyeong losing 84.4%.
  • Deposit costs surge: In districts like Seoul’s Gangdong-gu and Gyeonggi's Hanam, rental deposits have jumped nearly 30% in two years, forcing tenants to find an extra 150 million KRW (about $110,000) just to renew their leases.
  • Historic supply bottleneck: Annual new apartment completions in Seoul are projected to fall below 10,000 units for the first time in 30 years, driven by lingering construction delays and market missteps from Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure.
  • For-sale listings surge in select pockets: Driven by tax shifts and profit-taking, sale listings are surging in districts like Seongbuk-gu (up 37.7%) and satellite cities like Bundang (up 27.6%), creating a brief window for buyers.

A Perfect Storm for Renters and a Historic Supply Bottleneck

The collapse of available listings in South Korea’s unique jeonse system—where tenants hand over a large, interest-free refundable deposit to landlords instead of paying monthly rent—is rapidly turning into a structural crisis. With annual completions of new apartments in Seoul dropping below 10,000 units for the first time in three decades, the core issue is an absolute shortage of housing supply. While President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is moving to accelerate public housing programs and new town construction, physical building timelines mean new supply cannot arrive immediately. The market continues to absorb the severe supply disruptions and policy errors left behind from Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, leaving current tenants squeezed by deposit demands that far outpace wage growth.

The Paradoxical Surge in Sale Listings Offers a Strategic Exit

Crucially, this rental crisis coincides with an unexpected surge in homes put up for sale in specific sub-markets. In districts such as Seongbuk-gu, for-sale inventory has jumped 37.7%, driven by homeowners eager to lock in capital gains of over 1 billion KRW (about $750,000) amid recent tax policy shifts. Similar inventory gains are appearing in satellite cities like Bundang and Gwacheon. For tenants facing ruinous deposit hikes at lease renewal, market analysts are advising an immediate pivot from renting to homeownership—taking advantage of motivated sellers in these listing-heavy pockets before broader supply shortages drive property purchase prices even higher.
