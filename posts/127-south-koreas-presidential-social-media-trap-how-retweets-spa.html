South Korea's Presidential Social Media Trap: How Retweets Sparked a Political Crisis

September 29, 2026

  • South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing mounting backlash and a sliding approval rating over a series of controversial social media retweets throughout September 2026.
  • Critics, including pro-government figures, argue that unverified retweets of critical or extreme accounts expose a severe lack of vetting and fuel unnecessary political infighting.
  • Experts warn that political timelines are increasingly manipulated by organized networks and AI-driven bots, creating a digital "echo chamber" or trap for high-ranking leaders.
  • The controversy has amplified calls for the president to abandon retweeting entirely, halt emotional online responses, and rely instead on formal state communications.

President Lee Jae-myung’s habit of sharing content on social media—intended to project approachability and direct communication—has instead transformed into a liability. In late September 2026, the presidential office was forced into defense mode after Lee repeatedly shared posts from random users without proper background checks, inadvertently amplifying criticisms of his own administration. When challenged, Lee defended his actions by arguing that sharing a post does not equal a full endorsement of the author's past. Yet, this defense fell flat: critics were quick to point out the glaring hypocrisy of aggressively blocking critical voices while claiming ignorance about the history of accounts he chooses to spotlight.

Political analysts and IT experts suggest this goes beyond mere carelessness, pointing instead to sophisticated, AI-assisted operations designed to hijack the feeds of major political figures. These coordinated networks feed leaders emotionally tailored, seemingly innocuous content to lower their guard before pulling off framing traps. Trapped in a digital echo chamber, politicians risk losing touch with broader public sentiment while mistaking online noise for genuine popular support. For an administration navigating a delicate political climate, these self-inflicted digital wounds threaten to erode public trust far faster than traditional policy missteps.
