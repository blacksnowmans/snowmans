South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Slumps to the Mid-30s in Approval Ratings

October 13, 2026

  • South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has fallen to the low-to-mid 30s, down steeply from around 67% six months ago.
  • In party support, the conservative opposition People Power Party has edged ahead of the progressive ruling Democratic Party in recent polls.
  • The slide is driven by public anger over soaring housing costs, policy flip-flops, and a growing perception of government high-handedness.
  • Discontent is particularly sharp among young voters, with 18-to-29-year-olds heavily favoring the opposition over the ruling party.

South Korean politics is on edge following a brutal stretch of polling data for President Lee Jae-myung. A series of surveys from pollsters Realmeter and Gallup show the president's approval rating tumbling to around 35%, while disapproval climbs past 60%. More alarmingly for the ruling Democratic Party, the conservative People Power Party has taken a narrow lead in party support. This sudden reversal—losing ground in almost every region outside the party's traditional Honam stronghold—suggests the honeymoon phase of the current administration has slammed into a wall.

The core of the backlash lies in three familiar political hazards: appointments, security, and housing. Voters are feeling squeezed by stubborn living costs, while botched policy rollouts—ranging from a clumsy attempt to alter basic pensions to mixed signals on real estate regulations—have alienated even core supporters. Particularly damaging is the youth vote: among 18-to-29-year-olds, support for the opposition has surged to nearly 48%, dwarfing the ruling party's share. Commentators note that a growing wave of undecided voters points to deep, systemic exhaustion with the current political establishment.

Housing, Policy Missteps, and the Danger of Out-of-Touch Governance

Nowhere is the voter revolt more visible than in housing policy, where negative sentiment has hit 61% against a meager 19% approval. Despite repeated government pledges to boost housing supply, soaring prices, heavy tax burdens on the middle class, and an unforgiving jeonse (lump-sum lease) market have left Seoul's voters furious. High housing costs directly eat into disposable income, crushing domestic consumption and destroying the hopes of young, first-time homebuyers.

Compounding the economic anxiety is a perceived tone-deafness from the administration. Whether it is sudden policy shifts pushed without adequate public consultation or a series of rough-edged personnel moves, the government is increasingly earning a reputation for unilateralism. Observers argue that voters are no longer willing to accept vague promises of hard work; they demand tangible results and a genuine connection to their daily struggles. If the administration fails to recalibrate its priorities, the current slide risks hardening into permanent alienation.
