South Korea’s Interior Design Market Hits a Structural Wall

September 02, 2026

South Korea’s once-booming interior design industry is facing a severe, structural downturn as households prioritize essential spending over home renovations.
  • The "Move-in" Engine Failed: Traditionally, moving to a new home triggered a cascade of renovations. High borrowing costs and a stagnant housing market mean people are staying put, killing demand.
  • Cost Crisis: Construction costs have jumped over 30% since 2020, creating a standoff between contractors needing profit and consumers unable to afford premium prices.
  • Broken Trust: Widespread reports of "ghosting" contractors and unexpected mid-project price hikes have turned consumer caution into total market paralysis.
  • Market Fragmentation: Large interior platforms are struggling, while the market pivots toward smaller, budget-conscious "partial renovations" targeting younger demographics.

The End of the "Total Renovation" Era

The narrative that a recovering housing market will save the renovation industry is proving false. Recent data shows that even when housing transactions ticked up by 14%, furniture and interior sales dropped by nearly 9%. This disconnect highlights a fundamental shift: buying a home no longer automatically guarantees an appetite for a total overhaul. The industry is currently suffering from a "double squeeze." On one side, the cost of materials has skyrocketed, making it impossible for small firms to offer lower quotes. On the other, years of industry malpractice—hidden fees and unfinished projects—have left consumers deeply cynical. When a project becomes a risky investment rather than an exciting improvement, homeowners simply choose to do nothing at all.

A Shift in Lifestyle, Not Just Spending

The decline of the "all-in" renovation model reflects a deeper change in Korean society. With the rise of single-person households and an aging population, the focus is shifting away from fixed, permanent installations toward flexible "home styling"—using light furniture and lighting rather than tearing down walls and replacing kitchens. In my view, this isn't just a temporary recession; it is a paradigm shift. We are moving away from the era of hyper-consumption in home decor. People are beginning to value the "lived-in" history of a space rather than the sanitized, glossy look of a showroom. For interior firms, the strategy of pushing expensive, comprehensive makeovers is effectively dead. The players who will survive this downturn are not the ones with the largest marketing budgets, but those who can pivot to provide long-term, incremental value that respects a homeowner’s budget and autonomy. The days of mindless, industry-driven excess are over.
