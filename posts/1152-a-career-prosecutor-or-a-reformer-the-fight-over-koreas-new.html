A Career Prosecutor or a Reformer? The Fight Over Korea’s New Investigative Body

October 14, 2026

The confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong, the nominee to lead South Korea's newly established Major Crimes Investigative Office (MCIO), has ignited a fierce debate over whether a career prosecutor who once fought judicial reform can be trusted to lead its most ambitious agency.

  • The Reform Paradox: Kim is the pick to lead the MCIO, a powerful new body created to take over the investigative powers of the Prosecution Service—an organization Kim spent his career defending against these very changes.
  • Accountability Concerns: Representative Park Ju-min of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) grilled Kim over his failure to keep written records of internal directives during politically sensitive cases, calling it a "black box" of decision-making.
  • The Accomplice Allegation: Lawmakers accused Kim of being a silent accomplice to "political prosecutions" during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached and is currently imprisoned.
  • The 2026 Deadline: The appointment is critical as the Prosecution Service is set to be officially abolished on October 2, 2026, splitting its functions into the MCIO (investigation) and the Prosecution Office (indictment).

The Black Box of Prosecutorial Power

Kim’s testimony revealed a troubling administrative culture where high-stakes decisions were made without a paper trail. When questioned about controversial cases from the previous administration—including the prosecution of former Blue House secretary Lee Kwang-cheol, who was eventually found innocent by the Supreme Court—Kim admitted he "did not keep separate records" of internal directives. This lack of documentation is a hallmark of an era where the Prosecution Service wielded unchecked power, allowing officials to issue orders while evading future accountability. For an agency like the MCIO, which will manage 2,874 staff members and handle major corruption and economic crimes, this "paperless" leadership style is a significant red flag.

A Convenient Change of Heart

Critics also highlight Kim’s dramatic shift in legal philosophy. In 2022, while serving at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, Kim helped lead a campaign against the very reforms that created the MCIO, labeling them "unconstitutional" and spreading alarmist claims that the changes would paralyze the justice system. Now that he is the nominee for the MCIO’s top post, he has softened his stance, admitting his past views were "partially wrong." This perceived opportunism—opposing reform when it threatens one's organization, then embracing it when it offers a promotion—suggests a lack of the firm conviction required to build a new, transparent investigative culture.

The stakes for the President Lee Jae-myung administration are high. The MCIO is intended to be a professional body that operates under the objective control of the Prosecution Office. If the agency is led by someone who spent his career defending the "Prosecutorial Republic" of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the MCIO risks simply becoming a new version of the old, unconstrained Prosecution Service. As Representative Park noted, giving the keys to the house to someone who previously "opened the door for the thieves" is a dangerous gamble for Korea’s judicial future.
