South Korea's Ruling Party in Crisis as Leader's Heavy-Handed Tactics Alienate Voters

September 11, 2026

  • What happened: Kim Min-seok, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), has faced fierce internal backlash after ordering party lawmakers to actively appear on progressive YouTube channels.
  • The political toll: Fresh polling data shows the administration of President Lee Jae-myung plunging to a 37.4% approval rating—matching the nadir of the previous conservative administration—with support among 30-year-olds on key issues like real estate dropping to a staggering 0.8%.
  • Who is affected: The ruling liberal bloc faces mounting pressure, with speculation growing that Kim's fragile leadership could collapse into an emergency interim leadership committee ahead of upcoming elections.

South Korean politics is currently a masterclass in self-inflicted wounds. When the ruling Democratic Party's leader commands lawmakers to boost their profiles on specific partisan YouTube channels—as if media outlets were subsidiary contractors bound to obey party directives—it reveals an arrogance that voters find deeply grating. For an administration that swept to power on promises of systemic reform, treating the media like a political mouthpiece is alienating even its core base.

Worse still is the growing disconnect over policy delivery. Key reform promises, from restructuring prosecutorial powers to tackling real estate speculation, have devolved into empty rhetoric. When young voters in their thirties—who once pinned their hopes on the current administration—see their economic survival reduced to piecemeal policy fixes and tone-deaf social media lectures, goodwill turns rapidly into outright hostility. The administration is discovering the hard way that you cannot govern by political engineering alone.

A Vulnerable Leadership Facing Imminent Collapse

Kim Min-seok's heavy-handed grip on the party is paradoxically a symptom of his extreme political weakness. Often viewed internally as a temporary leader whose tenure hangs by a thread, Kim risks swift replacement by party heavyweights if public discontent spills over into open rebellion. Critics within his own party recall his 2002 defection during a presidential race, drawing sharp parallels between his past political opportunism and his current attempts to silence dissenting voices.

If the party suffers major setbacks in forthcoming local elections, the current leadership will likely fall, paving the way for an emergency steering committee. But superficial leadership shuffles will do little to fix the core rot. Unless the ruling camp abandons its bureaucratic complacency and delivers genuine, tangible reforms, it risks squandering its mandate entirely.
