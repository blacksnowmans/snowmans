The Growing Trend of High School Dropouts in South Korea’s "Capital-Driven" Education System

September 10, 2026

South Korean high schools have increasingly transformed from places of learning into high-stakes testing grounds where a student's success is determined more by their family’s financial investment than by their academic effort.

  • The "Advanced Learning" Gap: Public school teachers frequently pace their lessons based on students who have already mastered the material through expensive private tutoring, alienating those who have not.
  • The Illusion of Meritocracy: While the government has increased reliance on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT or Suneung) in the name of fairness, statistics show that students from the wealthiest families dominate top university admissions.
  • The Dropout Surge: Facing a system that feels rigged, a growing number of students are choosing to drop out of high school to study independently, trading the classroom for a lonely, self-funded path to university.

When the Classroom Becomes a Filter for Wealth

For many students, the transition to high school brings a sense of despair rather than academic rigor. It is now common for students to enter Grade 10 having already "pre-studied" the entire curriculum at private academies (hagwons). Those who haven't—or cannot afford to—find themselves in classrooms where teachers move at a speed that assumes prior knowledge. When a student struggles, the common sentiment among peers is that they are simply "not keeping up," a social pressure that effectively pushes non-wealthy students out of the system.

This reality has turned the classroom into an inefficient market. Students often feel that the public school system exists not to teach, but to confirm who has "invested" more in private education. As one student lamented, their grades are not a reflection of their intellect or hard work, but a metric of how much money their parents spent on coaching and exclusive study materials.

The Myth of "Fair" Admissions

The belief that expanding the CSAT-based admissions process is "fair" is increasingly viewed as a myth that favors the elite. Data from the Korea Education Development Institute (KEDI) reveals a stark reality: students from the highest socioeconomic bracket (top 20%) are far more likely to take a gap year or repeat a grade to re-apply for college than those from the lowest bracket (bottom 20%).

By making admission heavily dependent on standardized testing, the system has created a "high-speed lane" for those who can afford high-end private exam prep centers, which can cost several million won (thousands of dollars) per month. For the average student, the dream of "self-study" often leads to a lonely experience in a study café, where they lack the strategic guidance that wealthier peers receive at elite prep institutes.

School as a Disposable Tool

As the pressure mounts, many students are beginning to view high school as a redundant, even obstructive, step in their career. The trend of dropping out is particularly high in affluent districts, where students leave the moment they feel their internal high school grades (naesin) are too low to get into a top-tier university. They choose to trade the "sub-contractor" nature of public schools—which they feel only exist to feed the private tutoring industry—for the sole, albeit grueling, focus of the CSAT.

Ultimately, the Korean education system has reached a point where the classroom is failing its primary objective. Unless there is a fundamental reform that decouples education from parental wealth and provides a more equitable support system for all students—whether they remain in the school system or seek alternative paths—the classroom will remain a site of exclusion rather than opportunity.
