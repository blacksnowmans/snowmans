Lee Jae-myung’s Slide in the Polls Splits South Korea’s Ruling Bloc

November 17, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has fallen to 39% while his ruling Democratic Party of Korea trails the conservative opposition by 8.4 percentage points, exposing a widening rift between household economic anxiety and internal loyalty battles.

  • A sharp polling reversal: A November 13 Gallup Korea survey put Lee’s approval at 39% against 50% disapproval, down from a 66% peak in March. On November 16, pollster Realmeter recorded the Democratic Party at 34.6%, behind the main conservative opposition People Power Party at 43.0%—even though the conservative party produced Yoon Suk-yeol, who received a life sentence in a first-instance ruling over the December 3 insurrection.
  • Voters in their 30s pull back: According to SBS Fact Check and Pai Chai University professor Kim Hyung-joon, 30-somethings have shifted from mid-pack supporters to one of Lee’s weakest age groups amid Seoul-area rent and housing pressures and estimated retail investor losses of about $39 billion (54 trillion won) in single-stock leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs).
  • Political drags outweigh welfare spending: Surveys by Gallup and Realmeter point to controversies over dropping criminal indictments tied to Lee (cited by 24.3% in a September poll), cabinet nominations, and prosecution restructuring as major negatives, blunting any boost from roughly $45 billion (63 trillion won) in excess-tax welfare plans.
  • Open factional warfare: Allies of Democratic Party Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok and lawmaker Jung Chung-rae are clashing over a closed-door apology for unproven claims linking rivals to the Shincheonji religious group and over whether to merge with the minor Rebuilding Korea Party before January 2027, while broadcaster Kim Eo-jun and political writer Rhyu Si-min have accused Lee’s camp of sidelining traditional party voices.

Why the "structural majority" is fracturing

When Lee met former President Moon Jae-in in July to outline a "structural majority" built on internal unity and broader outreach, the stated goal was governing stability. Instead, commentators such as the political YouTube channel "Jungchi Ilgeojuneun Yeoja" argue the coalition has splintered around a "New Lee Jae-myung" phenomenon—supporters loyal to Lee personally rather than to the Democratic Party as an institution.

That shift is colliding with algorithmic politics. As the channel’s analysis notes, online homophily and social media filter bubbles have created echo chambers where policy critics are branded "anti-Lee" traitors and unfavorable polls are dismissed as manipulation. Meanwhile, traditional Democratic figures argue that sidelining the party’s established core—such as the combined 40% support base for Moon and former President Roh Moo-hyun cited by Kim Eo-jun from 2024 Gallup data—risks shrinking the coalition further.

Ultimately, the ruling bloc faces a gap between what insiders fight over and what households experience. Rebuilding support likely depends less on policing loyalty or setting merger deadlines, and more on addressing the housing costs and retail market losses pushing younger voters away—a challenge that even tens of billions of dollars in welfare announcements have not yet overcome.
