How to Survive the Camino de Santiago: Essential Tips From South Korean Pilgrims

September 07, 2026

South Koreans have quietly become one of the largest non-European nationalities tackling the Camino de Santiago, turning the ancient Spanish pilgrimage into a major bucket-list item back home—and bringing a distinctly meticulous, gear-heavy approach to preparation that offers a masterclass in survival.

  • Size up your shoes: Buy hiking shoes 15mm larger than your normal size to accommodate swelling feet, and skip wearing multiple pairs of socks, which causes blistering friction.
  • Pack light and smart: Stick to a backpack with a sturdy steel frame to protect your posture, and limit your wardrobe to just two sets of functional clothing you can wash daily.
  • Budget for the trail: Expect to spend roughly €30 to €40 ($33–$44) a day for basic living expenses, including €11 ($12) pilgrim meals and modest taxi fares.
  • Lean on locals: Rely on the local operators of albergues (pilgrim hostels) rather than unreliable bus apps for transit schedules and practical advice.

Foot Care and Gear: The Rules of the Road

On the Camino, your feet are your only transit system. The most common hazards—blisters and plantar fasciitis—can derail a trip in days. Experienced Korean travelers emphasize that vanity and over-packing are the ultimate enemies on the trail. Heavy knee braces cut off circulation and cause heat rashes if worn all day, while redundant gear like hiking poles often ends up abandoned within ten days as dead weight. Instead, success lies in radical simplification: carrying a compact sports towel instead of bulky bath towels, buying cheap €4 ($4.50) sunglasses you won't care about losing, and relying on basic blister tape and petroleum jelly.

Daily routines matter more than high-tech gear. Stopping every one or two hours to strip off your shoes and fully dry your feet is non-negotiable for preventing severe blisters. Similarly, managing your expectations around language and logistics keeps stress low. Basic Spanish pleasantries—a simple "Hola" and "Buen Camino"—go a long way, and your nightly hostel host will always give you better directions than Google Maps. While bedbug fears loom large for first-time visitors, modern hostels with protective mattress covers mean the threat is largely manageable with basic common sense.

The Mental Shift: Why Koreans Are Flocking to the Camino

The surge of South Korean interest in the Camino reflects a broader cultural craving for physical unplugging and solitary challenge in a society hyper-focused on speed and digital connectivity. Completing the trail has become a popular rite of passage for Koreans seeking a break from corporate burnout. Yet the advice coming back from those who have finished is refreshingly unromantic: treat it like an endurance hike, not a spiritual retreat, until your body adapts to the daily punishment.

Ultimately, the secret to finishing the pilgrimage isn't expensive equipment or exhaustive preparation—it's the willingness to discard what you don't need, day by day. Whether it's shedding useless gear at a roadside rest stop, writing daily notes in a small pocket journal, or simply slowing down to drink a €2 ($2.20) café au lait during afternoon siesta hours, the journey rewards those who travel light and pay attention to their bodies. As seasoned pilgrims note, the blisters will heal, but the habit of daily self-care is what gets you to Santiago.
