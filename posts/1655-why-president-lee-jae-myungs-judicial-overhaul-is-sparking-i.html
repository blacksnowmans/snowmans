Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Judicial Overhaul is Sparking Internal Alarm

October 29, 2026

  • South Korea's push to separate police investigative powers from prosecutors and establish a new prosecution office is facing heavy criticism not for its goals, but for a total lack of public consultation.
    •
  • President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is increasingly dismissing constructive feedback and labeling critics within the progressive camp as disloyal.
    •
  • Commentators and supporters warn that an arrogant "my way" style of governance risks squandering the administration's political capital and alienating its base.
    •
  • Critics argue that absolute power has corrupted Lee's communication style, turning a historic working-class political rise into a lesson on the dangers of unchecked authority.
    •

South Korea's push for judicial reform—centered on stripping prosecutors of direct investigative powers and creating a dedicated prosecution office—was supposed to be a crowning achievement for President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party. Instead, it has become a lightning rod for discontent. While the policy goals enjoy broad backing among liberals, the administration has rolled them out with zero meaningful public explanation or debate. By treating any policy questions as outright betrayal and dismissing skeptics as anti-Lee, the government has traded persuasion for intimidation.

This heavy-handed approach has alarmed even friendly commentators and progressive media figures who once championed Lee's rise. Pushed to the margins, critics within the movement have increasingly resorted to sharp satire—comparing the president to an unruly child refusing therapy, or warning darkly that an unyielding leadership risks the same political ruin that befell imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol. Observers note that Lee is not inherently a fraud; rather, reaching the absolute pinnacle of power has warped his political instincts, replacing grassroots humility with defensive bluster.

Ultimately, South Korea's politically engaged electorate has little tolerance for an arrogant administration that demands blind obedience rather than offering sincere persuasion. If the Lee administration wants to see its signature reforms survive without triggering a severe backlash, it must drop the hostile rhetoric, accept internal dissent, and remember that democratic leadership requires listening just as much as it demands driving policy.
