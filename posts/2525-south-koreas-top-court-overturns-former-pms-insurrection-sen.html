South Korea’s Top Court Overturns Former PM’s Insurrection Sentence, Triggering Chief Justice Impeachment Push

November 20, 2026

According to a political briefing by Korean media blog Black Snowman, South Korea’s Supreme Court on November 19 overturned a 15-year prison sentence for former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo on insurrection charges, prompting the ruling bloc to pursue the impeachment of Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae.

  • Partial acquittal ruling: While reaffirming that the December 3 martial law declaration by Yoon Suk-yeol was an unconstitutional act of insurrection, the Supreme Court’s full bench ruled there was insufficient proof of direct intent to subvert the constitution in Han’s convening of a martial law Cabinet meeting—one of three charges against him—and sent the case back to the Seoul High Court for retrial while extending his detention by two months.
  • Ripple effects for other officials: By distinguishing between pre-declaration plotting and post-declaration administrative duties, the ruling is expected to shape upcoming trials for other officials who attended the martial law Cabinet meeting, including former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min and former Justice Minister Park Sung-jae.
  • Ruling bloc’s impeachment drive: Condemning the verdict as "historical treason," the ruling Democratic Party of Korea moved on November 20 to adopt Chief Justice Jo’s impeachment as its official party line and form a dedicated task force to fast-track a plenary vote, while allied groups such as the Rebuilding Korea Party and the Progressive Party held candlelight rallies accusing the court of acting as an "insurrection laundromat."
  • Opposition backlash: South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, welcomed the decision as a check on political overreach and denounced the impeachment bid as "thug-like retaliation," while the minor conservative Reform Party warned that punishing judges over legal rulings threatens the constitutional separation of powers.

Judicial Formalism Meets Legislative Escalation

The Supreme Court’s decision exposes a widening gulf between narrow legal formalism and public expectations of accountability. As the Black Snowman briefing notes, treating a prime minister’s oversight of a martial law Cabinet meeting as potentially routine official duty—amid an unconstitutional attempt to suspend the legislature—risks appearing to soften the legal consequences for senior officials, with critics in the ruling bloc warning it could even weaken future proceedings against Yoon Suk-yeol.

At the same time, the legislature’s swift turn toward impeaching the nation’s top judge carries severe institutional risks of its own. Reaching for an unprecedented impeachment before the appellate retrial even begins turns a contested legal interpretation into a direct clash between co-equal branches of government, lending weight to opposition warnings about the erosion of judicial independence.

Caught between a ruling seen by critics as overly lenient and a retaliatory impeachment campaign, South Korea’s year-end politics now face an uncompromising standoff. Whether the Democratic Party follows through with a floor vote will determine if this judicial dispute hardens into a broader constitutional crisis.
