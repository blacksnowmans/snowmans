South Korea's Prosecution Reform Faces Backlash Over 'Resurrection' Fears

September 10, 2026

    What happened: South Korea is set to dissolve its 72-year-old prosecution system and split investigative and indicting powers, launching the new Prosecution Service (focusing on indictments) and a separate major crimes investigation agency. However, the Ministry of Justice's new organizational proposal for the Prosecution Service retains over 8,400 staff members, including more than 2,200 prosecutors.
  • What it changes: Critics argue that keeping thousands of investigative personnel and creating new oversight units allows prosecutors to maintain de facto investigative powers, effectively giving them a backdoor to resurrect their old authority.
  • Who is affected: Reform-minded politicians within the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main opposition party, currently critical of the plan), the Ministry of Justice, and the broader South Korean legal and political establishment.

South Korea’s long-awaited overhaul of its criminal justice system was meant to permanently strip prosecutors of their sweeping investigative powers and curb decades of institutional overreach. But as the transition date approaches, even members of the ruling and reforming camps are crying foul over a structural reorganization plan that critics are calling a Trojan horse for the prosecution's return.

The core controversy lies in headcount and turf. Despite losing direct investigative duties, the newly refocused Prosecution Service is slated to keep 8,412 employees—including 2,292 prosecutors and thousands of investigators. Reformists argue that maintaining this massive footprint, alongside newly proposed units like a "judicial control" department to review dropped cases, sets the stage for prosecutors to continue meddling in investigations under the guise of trial preparation. Without deep staff reductions, the reform risks becoming a mere cosmetic name-change.

Old Habits Die Hard in South Korea's Legal Bureaucracy

The Ministry of Justice has defended the headcount by pointing to existing statutory limits on prosecutor positions, but critics say the government is hiding behind red tape instead of doing the hard work of rewriting the law. Figures within the progressive camp, such as lawmaker Kim Yong-min, have warned that leaving the old guard's personnel, budget, and organizational structure intact means the prosecution can be restored to full power the moment political winds shift.

Compounding these fears is the appointment of figures with ties to the old prosecutorial establishment to key leadership roles, fueling accusations that political leaders are dodging accountability. For a public that has long demanded a neutering of political prosecution and an end to unchecked prosecutorial power, the current blueprint looks less like an irreversible structural revolution and more like business as usual.
