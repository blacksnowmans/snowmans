Nvidia Eyes TSMC for 3D DRAM, Signaling a Crisis for Korean Memory Giants

November 08, 2026

Reports that Nvidia has requested TSMC to directly produce next-generation 3D DRAM are sending shockwaves through South Korea’s semiconductor industry, signaling a direct threat to the long-standing dominance of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

  • A Direct Threat: Industry reports suggest Nvidia is in talks with TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), the world's leading foundry, to manufacture memory chips directly to bypass current performance bottlenecks in AI hardware.
  • Supply Chain Disruption: If TSMC enters the memory market, it would dismantle the current division of labor where South Korean firms provide High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) for TSMC to integrate into Nvidia’s GPU packages.
  • Explosive Price Projections: Market analysts warn that DDR5 memory prices could skyrocket by 450%, rising from approximately $8.70 per chip to $48 by 2026 as AI server demand outstrips global supply.
  • Strategic Pivot: The Lee Jae-myung administration, South Korea's current government, is facing calls to accelerate national semiconductor strategies to protect the country's "memory sovereignty" against this Taiwan-U.S. alliance.

The Blurring Lines of Chip Supremacy

For years, the semiconductor industry followed a clear script: South Korea made the memory, and TSMC made the logic chips. That wall is now crumbling. Nvidia’s reported outreach to TSMC for 3D DRAM—a next-generation technology that stacks data cells vertically—suggests that the "AI bottleneck" has become so severe that Nvidia is willing to upend the entire global supply chain to solve it. While TSMC has officially denied these reports, the market's reaction reflects a deeper truth: in the AI era, the distinction between a foundry and a memory maker is becoming a liability rather than an asset.

This move is particularly dangerous for Samsung Electronics, the world’s largest memory maker, and SK Hynix, the current leader in HBM. Samsung has long marketed its "turn-key" service—the ability to handle design, memory, and packaging under one roof—as its primary edge over TSMC. If TSMC successfully adds 3D DRAM to its arsenal, Samsung’s unique selling point vanishes. Furthermore, with the importance of the "logic die" (the base layer of HBM) increasing, TSMC’s superior ultra-fine fabrication processes give it a natural advantage that could relegate Korean firms to being mere sub-contractors for low-end components.

A New Strategic Mandate for Seoul

The timing of this technological shift places a heavy burden on the Lee Jae-myung administration. The current government is currently working to repair the industrial damage and diplomatic isolation seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office. During the Yoon years, R&D budget cuts and a lack of coherent trade strategy left Korean tech giants vulnerable to aggressive moves by foreign competitors and changing U.S. subsidies. President Lee’s government has pivoted toward a "Semiconductor Mega Cluster" and aggressive tax incentives, but the TSMC-Nvidia alliance represents a challenge that requires more than just infrastructure—it requires high-level industrial diplomacy.

Ultimately, the threat of TSMC entering the 3D DRAM space is a wake-up call. South Korea can no longer rely on the assumption that its memory lead is unassailable. As Micron, the leading U.S. memory maker, also gains ground with significant backing from Washington, the Lee administration must use its diplomatic leverage to ensure Korean firms are not squeezed out of the AI value chain. If Samsung and SK Hynix cannot prove they are "irreplaceable" through immediate technical breakthroughs in 3D DRAM and HBM4, the center of gravity for the world's most important technology may shift permanently away from the Korean peninsula.
