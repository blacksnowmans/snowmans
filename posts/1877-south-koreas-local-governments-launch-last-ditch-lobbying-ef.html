South Korea’s Local Governments Launch Last-Ditch Lobbying Effort for Budget Survival

November 03, 2026

As the National Assembly begins its crucial budget review process this November, mayors and local leaders from across South Korea are descending on Seoul in a desperate bid to secure state funding for survival-level projects.

  • The "Budget War": Local governments, facing depleted coffers due to austerity measures and reduced state subsidies, are lobbying the government and lawmakers to prevent critical projects from being slashed.
  • Infrastructure Push: Major urban centers like Seoul's Nowon District are demanding expedited approval for the GTX-E express rail line, even calling for exemptions from standard feasibility studies.
  • Long-term Strategies: Cities like Daejeon have set up temporary "National Assembly camps" to ensure they secure not only next year’s budget but also mid-term funding through 2027.
  • Regional Crisis: Rural areas, such as Hampyeong, are pleading for basic income pilot programs to stave off total population collapse, highlighting the extreme financial disparity between Seoul and the provinces.

A Symptom of Fiscal Centralization

The annual spectacle of local leaders "begging" in the halls of the National Assembly is a recurring symptom of South Korea’s highly centralized fiscal structure. While the country operates under a system of local autonomy, the reality is that most basic infrastructure and welfare projects are entirely dependent on central government subsidies. With local fiscal self-reliance rates often hovering below 20-30%, mayors have little choice but to lobby powerful central bureaucrats and legislators to keep their regions afloat.

The current scramble—where leaders from regions like Daegu, Gwangju, and Gangwon-do must pitch their needs against one another—reveals the fragility of regional development. Even as the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) oversees the national budget, the competition remains fierce. For rural counties facing extinction, the failure to secure a single line item in the national budget can mean the loss of essential transportation or welfare services for the coming year.

The Need for Genuine Fiscal Decentralization

While lawmakers often engage in "pork-barrel" politics, the system lacks a transparent mechanism for distributing resources based on objective need. Too often, projects are either buried in bureaucratic red tape or salvaged through behind-the-scenes "napkin budgets"—informal, opaque agreements made in the final stages of parliamentary review.

True reform will remain out of reach until the government moves beyond performative rhetoric and grants local governments the power to raise and manage their own tax revenue. Until then, the "Seoul relay" will continue, forcing local leaders to abandon their duties in their hometowns to chase funding in the capital. This isn't just a matter of regional politics; it is a fundamental challenge to the constitutional promise of balanced national development.
