How a 36-Year-Old Korean House Found New Life

October 04, 2026

A recent renovation project by "Building Remaker" Shin Bit-na demonstrates that a 36-year-old single-family home can be transformed into a modern, highly functional living space through precise design rather than simple demolition.

  • The project: A 1989-built red brick house was gutted and redesigned, focusing on structural reinforcement and internal flow rather than just aesthetic updates.
  • The cost: The total renovation amounted to approximately $330,000 (450 million KRW), covering major structural work, waterproofing, and specialized interior modifications.
  • Design innovation: The remodel overcame low ceiling heights and awkward floor plans by using "exposed ceilings," custom-height island kitchens, and clever spatial planning.
  • Policy note: South Korea offers significant low-interest loan programs for homeowners renovating properties over 20 years old, but these must be applied for before construction begins.

Mastering the "Small House" Challenge

The transformation highlights how to maximize value in limited square footage. The architects addressed the building’s complex, dated exterior by using a two-tone facade—dark gray brick on the bottom to ground the structure, and lighter colors above to minimize visual clutter. Inside, the second floor was prioritized as the main living space to take advantage of higher ceilings. In the kitchen, the designers implemented a dual-height island: 900mm for standing food preparation and 750mm for comfortable dining, proving that intelligent furniture design can significantly enhance daily life in a compact footprint.

Structural limitations were treated as opportunities. On the first floor, where ceilings were uncomfortably low, the team used exposed concrete ceilings and indirect lighting to create a sense of height and a "vintage" aesthetic. Where structural pillars couldn't be removed, they were incorporated into the design as rounded, curved features rather than hidden, turning potential eyesores into signature architectural details.

The True Cost of Modernizing Older Homes

While a $330,000 renovation price tag may shock some, the budget was largely consumed by essential, invisible work. In South Korea, where many "remodeling" projects fail due to poor waterproofing or plumbing issues, this project prioritized the building’s skeleton. Prospective owners should note that while local governments provide financial support—such as Seoul’s low-interest loans—these are strictly conditional. If you start construction before applying, you lose eligibility for all government aid.

Ultimately, this project challenges the "Apartment Republic" mindset common in Korea, where older homes are often seen as obstacles to be razed. By respecting the structure’s history while integrating modern amenities like motion-controlled beds and advanced ventilation, this renovation offers a blueprint for sustainable, thoughtful urban living.
