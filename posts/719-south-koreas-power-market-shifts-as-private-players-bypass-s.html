South Korea’s Power Market Shifts as Private Players Bypass State Monopoly

September 11, 2026

  • South Korea's decades-old renewable energy support system (RPS) is ending this year, forcing generators away from spot markets and toward direct Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs).
  • Private developers like SK D&C spin-off SK Eternix are stepping in to aggregate small solar operators and sell directly to corporations desperate for RE100 compliance.
  • While state utility giant KEPCO struggles under massive debt and government price caps, private capital—backed by global funds like KKR—is seizing control of the most profitable corners of the market.
  • Regulatory risks remain high: the government has a history of stepping in with wholesale price caps whenever private profits swell too high.

South Korea’s tightly controlled power sector is undergoing a structural rewiring. For decades, the state-run Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) held a near-total monopoly on buying and distributing electricity. Today, that centralized model is cracking under the weight of renewable energy targets and KEPCO's chronic financial losses, opening the door for private developers to dominate a burgeoning direct PPA market.

The catalyst is the retirement of the country’s Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS) system by the end of this year, which will severely limit the issuance of new Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs). With spot-market certainty vanishing, renewable energy operators are pivoting to 25-year direct PPAs. This allows them to lock in predictable cash flows while shielding themselves from KEPCO's state-mandated lower purchase prices. Companies like SK Eternix—now operating under a joint venture between South Korea's SK Group and global private equity firm KKR—are aggressively aggregating fragmented, sub-megawatt solar producers into massive distribution networks. By doing so, they can bundle and supply the stable, long-term renewable power that major corporations need to meet global RE100 commitments.

The Tug-of-War Between Public Burden and Private Profit

This shift has created a high-stakes zero-sum game between the state and private enterprise. KEPCO is staggering under the cost of purchasing electricity far above what it is legally allowed to sell it for to the public, leading to severe financial distress. In response, the government has imposed wholesale price caps (SMP caps) to limit how much generators can charge, aiming to cushion KEPCO's balance sheet. But these interventions have largely served as blunt instruments, angering small-scale renewable operators and chilling market sentiment whenever commodity prices spike.

The tension points to a deeper identity crisis in South Korea's energy architecture. KEPCO is left maintaining the expensive transmission and distribution infrastructure while private developers skim the high-margin generation and retail business. Critics argue it is a half-baked market opening where the state assumes all the public safety-net risks while private capital walks away with the spoils. Furthermore, experts warn that an over-reliance on solar and wind without sufficient Energy Storage Systems (ESS) will destabilize the grid, given that South Korea's peak power demand hits during scorching summer heatwaves, while wind generation peaks in winter.

Ultimately, the era of state-monopolized power in South Korea has crossed a Rubicon. Despite the constant threat of regulatory crackdowns and price interference, the sheer gravitational pull of corporate RE100 demand and private capital means the shift toward a decentralized, private-led PPA market is locked in.
