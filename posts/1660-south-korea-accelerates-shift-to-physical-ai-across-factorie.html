South Korea Accelerates Shift to 'Physical AI' Across Factories, Cities, and Homes

October 29, 2026

South Korean local governments, central ministries, and tech conglomerates are making a coordinated push to transition artificial intelligence from screen-based software into real-world physical robotics across manufacturing hubs, public infrastructure, and homes.

  • Regional hub investments: Gyeonggi Province, South Korea's most populous region surrounding Seoul, launched a 66-institution alliance backed by over 3 billion won ($2.2 million) to retrofit legacy manufacturing zones, while Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province committed 60 billion won ($44 million) for specialized AI talent development.
  • Big tech alliance: LG Electronics announced a collaboration with Nvidia to secure 500,000 hours of physical robot training data, targeting a full commercial launch of home humanoid robots by 2030.
  • Public safety and care expansion: Real-world deployments now include autonomous track-inspection drones, hazard-patrol robots, and a state-supported initiative to roll out caregiving robots for elderly and disabled residents by 2029.
  • Labor model reform: The Hyundai Motor trade union has begun reviewing a move toward a fixed monthly salary structure as physical AI machines replace traditional hourly assembly tasks.

From Software Chatbots to Physical Hardware

The transition to "Physical AI" represents a major pivot in South Korea’s technology strategy. While large language models rely on digital text, physical robotics demands vast datasets of real-world vision, spatial perception, and motion control. LG Electronics’ effort to gather 500,000 hours of physical learning data in partnership with Nvidia highlights this critical bottleneck. Building proprietary physical data pipelines is becoming the key battleground for hardware manufacturers seeking to lead the robotics market.

At the same time, regional governments are using physical AI as an industrial lifeline. Injecting spatial intelligence and autonomous patrol systems into aging manufacturing complexes—such as the Sihwa Industrial Complex in Siheung—aims to boost productivity among smaller suppliers. Central government support, including site visits by the Ministry of Planning and Budget, signals that the Lee Jae-myung administration views physical robotics as a crucial tool for balanced regional development outside Seoul.

Overhauling Public Infrastructure and Industrial Labor

Beyond factory floors, South Korea's initiative stands out for its aggressive application in public safety and social services. Autonomous drones and quadrupedal "robot dogs" are already replacing high-risk manual labor along railway tracks and in fire-hazard zones. Furthermore, the planned 2029 deployment of home care robots directly addresses South Korea’s demographic pressures, turning automation into an essential public service for an aging population.

This rapid deployment is already reshaping labor relations. As machines take over high-danger tasks and routine assembly, the role of human workers is shifting toward system oversight and maintenance. The Hyundai Motor union’s decision to study a full monthly wage model reflects how machine intelligence will force a overhaul of traditional hourly pay structures, requiring new labor-management consensus alongside technological adoption.
