South Korea's Ruling Party Fractures as Key Intellectual Withdraws Support from President Lee Jae-myung

October 05, 2026

  • Prominent economist and former supporter Prof. Choi Bae-geun has formally withdrawn his support for President Lee Jae-myung, accusing the administration of abandoning core reform promises like wealth redistribution and prosecution reform.
  • The ruling Democratic Party is mired in bitter internal feuds, with critics targeting party leader Kim Min-seok for prioritizing his personal 2030 presidential ambitions over party unity and effective governance.
  • The fallout exposes deep disillusionment among South Korea's progressive base, who feel alienated by pragmatic compromises, factional infighting, and a perceived lack of economic competence.

South Korean politics is facing a moment of reckoning. When a loyal intellectual publicly breaks with a president he helped elect, it signals more than just a personal falling out — it exposes a deep ideological fracture within the progressive camp. For international observers watching Seoul's political landscape, the current turmoil within the ruling Democratic Party offers a stark reminder of how quickly reformist zeal can curdle into factional self-preservation once power is secured.

The core of the crisis lies in a broken social contract. Voters who backed President Lee Jae-myung envisioned a sweeping overhaul of entrenched privileges and an economy focused on the working class. Instead, critics argue that the administration's obsession with broad-tent politics and pragmatism has diluted its reform agenda. Economists point to structural vulnerabilities — such as over-reliance on a volatile semiconductor industry alongside widening wealth inequality — as proof that the government's economic vision is falling short.

Factional Warfare and the Crisis of Leadership

Compounding the administration's policy struggles is an acute leadership crisis within the Democratic Party. Party leader Kim Min-seok has faced scathing attacks from within his own ranks for prioritizing his political positioning for the 2030 presidential race over legislative strategy. Rather than bridging internal divides between party factions, the current leadership is accused of deepening them through clumsy faction-building and a reactive, defensive posture against political rivals.

This infighting has paralyzed the party's ability to govern effectively or present a unified front. When ruling party heavyweights openly trade barbs and key policy initiatives get bogged down in partisan posturing, the administration loses its mandate for change. Ultimately, this public fracture hands a strategic gift to conservative opponents, proving once again that the most damaging blows to a progressive government often come from within.
