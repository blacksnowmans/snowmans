South Korea’s Ruling Party Fractures as Kim Min-seok Hits 100-Day Mark

November 02, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea is sliding into an intense factional power struggle as party leader Kim Min-seok approaches his 100th day in office under heavy fire from senior party figures.

  • Open internal revolt: Senior lawmaker Jung Chung-rae publicly declared that Kim's tenure will face judgment at the 100-day mark, floating his own roadmap for a January merger with the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party without leadership approval.
  • Proxy war over party machinery: The race for the Democratic Party's National Women’s Committee chair has turned into an acrimonious battle between the pro-presidential mainstream and Jung's faction, drawing official election committee sanctions against candidate Moon Jung-bok for rule violations.
  • Sectarian infiltration scandal: The party faces immediate fallout this week when leadership briefs lawmakers on an internal investigation into claims that the Shincheonji religious group systematically infiltrated the party roster to manipulate primary votes.
  • Sinking public support: Despite turmoil within the conservative opposition People Power Party, the Democrats' poll numbers are tumbling toward the 20% range, threatening the agenda of President Lee Jae-myung.

Ambition and Scandals Threaten Party Discipline

Kim assumed leadership pledging stability to back the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, who took office after the impeachment, removal, and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Instead, party heavyweights are already jockeying for leverage over candidate nominations for upcoming legislative races. Jung's unauthorized talk of merging with the Rebuilding Korea Party—led by Cho Kuk—drew sharp public rebuke from party loyalists like Representative Lee Hae-sik, who criticized Jung for acting like a unilateral prophet and eroding the leadership's authority.

At the same time, this week’s legislative caucus report on Shincheonji infiltrations poses a severe test. If investigators reveal that the controversial sect secured voting rights and swayed internal elections, leadership rivals will immediately weaponize roster mismanagement against Kim. What should have been an administrative housecleaning risks degenerating into an all-out factional blame game over the party's grassroots integrity.

Stalled Momentum on Crucial Reforms

This infighting arrives at an exceptionally steep cost for the ruling party. Crucial legislative battles loom, including a parliamentary inquiry into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s conduct during the Dec. 3 martial law crisis, as well as a special prosecutor bill targeting past politicized indictments by state prosecutors. Passing these contentious measures against stiff opposition resistance requires an ironclad, unified voting bloc.

Instead, internal warfare has alienated core progressive supporters, leaving the party unable to capitalize on the troubles facing the opposition People Power Party. If Kim cannot reassert control over party discipline and shift the spotlight from petty factional battles to concrete governing achievements, his leadership may unravel before his agenda ever takes off.
