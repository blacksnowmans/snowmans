South Korea's National Audit Enters Week Two: Prosecutor Reforms, Delivery App Caps, and Opposition Woes

October 12, 2026

  • Prosecution Overhaul Clashes: South Korea's Ministry of the Interior and Safety ordered an inspection of Lim Eun-jung, head of the Gwangju Investigation Office (part of the newly launched Investigative Headquarters/Jungsucheong), drawing fierce pushback from the ruling Democratic Party, who view it as a blow to judicial reform.
  • Delivery App Fee Caps: Lawmakers across party lines are pushing to cap delivery app commissions as surging fees crush self-employed merchants, though debates remain over how strictly to regulate platform tech giants.
  • Conservative Party Identity Crisis: The conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition, is struggling with flat approval ratings despite the government's missteps, worsened by a looming election law trial involving imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol that threatens about $29 million (39.7 billion KRW) in campaign cost repayments.

As South Korea's annual parliamentary audit enters its second week, political battlelines in Seoul are hardening around institutional control, the gig economy, and opposition struggles. Rather than focusing on routine policy, the current sessions are zeroing in on high-stakes turf wars between the executive branch and investigative agencies. The upcoming confirmation hearing for Jungsucheong nominee Kim Ji-yong on October 14 is shaping up into a major flashpoint, with the opposition aiming to use the vetting process to dismantle the current administration's flagship prosecutorial reforms.

At the same time, lawmakers are turning their attention to pressing livelihood issues, chief among them the crushing commission fees charged by food delivery apps to small business owners. While both the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition agree that relief is needed, deep philosophical differences over market regulation remain. The ruling party favors heavy-handed public control of platform profits, whereas the opposition pushes for lighter market-driven compromises. If politics is meant to alleviate everyday economic pain, the current environment risks turning these urgent livelihood measures into mere legislative tokens.

Opposition Struggles and the Shadow of Yoon Suk-yeol

Perhaps the most baffling dynamic in current South Korean politics is the inability of the conservative People Power Party to capitalize on public dissatisfaction with the ruling administration led by President Lee Jae-myung. Lacking a compelling alternative vision and failing to break free from the ghosts of past administrations, the party's approval ratings remain stubbornly stagnant. Compounding this is the impending court ruling on election law violations tied to imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, which could financially cripple the party if it is forced to return tens of millions of dollars in state-disbursed campaign funds.

Ultimately, the current political theatre highlights a widening gap between what politicians argue about and what ordinary citizens actually face. Whether dealing with bureaucratic power struggles or platform monopolies, the political class is finding it easier to fight over rhetoric than to deliver tangible relief. As this round of parliamentary audits continues, the fundamental test for Korean politics remains unchanged: whether lawmakers can step outside their partisan trenches and address the real economic anxieties of the public.
