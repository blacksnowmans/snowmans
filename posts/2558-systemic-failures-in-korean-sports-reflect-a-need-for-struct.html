Systemic Failures in Korean Sports Reflect a Need for Structural Rebalance

November 20, 2026

South Korea’s national sports programs are facing a crisis of leadership and development, mirroring the physical imbalances that plague modern citizens due to repetitive labor and poor ergonomic habits.

  • Football: Interim manager Moreno’s future is in doubt after an underwhelming 50% win rate (three wins in six matches) failed to provide a clear tactical vision for the national team.
  • Baseball: The national team has extended a humiliating 13-game losing streak against Japan, a drought spanning 11 years that critics blame on a lack of elite starting pitchers.
  • Health: Experts warn that systemic neglect in sports reflects physical neglect in the body, where activities like kimjang (the traditional winter kimchi-making season) and office work are causing chronic wrist and hip issues.
  • Recovery: New rehabilitation techniques, such as the "Rock-Paper-Scissors" stretch and sensory foot exercises, are being promoted to restore balance to overworked muscles and joints.

A Leadership Vacuum in Football and Baseball

The Korea Football Association (KFA), the governing body for soccer in the country, currently finds itself in administrative limbo. With the head of the association effectively absent, the decision-making process for appointing a permanent manager has stalled. While interim manager Moreno recently concluded his tenure with a win, his overall record of 3 wins, 1 draw, and 2 losses is considered mediocre. For a nation that has qualified for 12 consecutive World Cups, there is a growing consensus that the team requires a leader with higher "name value" and a proven tactical philosophy to compete on the world stage.

The situation in baseball is arguably more dire. Since the 2015 Premier 12 tournament, South Korea has been unable to defeat its rival, Japan. This 13-game slide is attributed to a structural failure within the KBO League (South Korea’s professional baseball league). In pursuit of immediate club results, teams often overwork young prospects as short-inning relievers rather than developing them into "ace" starting pitchers like Ryu Hyun-jin. Without a radical shift in how pitchers are nurtured, experts warn the losing streak against Japan could easily extend to 20 games.

Restoring Balance: From Organizations to Anatomy

The same "quick-fix" mentality that hampers sports administration is often applied to physical health. During the winter kimjang season, many Koreans suffer from wrist pain, which they mistakenly treat by massaging the joint itself. However, specialists note that the pain usually stems from the forearm muscles. They recommend the "Muk-Jji-Ppa" (Rock-Paper-Scissors) stretch: applying pressure to the forearm with the opposite thumb while slowly forming a fist, a "scissors" hand, and an open palm. This forced release of the fascia provides more relief than local treatment of the wrist.

Furthermore, the modern reliance on heavily cushioned footwear has "numbed" the sensory receptors in the feet, leading to poor balance and hip misalignment. To counteract this, health experts suggest weight-shifting exercises—moving one’s center of gravity in a circle while standing—to re-engage the brain’s connection with the ground. Just as the national sports teams must move past temporary bandages to fix their developmental foundations, individuals are encouraged to focus on the core "engine" of movement: the hips and the soles of the feet.

"Whether in a sports organization or the human body, ignoring the fundamental structure in favor of temporary fixes eventually leads to a total collapse of performance."
