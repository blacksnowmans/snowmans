South Korean Chipmakers Caught Between Expanded US Export Controls and an Antitrust Trial

November 18, 2026

South Korea’s semiconductor giants face a simultaneous two-front squeeze as Washington closes remote-access loopholes on AI chip exports to China and a US court clears a major DRAM price-fixing lawsuit to proceed to trial.

  • Closing the cloud loophole: The US Department of Commerce expanded export controls beyond physical shipments to block Chinese firms from remotely accessing advanced AI chips via overseas cloud data centers, while replacing flexible "fast-track" procedures with strict individual licensing for five countries across Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Brazil.
  • Supply chain and Nvidia ripples: Washington warned Nvidia it could halt chip supplies altogether if it sells to third-country firms that rent more than half their GPU capacity to Chinese clients, while South Korean foundries and outsourced assembly and test (OSAT) firms operating through Southeast Asia warn of higher logistics costs, delivery delays, and potential disruptions in domestic manufacturing hubs like Daegu-Gyeongbuk.
  • Heavy exposure in China: SK Hynix, which keeps a significant share of its memory manufacturing capacity in China, faces growing uncertainty over exporting those chips elsewhere, while both Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are pouring administrative resources into end-user verification for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), a vital AI component.
  • A multi-year legal fight: A US court rejected motions by Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, and US rival Micron to dismiss a DRAM price-fixing suit—citing "lockstep" output cuts and pricing—exposing the memory trio to years of litigation, internal document discovery, and potential damages reaching trillions of won (potentially billions of dollars).

Our Read: A Double Bind for Korea’s Flagship Industry

What makes Washington’s latest export push particularly unsettling for Seoul is that compliance is no longer confined to shipping manifests. By policing downstream business models and cloud rentals, the US government is effectively forcing hardware makers and component suppliers to act as global regulators. To many in South Korea’s semiconductor sector, security-driven US trade policy increasingly looks like economic nationalism that sidelines allies’ commercial interests and risks chilling global data center investment.

At the same time, the US antitrust ruling threatens the basic survival mechanism of the memory chip market. Memory manufacturing requires massive capital spending, and cutting supply during downturns has long been standard inventory management rather than a cartel tactic. If US courts treat synchronized production cuts as illegal collusion, Samsung and SK Hynix could lose their ability to manage brutal industry cycles, turning a hard-won earnings recovery into a prolonged legal overhang.

Taken together, these dual shocks underscore the limits of South Korea’s export-heavy semiconductor model. With foreign investors already selling off Asian chip leaders like Samsung and Taiwan’s TSMC over geopolitical risk, Korean firms cannot simply wait for the regulatory storm to pass. Diversifying supply routes away from high-risk transit hubs, moving further into specialized high-value chips, and pressing Seoul for stronger diplomatic pushback against unilateral US rules are rapidly becoming matters of industrial survival.
