South Korea Seeks 20-Year Prison Term for Ex-Ruling Party Floor Leader Over Martial Law Role

October 04, 2026

  • South Korea's special counsel has demanded a 20-year prison sentence for Choo Kyung-ho, former floor leader of the conservative People Power Party, on charges of playing a key role in last December's abortive martial law bid.
  • Prosecutors argue Choo deliberately stalled lawmakers from voting to lift the emergency decree by shuttling them between party headquarters and the National Assembly on the night of December 3.
  • The case has become a critical test for the judiciary, with observers warning that a lenient ruling could threaten constitutional order and spark calls for the dissolution of the ruling party.

The push for a 20-year sentence against Choo Kyung-ho marks a major turning point in how South Korea deals with the fallout from the unprecedented December 3 emergency decree declared by impeached President Yoon Suk Yeol. While Choo and his supporters have dismissed the prosecution as a politically motivated attack, investigators have built a detailed timeline showing how he allegedly worked from inside the legislature to paralyze parliament while troops moved outside.

Special prosecutors describe Choo's actions using the traditional metaphor of "jul-tak-dong-si"—where a chick and its mother break the egg from inside and outside simultaneously. According to the state's case, while outside forces tried to seize parliament, Choo kept ruling party lawmakers moving in circles to prevent the quorum needed to vote down the martial law decree. Key testimony from other political figures and officials has further chipped away at the defense's argument that the chaos of the night was merely a series of misunderstandings.

What the Trial Means for Korean Democracy

Beyond an individual prison term, the verdict will establish a legal precedent for how South Korea handles complicity in undermining the constitution. Under South Korean law, insurrection carries the heaviest of penalties, designed to stop attempts to physically dismantle state systems. If the court finds that a senior party leader systematically worked to block parliament's constitutional duty to check executive overreach, the ruling party itself could face legal scrutiny over its institutional complicity.

With a lower court ruling expected soon, all eyes are on the judiciary. For international observers watching South Korea's fragile political ecosystem, the outcome will signal whether the nation's democratic institutions can firmly hold political actors accountable for backroom maneuvers during a constitutional crisis, or whether grey zones will continue to protect political elites.
