Navigating the Storm: Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Party Faces a Political Crossroads

September 16, 2026

The Rebuilding Korea Party, led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, is struggling to transition from a protest movement into a sustainable political force while balancing its complex relationship with the main opposition Democratic Party of Korea (DPK).

  • Leadership Strategy: Party leader Shin Jang-sik is attempting to solidify the party’s base by avoiding the extremes of the political spectrum and focusing on "livelihood politics" through regional outreach.
  • The "Cho Kuk" Effect: Despite the high public recognition of its leader, Cho Kuk, the party faces stagnant single-digit approval ratings, highlighting the difficulty of converting name recognition into structural political support.
  • Independence vs. Alliance: The party is under constant pressure to merge or align closely with the DPK, but leadership remains adamant about maintaining an independent identity to avoid becoming a mere "subsidiary" of the larger party.
  • Reform Agenda: The party has doubled down on its core mission of "prosecution reform," positioning itself as the true successor to the reformist spirit that defined the Moon Jae-in administration.

The Struggle for Independent Identity

For a new party, the Rebuilding Korea Party is experiencing severe "growing pains." While recent polls have shown a slight uptick in approval—from the 2-3% range to 4-5%—this growth is insufficient to challenge the status quo. Shin Jang-sik’s strategy of building "trenches" through grassroots organizing and professionalizing the party’s communications—evidenced by the rise of new spokespeople like Im Myeong-hyeon—is a direct response to critics who view the party as a temporary vehicle for grievance politics.

However, the fundamental challenge remains: the party must prove that its "clarity" goes beyond rhetoric. While the party is praised for its articulate messaging, it has yet to prove its effectiveness in tangible policymaking. The political landscape in South Korea is unforgiving; unless the party can translate its reformist ideals into clear improvements for the average voter, it risks being overshadowed by the institutional weight of the DPK.

Managing the DPK Relationship

The tension between the Rebuilding Korea Party and the DPK is defined by a delicate paradox. The two parties share a common enemy in the Yoon Suk-yeol administration, which necessitates a tactical alliance. Yet, they remain fierce competitors for the same pool of liberal voters. Media speculation regarding a potential merger or "seat-sharing" deals frequently forces the Rebuilding Korea Party to clarify its boundaries.

The party leadership views "seat-sharing" as a form of "anti-politics" that would erode their voter base’s trust. By maintaining distance from the DPK, they hope to preserve their identity as a distinct, reform-driven entity. Whether this "healthy tension" will lead to genuine cooperation or a damaging fracture remains the defining question of their electoral strategy. As the party moves forward, its survival will likely depend on its ability to convince voters that it offers a unique, principled vision for the country, rather than serving as a satellite of the larger liberal establishment.
