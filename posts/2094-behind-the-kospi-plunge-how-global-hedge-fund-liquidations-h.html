Behind the KOSPI Plunge: How Global Hedge Fund Liquidations Hit Korean Equities

November 07, 2026

The recent steep decline in South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI index was driven not by macroeconomic deterioration, but by aggressive forced liquidations from global hedge funds closing out books after their worst performance month in 16 years.

  • The sell-off: The benchmark KOSPI broke below key psychological support at 2,520, while the tech-heavy KOSDAQ fell toward 644, dragged down by foreign net selling exceeding 2.7 trillion won (about $2 billion) in cash equities.
  • The catalyst: Global CTA and momentum funds suffered historic October drawdowns amid sharp swings in tech shares and U.S. Treasury yields, triggering mandatory "big bath" deleveraging and fund unwinds ahead of year-end book closing.
  • The targets: Liquidations at major funds—including Walleye ($17 billion), Engineers Gate ($23 billion), and Situational Awareness 2 ($10 billion)—forced indiscriminate, market-order selling of liquid tech bellwethers like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.
  • Policy response: South Korean financial authorities convened emergency "F4" macro meetings and temporarily waived the mandatory 140% margin loan collateral ratio to protect retail accounts from immediate forced liquidation.

A Supply-Demand Shock Masquerading as a Crisis

South Korea’s domestic stock market became collateral damage in a cross-border unwinding of leveraged bets. Market watchers often attribute foreign outflows to institutional foresight or deteriorating national fundamentals. The reality of this sell-off is far more mechanical: overleveraged quantitative and momentum funds hit strict stop-loss limits and dumped liquid assets at market price to return cash to lenders before closing their books for the year.

Crucially, foreign behavior revealed a sharp divergence between spot and derivatives trading. While dumping cash equities, foreign investors simultaneously net purchased 7,171 KOSPI futures contracts and over 15,500 KOSDAQ futures contracts. This split strategy indicates that offshore capital was mechanically scrubbing losing spot positions while quietly positioning for a technical rebound underneath.

Policy Limits and Macro Anchors

The newly formed administration under President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea has scrambled to contain retail panic. Top economic officials—led by the Finance Ministry, the Bank of Korea, and the Financial Services Commission—have focused on softening forced liquidations for retail margin borrowers. Yet domestic administrative relief cannot fully counteract the global contraction in private equity and hedge fund leverage, particularly as the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission tightens rules on non-transparent pod shops and complex leveraged strategies.

The real signal lies in the foreign exchange market. Despite multi-trillion-won foreign equity dumps, the Korean won held inside a steady band between 1,320 and 1,380 per dollar, anchored by strong current account surpluses. When a market drop leaves the currency largely unbattered, it is a supply-demand shock rather than capital flight fleeing economic decay.
