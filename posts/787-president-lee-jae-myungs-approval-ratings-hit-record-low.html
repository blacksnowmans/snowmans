President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Ratings Hit Record Low

September 16, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing his most significant political crisis to date, with his disapproval rating climbing to a record high of 59.4% in the latest Korea Society Opinion Institute (KSOI) survey.

  • Record Disapproval: The 31st KSOI poll shows only 35.9% of respondents approve of the President's performance, a sharp decline from previous highs.
  • Structural Cracks: The drop is not limited to political opponents; the President’s core base and traditional strongholds across all age groups and regions are showing signs of deep disillusionment.
  • Personnel Controversy: Public backlash against controversial cabinet appointments, including the nominee for Justice Minister, has further eroded faith in the administration.
  • Internal Discord: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's liberal party) is embroiled in turmoil following party leader Kim Min-seok's polarizing remarks regarding potential impeachment.

A Crisis of Confidence

The numbers from the recent KSOI survey are stark. With 40.6% of respondents stating the government is "not doing well in any area," the administration is struggling to find a narrative that resonates with a public fatigued by economic stagnation and a perceived lack of communication. Even the Democratic Party, which has been the President’s primary political vehicle, has seen its support level dip, now nearly tied with the People Power Party, the country's main conservative party.

The core issue appears to be a breakdown in trust. Supporters who once felt represented by this government are now watching from the sidelines, frustrated by what they perceive as political arrogance and a failure to deliver tangible results. The administration's tendency to blame external factors—such as media bias or opposition maneuvers—rather than addressing legitimate grievances has only widened the gap between the Blue House and the public.

The Impeachment Specter and the Road Ahead

The political landscape turned chaotic after Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok invoked the word "impeachment"—a taboo in Korean politics—when discussing the current government's trajectory. By drawing parallels to the 2004 impeachment of the late President Roh Moo-hyun, a traumatic event for the liberal bloc, Kim has inadvertently handed ammunition to the opposition and deeply offended the President’s traditional base.

This internal friction, combined with controversial ministerial appointments that have sparked public outcry, has left the government isolated. Observers argue that the administration is trapped in a cycle of "self-inflicted wounds," where a refusal to compromise on personnel choices is preventing necessary administrative reform.

President Lee is scheduled to hold a press conference on September 18. For the administration to recover, this event cannot be another exercise in scripted rhetoric. It must serve as a genuine moment of introspection. To regain its footing, the government must move beyond superficial marketing and demonstrate a real commitment to reshuffling its inner circle, listening to its disillusioned supporters, and refocusing on the bread-and-butter issues that have been neglected in favor of partisan infighting.
