South Korean Justice Oh Kyung-mi Champions Minority Rights Amid Political Scrutiny

November 20, 2026

South Korean Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyung-mi is challenging the country’s traditionally elitist judiciary by arguing that courts exist above all to shield vulnerable minorities when majority rule fails them.

  • Breaking the judicial mold: A 25-year veteran judge and the daughter of a farmer from Iksan in southwestern South Korea, Oh argues her non-elite background pushes her to prioritize ordinary people's lived realities over rigid legalism.
  • Landmark protections: According to a Korean profile of her career, Oh broke conservative precedent by recognizing refugee status for a bisexual Ugandan woman, protected rural and West Sea island residents from speculators exploiting gaps in historical land records, and linked verbal school bullying and hate speech to a student’s suicide.
  • Gender-sensitive judging: Countering critics who fear bias in gender-related cases, Oh maintains that understanding a victim’s context removes male-centric blind spots without weakening the presumption of innocence or a defendant's rights.
  • Political pushback: During her confirmation hearing, lawmakers pressed Oh over public distrust in the courts—citing a disputed Supreme Court ruling that cleared current President Lee Jae-myung (then a provincial governor) of election-law violations, as well as long delays in a trial involving former Justice Minister Cho Kuk’s family.

Expanding the Bench’s Field of Vision

In a South Korean legal establishment often criticized as insulated and dominated by prestigious university networks, Oh’s emphasis on empathy marks a distinct shift. The profile notes that her philosophy was shaped early on by "Wonju Correspondence," an essay collection by acclaimed author Park Kyung-ni that instilled in her a deep reverence for human life. Rather than acting as a mechanical rule-enforcer, Oh frames the courts as a place to find a "point of peace" where clashing generations, genders, and ideologies can coexist without silencing the weakest party—a view reflected in her willingness to recognize the unwritten history of remote islanders or the fatal toll of non-physical bullying.

The Neutrality Tightrope

Yet bringing an explicitly minority-focused lens to the Supreme Court carries political risk in a deeply polarized South Korea. At her confirmation hearing, lawmaker Yoo Sang-bum attacked the earlier ruling on President Lee’s televised debate remarks as absurd, questioning whether the top court had bowed to political power, while pointing to Judge Kim Mi-ri’s prolonged delays in the Cho Kuk family case as signs of partisan bias.

Oh navigated that scrutiny cautiously, declining to comment directly on President Lee’s ruling while acknowledging the legal debate over criminal intent, and agreeing that protracted trial delays are unacceptable and judicial assignments must stay free of political favoritism. Her ongoing test on the bench is convincing skeptics that empathy for the marginalized reinforces—rather than compromises—impartial justice, a balance she says she guards by keeping two letters from past litigants: one thanking her, and one harshly rebuking her.
