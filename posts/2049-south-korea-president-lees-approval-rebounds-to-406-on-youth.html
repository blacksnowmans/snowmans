South Korea: President Lee’s Approval Rebounds to 40.6% on Youth Surge, but Ruling Party Faces Hidden Risks

November 06, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s job approval rating has climbed back above the 40% threshold to 40.6%, buoyed by a double-digit jump among voters in their twenties, according to a survey by local pollster NewsTomato.

  • Youth vote powers the rebound: Approval among voters aged 18 to 29 surged by 12.7 percentage points into the 40% range, reversing recent alienation and recharging governing momentum for the administration following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • Deadlock at the top: The main conservative opposition People Power Party and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea remain locked in a virtual dead heat at 37.7% and 37.6% respectively, separated by just 0.1 percentage point within the margin of error.
  • Third-party presence holds: The Rebuilding Korea Party secured 8.7% support, followed by the conservative splinter Reform Party at 2.4% and the leftist Progressive Party at 1.5%.
  • Defections in election simulation: When asked which party’s candidate they would support if a parliamentary election were held tomorrow, support for the Rebuilding Korea Party jumped to 11%, showing clear leakage away from Democratic Party candidates.

Youth Volatility and the Illusion of Internet Politics

The 12.7-point spike among twenty-somethings reveals just how volatile South Korea’s younger demographic remains. Analysts attribute part of this shift to shifting sentiment across online platforms such as FM Korea (known locally as "Femco"), a male-dominated community forum. However, treating viral internet sentiment as a solid political base is risky. In the same poll, 60% of respondents in their twenties rated public remarks by prominent progressive writer and commentator Rhyu Si-min as appropriate (versus 30% inappropriate)—directly contradicting the loud, anti-progressive rhetoric typical of anonymous forums. Online outrage does not reflect the broader youth electorate, and tailoring governance to noisy message boards obscures core structural issues like youth unemployment and housing costs.

A Warning Sign for the Ruling Democratic Party

For the ruling Democratic Party, these numbers provide little reason to celebrate. Even with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol removed from power and behind bars, the ruling bloc has failed to pull ahead of the battered conservative opposition. The political tailwind from Mr. Yoon’s downfall has run its course, and swing voters appear hesitant to offer unconditional backing without visible legislative and economic results.

The most telling metric is the simulated general election ballot, where support for the Rebuilding Korea Party climbed to 11% while the Democratic Party saw its voter intent soften. Reform-minded voters are signaling that their allegiance cannot be taken for granted. If the ruling camp mistakes praise within social media echo chambers like X (formerly Twitter) for broad national consensus, it risks drifting out of touch with working-class realities and driving reform-minded voters into the arms of sharper third-party alternatives.
