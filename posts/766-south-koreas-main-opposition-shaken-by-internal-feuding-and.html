South Korea's Main Opposition Shaken by Internal Feuding and Factional Clashes

September 15, 2026

South Korea's main opposition Democratic Party is facing severe internal friction as top figures trade political blows, risking voter support through infighting and perceived factional maneuvering.

    Democratic Party Infighting: Supreme Council Member Kim Min-seok publicly invoked the taboo word "impeachment" while criticizing senior party figure Choo Mi-ae, drawing swift backlash from party supporters. Think Tank Controversy: Kim's push to appoint loyalist lawmaker Kang Duk-goo to lead the Democratic Research Institute has sparked accusations of privatizing the party's central policy hub. Strengthened Rivals: Aggressive targeting of Choo Mi-ae has ironically boosted her standing as a leading political contender, particularly among core party bases in Gyeonggi Province. Political Fallout: Critics argue that prioritizing internal purges over broader vision risks alienating voters and eroding the party's hard-won momentum.

The Perils of Invoking Impeachment

When a senior politician publicly floats the specter of impeachment against a fellow party member, the psychic damage to the base is immediate. Kim Min-seok's comparison of Choo Mi-ae’s high-profile policy advocacy to the disastrous 2004 impeachment of former President Roh Moo-hyun was meant as a warning against disunity. Instead, it planted the radioactive word "impeachment" firmly in the public discourse. In South Korean politics, invoking historical traumas associated with the downfall of progressive presidents is a dangerous game that usually alienates weary voters.

Compounding these missteps is the controversy surrounding the leadership of the Democratic Research Institute, the party's key policy think tank. Installing a close ally at the helm of an institution meant to generate broad, inclusive strategies sends a clear message that loyalty trumps competence. Rather than functioning as an open intellectual engine for the party, the institute risks becoming a personal fiefdom. For an opposition party hoping to project readiness for national leadership, such heavy-handed consolidation of power looks less like strength and more like political insecurity.

How Targeted Attacks Backfire

The campaign to marginalize Choo Mi-ae within the party is having the exact opposite of its intended effect. As a seasoned political operator and former justice minister, Choo has weathered far tougher battles than those launched by party detractors. By painting her as a disruptive force, leadership has inadvertently cast her in the role of a persecuted fighter for reform, driving up her numbers in local leadership polls.

Ultimately, these episodes highlight a recurring blind spot among party strategists who mistake internal compliance for public mandate. When progressive politics shifts away from broad reform and toward the containment of internal rivals, the base notices. Unless leadership pivots from managing factional rivalries to addressing substantive national issues, the Democratic Party's greatest threat in upcoming political contests will come not from its conservative opponents, but from its own self-inflicted wounds.
