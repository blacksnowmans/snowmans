South Korea Fast-Tracks Seoul Housing Starts and Targets Farmland Deregulation

October 31, 2026

The South Korean government and the ruling Democratic Party launched a coordinated push on October 30 to accelerate urban housing construction in Seoul and legalise long-standing informal farmland usage across the country.

  • Seoul speedup: Prime Minister Han Seong-sook inspected a redevelopment site in Seoul's Eunpyeong District, ordering ministries to clear administrative bottlenecks and shorten construction timelines.
  • 31,000-unit target: Hong Ji-sun, head of the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, pledged support to break ground on 31,000 private redevelopment homes in Seoul before year-end, while negotiating a 1.2-times floor area ratio relaxation with local authorities.
  • Farmland reform: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea announced a legislative push to pass a special farmland bill by the end of the year to legalize customary leasing and unauthorized land-use changes.
  • Transit disputes: In surrounding Gyeonggi Province, opposition members of the local council in Gimpo clashed over a planned 37,000-unit housing development, demanding rail infrastructure commitments before building proceeds.

Unclogging Bottlenecks in Capital Housing

The government's high-visibility site visits highlight official anxiety over a impending housing supply cliff in the capital. Soaring residential costs and stalled projects—trapped by rising material prices and builder-homeowner squabbles over construction costs—have brought public frustration to a boiling point. By targeting actual groundbreakings for 31,000 private sector units rather than announcing distant paper approvals, the Land Ministry is pivoting toward concrete, short-term results. Offering floor area ratio increases of up to 20 percent above legal limits aims to improve project profitability, though balancing higher urban density with local infrastructure requirements will test municipal cooperation.

Land Reform and Transit Friction Beyond Seoul

Outside the capital, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the progressive party aligned with President Lee Jae-myung—is touching a sensitive area of land policy by seeking to legalize informal agricultural leases and land-use conversions. While designed to provide legal relief to an aging rural population that can no longer farm its own land, critics caution that the measure risks granting accidental amnesty to corporate land speculators. Meanwhile, suburban development projects like the 37,000-unit Gimpo-Ara plan face sharp pushback from the main conservative opposition People Power Party, which argues that adding thousands of households without expanding overcrowded commuter rail lines will turn new developments into isolated transit bottlenecks.
