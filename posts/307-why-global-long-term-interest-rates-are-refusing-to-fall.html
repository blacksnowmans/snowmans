Why Global Long-Term Interest Rates Are Refusing to Fall

September 07, 2026

  • What happened: Despite the U.S. Federal Reserve cutting its benchmark interest rate, long-term bond yields are rising rather than falling, defying traditional economic expectations.
  • Why it's happening: Structural shifts—including stubborn inflation, massive government spending for de-globalization, and a ballooning U.S. national debt—are driving up the term premium.
  • Who is affected: Global stock and bond markets, overleveraged household and corporate borrowers, and export-driven economies like South Korea facing tighter capital markets.

The global economy has entered a permanent break from the low-inflation, low-interest-rate era of the past 40 years. While the central bank's rate cuts grab headlines, the underlying mechanics of the bond market are signaling a harsh new reality: 10-year Treasury yields pushing toward 5% are no longer a fringe warning, but a structural fixture.

This decoupling happens because the market is finally pricing in America's unsustainable fiscal trajectory. When the Federal Reserve cuts rates, bond investors interpret it as an agreement to issue even more government debt, demanding higher yields to absorb the supply. At the same time, massive capital demands for AI infrastructure and supply-chain reshoring are locking in high real interest rates. Washington's heavy reliance on short-term debt issuance only compounds the vulnerability, leaving financial systems exposed to rapid shifts in borrowing costs.

For investors and policymakers, this demands an urgent mindset shift. The era of cheap money is gone, punishing speculative plays and rewarding cash-generative value assets. Whether technological innovation via artificial intelligence can grow the global GDP fast enough to outpace mounting sovereign debt remains the ultimate open question—and the answer will define the next decade of market survival.
