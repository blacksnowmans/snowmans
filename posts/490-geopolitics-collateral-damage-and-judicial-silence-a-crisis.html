Geopolitics, Collateral Damage, and Judicial Silence: A Crisis of Empathy

September 09, 2026

    U.S. and Israeli Airstrikes in Iran: Recent strikes killed around 170 children and teachers at an elementary school in southern Iran, drawing fierce international criticism over civilian casualties and violations of international law.
  • Strait of Hormuz Standoff: As Iran threatens to choke the crucial oil shipping lane, foreign pressure mounts on South Korea to join multinational deployments—a move critics warn would entangle Seoul in dangerous great-power conflicts.
  • South Korea’s Judiciary in Turmoil: Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae faces backlash for bypassing traditional consultation processes to fill vacant Supreme Court seats via written appointments, drawing accusations of dodging political accountability.
  • The Cost of Apathy: Across both foreign policy and domestic governance, elites are trading foundational legal and human principles for self-preservation, threatening public trust at home and stability abroad.

The Brutal Reality of Power Politics in the Middle East

When diplomatic nuclear talks collapsed in Geneva, the Trump administration bypassed negotiation in favor of force, launching aggressive airstrikes inside Iran. Among the casualties was the Shajareh Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab, where faulty intelligence misidentified the facility as a former Revolutionary Guard site. The resulting blast killed roughly 170 children and teachers, sparking international outrage and accusations of war crimes from groups like Amnesty International. While Western leaders including French President Emmanuel Macron and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre have criticized the strikes as operating outside international law, the response from Washington remains muted.

This escalation has immediately endangered global trade by turning the Strait of Hormuz—through which about 20% of the world's oil passes—into a flashpoint. For South Korea, a country heavily reliant on stable energy imports, the crisis has revived intense pressure from Washington to dispatch naval forces to the area. However, joining a U.S.-led coalition would effectively tie Seoul to a unilateral military intervention while doing nothing to resolve the underlying diplomatic crisis. Instead of chasing short-term alignment, South Korea's safest and most principled path lies in multilateral frameworks centered on the United Nations and humanitarian corridors.

Judicial Independence Undermined at Home

Domestically, South Korea’s legal foundations are facing their own crisis of leadership. Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has left two Supreme Court vacancies hanging for over half a year before attempting to push through nominees via an unorthodox written appointment method, sidestepping formal consensus with the presidential office to dodge political blowback. This administrative sleight-of-hand follows a pattern of institutional silence from the country's highest court during major constitutional tests, including past martial law controversies.

The danger of this institutional cowardice was highlighted by outgoing Supreme Court Justice Lee Heung-goo in his farewell address, where he bluntly criticized the judiciary for failing to speak up during national crises, warning that silence eventually robs an institution of its voice entirely. When top legal authorities prioritize self-preservation over constitutional defense, the judiciary ceases to be a safeguard for the public and becomes merely an instrument of convenience for those in power.
