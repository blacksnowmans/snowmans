Cat Foreign Body Removal: A Quick Practical Brief

October 22, 2026

My 5-year-old, roughly 6.5 kg orange-and-white cat, Hongeo, had a foreign body removed at Suwon SKY Animal Medical Center after swallowing several cords that remained trapped in his stomach.

Warning signs I missed

  • Repeated daily retching: Following a two-week stay at another home, Hongeo began hacking and dry-heaving for 5 to 10 seconds at a time, stretching his neck forward as if trying to vomit.
  • Delayed regurgitation: He vomited small amounts of digested food roughly 4 hours after eating (once on Saturday and once on Monday).
  • Very small vomit volume: The regurgitated food was only one or two spoonfuls, which led me to mistake it for simple indigestion or hairballs.
  • Completely normal appetite and energy: He continued asking for food, playing with toys, and acting as affectionate as usual, which masked how serious the problem actually was.

What the vet did

Here is the timeline of our care at Suwon SKY Animal Medical Center in Suwon, South Korea:

  • Arrival (~1:00 PM): We arrived at the clinic to investigate the persistent gagging episodes.
  • X-ray: Initial radiographs suggested a foreign body might be present in the stomach, though soft linear materials cannot always be clearly identified on standard X-rays alone.
  • Abdominal ultrasound: The ultrasound confirmed foreign material inside the stomach and verified that it had not passed into the intestine.
  • Endoscopy under general anesthesia: The veterinary team placed Hongeo under general anesthesia to attempt gastroscopic removal, maintaining a backup plan to convert immediately to open abdominal surgery if the foreign object could not be extracted safely via endoscopy.
  • Successful extraction: The scope successfully retrieved two of my own trouser drawstrings and a long, tangled wad of unknown cord without requiring open surgery.
  • Discharge (~7:30 PM): Hongeo was monitored until he recovered from anesthesia and was discharged that evening with instructions to feed only about 1/3 of his normal food portion; at home, he showed a slight limp on his left hind leg that needed checking with the clinic.

Takeaways

  • Normal behavior does not rule out foreign objects: Cats can maintain normal energy, purr, and eat eagerly even while carrying a substantial bundle of cords in their stomach.
  • Take repetitive retching seriously: Any recurring gagging or vomiting of digested food several hours post-meal should be checked with a vet, even if the volume produced is minimal.
  • Guard against strings and drawstrings: Linear items like cords, ribbons, and garment strings are hazardous and should never be left where a cat can chew or swallow them.
  • Follow your vet's specific care plan: Endoscopic removal was only possible because the cords were still in the stomach and had not entered the intestines; post-procedure care depends on mucosal recovery, so always follow the exact guidance and feeding directions of your own veterinarian.

Note: This is a personal account of my experience and is not veterinary advice. Please consult your own veterinarian for diagnosis and treatment plans for your pet.
