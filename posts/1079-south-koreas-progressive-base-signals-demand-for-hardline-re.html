South Korea’s Progressive Base Signals Demand for Hardline Reform

October 13, 2026

A recent real-time poll conducted during a live broadcast on the political YouTube channel Park Si-young TV has revealed a deep-seated preference among liberal voters for confrontational, reform-oriented political figures over establishment moderates.

  • The Top Contenders: Former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook led the poll with 33%, followed by Rep. Choo Mi-ae (28%), Rep. Jung Chung-rae (22%), and Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party (21%).
  • The Shift: The survey highlights a growing disconnect between institutional opinion polls and the "raw" sentiment of the progressive base, which favors politicians who have aggressively challenged the previous administration.
  • What it Changes: The results serve as a direct signal to the ruling Democratic Party—the party currently led by President Lee Jae-myung—that its base expects a more radical approach to judicial and media reform rather than cautious governance.

The Rise of "Combat-Ready" Politicians

The YouTube poll, while informal, serves as a "barometer of public temperature" that traditional polling often misses. By excluding these figures from official candidate lists, mainstream political analysts risk alienating the core supporters who feel that the current political climate—under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—still requires a "clear and combat-ready" stance against the remnants of the past.

The figures who topped the poll—Choi, Choo, Jung, and Cho—are known for their combative history, particularly their roles in opposing the previous administration under the now-imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol. Supporters see them not merely as politicians, but as the vanguard of reform, willing to face the "blades" of the opposition and the prosecution. For these voters, the primary goal remains the systematic dismantling of the corruption that flourished during the Yoon era.

Reform: Survival, Not Just Policy

While the mainstream media and established political circles often frame these figures as "too extreme," the results indicate that the progressive base views such characterizations as evidence of the establishment's inability to read the room. For many voters, the reform of the judiciary, the prosecution, and the media is not a matter of political negotiation; it is a matter of survival.

The persistent support for these individuals reflects a belief that despite the recent change in power, the country’s core institutions remain untransformed. The message from the "YouTube base" is unambiguous: they are not looking for polite compromise. They are looking for leaders who will "wield the hammer" of reform firmly. As the Lee Jae-myung government continues its term, it faces a clear choice—listen to the grassroots demand for bold action, or risk losing the support of those who were the architects of the current political shift.
