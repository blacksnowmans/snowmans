South Korea’s Ministry of Justice is Back Under Prosecutor Control, and the Media is Failing to Police It

October 02, 2026

  • What happened: Despite past pledges to de-prosecutize South Korea's Ministry of Justice, prosecutors have retaken key bureaus, while dozens of active-duty prosecutors remain embedded across 18 government agencies.
  • Who is affected: Financial regulators, administrative bodies, and the broader public, as the boundary between investigating and governing blurs under heavy prosecutorial influence.
  • What it changes: Democratic oversight is effectively sidelined, turning parts of the bureaucracy into an extension of the prosecutors' network while mainstream media hides behind a false veil of neutrality.

The Quiet Re-Prosecutization of the Ministry of Justice

The push to strip prosecutors of administrative posts—a major policy goal of the liberal Moon Jae-in administration aimed at ending the prosecution's monopoly on power—has effectively been reversed. A close look at current personnel sheets shows that key bureaus within the Ministry of Justice, including the prosecution bureau, human rights bureau, and legal affairs divisions, are heavily staffed by active-duty prosecutors. This goes beyond routine staffing: it places core policy-making decisions directly back into the hands of the prosecution, turning an oversight body into a de facto command center for prosecutorial interests.

This reach extends well beyond the ministry. As of September 2026, 27 prosecutors are dispatched across 18 outside government agencies. High-profile outposts like the Financial Services Commission and the Financial Supervisory Service give prosecutors leverage over corporate investigations, wealth, and power. Critics point out that these deployments are less about administrative cooperation and more about information gathering and exerting leverage over state policy.

Mainstream Media, Fake Neutrality, and the Rise of New Media

While the state apparatus sees power consolidate around prosecutors, the country's mainstream press faces its own crisis of credibility. Legacy media outlets continue to style themselves as neutral, objective observers while acting as active political players and attacking independent or alternative creators. Outlets frequently dismiss YouTube commentators and alternative platforms as biased, yet they routinely mask their own partisan stances behind the banner of objective journalism.

This double standard has alienated readers. When legacy media organizations critique public figures or alternative commentators for being partisan while ignoring their own institutional biases, they forfeit their moral authority. True journalism requires rigorous self-criticism and an honest acknowledgment of political leanings, rather than hiding behind a pretense of neutrality while participating in the political fray.
