South Korea's Media Upheaval: YouTube Outlets Overtake Legacy TV as Scandals Rock Seoul

September 23, 2026

South Korea's political landscape is shifting rapidly, marked by a major YouTube political talk show beating traditional terrestrial networks in media trust, a presidential approval rating bounce amid mounting diplomatic and domestic pressures, and high-profile scandals hitting both the judiciary and ruling party ranks.

  • YouTube Dominance: The popular political YouTube program Kim Ou-joon's Humble is Hard News Factory ranked as South Korea's most trusted broadcasting program (6.3%), beating out major network news desks like MBC and KBS.
  • Judicial Scandal: Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de faces intense scrutiny and public outrage following revelations that court officials allegedly inflated meal headcounts to bypass spending caps and use public funds for luxury dining.
  • High-Profile Resignation: Justice Minister nominee Kim Seung-won abruptly resigned following explosive whistle-blower allegations of past misconduct and an alleged attempt to silence former aides.
  • Approval Rebound: President Lee Jae-myung saw his approval rating tick up to 34.8% after a 9-week slide, driven by a recent presidential press conference, though deep public skepticism remains.

The Death of Legacy Media's Monopoly

The latest media credibility survey by SisaIN serves as a stark reminder of how fast South Korea's information ecosystem is fragmenting. For decades, traditional terrestrial broadcasters held a virtual monopoly on public discourse. Today, a digital-native YouTube broadcast operating without physical transmission towers commands the top spot for public trust. Yet, this success underscores a deeply polarized media environment. Major outlets like MBC simultaneously rank first in trust and first in distrust, proving that the public no longer seeks neutral messengers—they want validation from "their side" of the political aisle.

At the same time, the broader political scene remains plagued by old habits. South Korea's diplomatic and security apparatus continues to play defense on the global stage, reacting defensively to foreign pressures rather than setting its own agenda. Meanwhile, the recent downfall of senior figures over ethical lapses and lavish spending exposes a persistent entitlement among elite institutions. When those tasked with upholding the law—whether in the judiciary or executive branch—treat public funds like private pocket money, it erodes the foundational trust required to govern.

A Fragile Recovery for the Executive

President Lee's slight bump in the polls offers a brief window of relief after weeks of continuous decline. Direct communication through a recent press conference helped stop the bleeding, particularly among moderate voters weary of endless political bickering. However, beneath the surface numbers lies a stubborn warning: a significant portion of former supporters now express active disillusionment, signaling that old loyalties are wearing thin.

Ultimately, rhetoric and media victories will not sustain political momentum. As the public makes clear through every poll and drop in support, the singular demand is the recovery of livelihood and economic stability. If Seoul's leadership fails to shift focus away from internal factional squabbles and toward tangible solutions for everyday citizens, even the most carefully managed political rebounds will prove short-lived.
