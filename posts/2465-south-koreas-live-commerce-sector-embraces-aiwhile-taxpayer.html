South Korea’s Live Commerce Sector Embraces AI—While Taxpayer-Funded Projects Face Backlash

November 18, 2026

South Korea's live commerce landscape is rapidly splitting into two realities: private platforms leveraging artificial intelligence and aggressive Black Friday discounts to drive record engagement, and costly public sector initiatives coming under fire for mismanaging municipal budgets.

  • AI moves to center stage: KT Alpha Shopping, a leading Korean home shopping operator, rolled out real-time AI product summaries and expanded virtual show hosts to automate broadcasts and cut fixed labor costs.
  • Black Friday price wars begin: Fashion vertical platform Ably kicked off its "Ab-Fri" campaign offering discounts up to 93%, alongside star-studded influencer streams on major portal Naver's Shopping Live.
  • Public e-commerce sparks scandal: A municipal live commerce program in Jeonju faced severe criticism for spending 5.8 billion won (about $4.2 million) in public funds while generating negligible sales and relying on non-competitive contracts.
  • Legacy retail channels collapse: Sponsored door-to-door sales at major consumer goods firms like LG Household & Health Care have halved to under 1 trillion won (about $720 million), accelerating the industry's shift toward mobile broadcasting.

Private Innovation vs. Bureaucratic Missteps

The Korean retail market is witnessing a technological shift toward data-driven live broadcasting. KT Alpha Shopping's recent interface overhaul replaces traditional host chatter with automated, real-time data summaries and interactive AI chat systems. Combined with the deployment of 24/7 virtual hosts, major operators are aggressively pursuing operational efficiency. This automated push comes alongside massive year-end discount campaigns from platforms like Ably and Gmarket, where live streams serve as real-time conversion engines fueled by limited-time coupons and creator marketing.

However, this private-sector momentum stands in stark contrast to government-funded live commerce initiatives. In the southwestern city of Jeonju, local council member Shin Dong-i exposed the dismal performance of the city's "Sodam Square" project, revealing that it spent roughly 10,000 won of taxpayer money for every 3,000 won in generated sales. Critics point to opaque sole-source contracting and a fundamental misunderstanding of commercial audience dynamics. As legacy offline sales methods collapse, the lesson for South Korean policymakers is clear: subsidizing bureaucratic, low-engagement broadcasts cannot substitute for genuine market competitiveness.
