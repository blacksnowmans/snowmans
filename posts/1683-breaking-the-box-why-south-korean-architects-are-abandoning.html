Breaking the Box: Why South Korean Architects Are Abandoning the Square

October 29, 2026

  • Architects Lim Hyung-nam and Roh Eun-joo are challenging the monotony of South Korea's "apartment-centric" urban landscape through fluid, non-linear residential designs.
  • Their latest project utilizes a "sunlight well"—a curved central structure—to maximize natural light and eliminate the sterile feeling of traditional rectangular floor plans.
  • The design emphasizes vertical connectivity and "asymmetric sightlines," allowing for privacy while maintaining an open, airy atmosphere in dense urban areas.
  • By integrating flexible commercial and residential spaces, the architects aim to create "living" buildings that adapt to the changing needs of their owners.

In South Korean cities, where the skyline is dominated by repetitive, uniform apartment blocks—often described by critics as "rectangular prisons"—architects Lim Hyung-nam and Roh Eun-joo are attempting a quiet revolution. Their recent work proves that even in cramped urban lots, clever geometric intervention can fundamentally alter a resident's psychological well-being. By swapping standard right angles for curved walls and unconventional floor plans, they are creating spaces that provide a much-needed antidote to modern architectural monotony.

The core of this design philosophy is the "sunlight well," a curved internal void that pulls natural light deep into the center of the building. This is not just a stylistic choice; it is a direct response to the lack of openness in typical South Korean commercial-residential buildings. By organizing rooms around these curves rather than rigid, boxy perimeters, the architects create a rhythmic, unfolding experience that changes as the resident moves through the home. It is a bold departure from the standard "cookie-cutter" layouts that define much of the country's residential real estate.

Architecture as a Catalyst for Urban Change

Beyond the internal aesthetics, Lim and Roh view these buildings as catalysts for "biological succession" within a neighborhood. They argue that one well-designed, non-traditional building can trigger a domino effect, inspiring surrounding property owners to move away from mundane, utilitarian construction. In a market where developers often prioritize short-term profit over long-term livability, this focus on "adaptive architecture"—where the building can transition from a bakery to a studio or a home—represents a shift toward sustainable urban regeneration.

Ultimately, these projects pose a provocative question to the average urban dweller: Why do we accept the standard square room as the only way to live? The architects suggest that our environment dictates our emotional state. By moving away from the "square box" culture, they are not just building houses—they are trying to restore the playfulness and unpredictability that modern, standardized living has systematically stripped away. Whether these daring designs will spark a broader trend remains to be seen, but they provide a compelling blueprint for how to inject life back into Korea's rigid, concrete-filled city centers.
