Internal Power Struggles Shake South Korea’s Main Opposition

September 07, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s main liberal opposition party, has concluded its national convention, but the event has left the party grappling with deep internal fissures rather than celebrating a unified path forward.

  • New Leadership: Kim Min-seok has been elected party leader, with a new Supreme Council featuring Han Min-soo, Choi Min-hee, Park Seon-won, Seo Mi-hwa, and Lee Sung-yoon.
  • Controversial Tactics: Leaked Telegram messages revealing internal plans to "seize control" and "neutralize" rivals have sparked public outcry, exposing a culture of backroom maneuvering.
  • Shift in Voting: The convention marked the debut of a "one member, one vote" system for party loyalists, resulting in razor-thin margins that underscored deep-seated factionalism.
  • Governance Fallout: The party’s focus on internal "shadow wars" is complicating its ability to challenge the current government during high-stakes personnel confirmation hearings.

The High Cost of Internal "Shadow Wars"

The convention was intended to solidify the party under the influence of the current administration’s supporters, but it instead devolved into a survival game. Han Min-soo, a newly elected Supreme Council member, was targeted by rival factions within the party as a "traitor," a label used by those seeking to consolidate their own power by sidelining others. The leaked Telegram messages between a senior party ethics official and a lawmaker—discussing "seizing control" of the party leadership—have confirmed the worst suspicions of the party's base: that internal oversight mechanisms are being weaponized for personal gain.

This factional infighting is taking place at a critical time. While the DPK should be focusing on challenging the government, its members are increasingly distracted by "internal hits." The reliance on "pro-Lee" (loyal to President Lee Jae-myung) or "anti-Lee" labels has created a toxic environment where policy debate is sacrificed for the sake of survival. For the party’s base, the sight of self-proclaimed "President's picks" acting as gatekeepers is not a show of strength, but a sign of systemic rot.

A Test of Legitimacy and Governance

Beyond the party’s internal dramas, the DPK is facing a difficult landscape in the National Assembly. During current personnel confirmation hearings, the ruling People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) has launched aggressive campaigns against DPK-backed candidates like Kim Seung-won and Ryu Ho-jeong, using old investigation records from the era of former Prosecutor General (now President) Yoon Suk-yeol. The DPK finds itself on the defensive, struggling to balance its role as an opposition watchdog with the need to maintain its own moral authority.

The DPK stands at a crossroads. The transition to a "member-sovereign" voting system was designed to democratize the party, but it has paradoxically amplified the intensity of factional competition. If the leadership cannot move past these "old man politics" and stop the internal "character assassination" of its own members, it risks alienating the voters who look to the party for an alternative to the status quo. The party’s ability to function as a unified force will determine not just its success in future elections, but its viability as a governing entity.
