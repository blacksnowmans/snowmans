South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Ratings Hit Critical Low

September 24, 2026

  • Record Low: President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has plummeted to 30.6%, with disapproval reaching 66.1%, signaling a potential collapse of his core support base.
  • Youth Exodus: Support among voters in their 30s has cratered to 20.1%, while traditional strongholds in the Honam region are showing significant cracks.
  • Legal Pressure: Nearly 78% of respondents believe the President's ongoing trials should resume immediately, highlighting a major public trust deficit.
  • Institutional Clash: The administration is now locked in a fierce power struggle with the judiciary over the appointment of Supreme Court justices, with some ruling party lawmakers even calling for the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

A Crisis of Confidence and Core Support

President Lee Jae-myung, leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), is facing a defining crisis as his administration fails to stem a rapid decline in public support. Recent polling by Research Well shows that the "30% floor"—a psychological threshold for South Korean presidents—is now under direct threat. More alarmingly, 56% of respondents chose the option "very bad" when rating the President's performance, indicating that this is not a reaction to specific policies, but a fundamental loss of trust in the direction of the government.

The desertion of voters in their 30s and 40s—the bedrock of Lee’s electoral victory—is particularly damning. Having campaigned on promises of fairness and sweeping reform, the administration is now viewed by its former supporters as having abandoned those ideals. Economic strain, combined with a perceived lack of transparency in judicial and prosecutorial reforms, has left middle-of-the-road voters feeling alienated. With four years left in his term, the President faces the specter of a prolonged "lame duck" period if he cannot pivot to address these mounting grievances.

Internal Fractures and Judicial Warfare

The administration’s isolation is deepened by its increasingly defensive posture toward its own traditional allies. Renowned public intellectual Rhyu Si-min, once a staunch supporter, recently issued a scathing critique, comparing the administration’s refusal to address minor political failures to "ignoring a pebble in one's shoe until it causes permanent injury." Rather than welcoming this feedback, the administration has reportedly moved to distance itself from critics, opting to rely on a tighter circle of loyalist media figures. This echo-chamber approach is widely seen as a tactical error that further alienates the moderate public.

Compounding these political woes is an escalating standoff with the judiciary. The administration is currently at loggerheads with Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over the appointment of Supreme Court justices, with the government claiming the Chief Justice’s refusal to defer to the President's will is an obstruction of executive authority. Some DPK lawmakers, viewing this as a form of "judicial coup," have initiated efforts to draft an impeachment motion against Cho. Whether this aggressive maneuver will reclaim lost ground or backfire by appearing as an overreach remains to be seen. For now, the focus of the administration appears stuck on survival and conflict, leaving the public’s pressing concerns regarding the economy and institutional reform effectively sidelined.
