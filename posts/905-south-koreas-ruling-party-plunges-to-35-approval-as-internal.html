South Korea's Ruling Party Plunges to 35% Approval as Internal Cracks Widening

October 10, 2026

  • What happened: Approval for the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party, has slumped to 35%, with support in key demographics like voters in their 30s and the Seoul region plummeting to 29%.
  • What it changes: The freefall shatters the administration's political momentum and exposes deep internal fractures over party leadership, endless superficial committees, and botched government appointments under President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Who is affected: Party leader Kim Min-seok faces mounting insurgent pressure from lawmakers, while core liberal supporters have shifted from active backing to cold, arms-length skepticism.

South Korea's political landscape is flashing bright red for the progressive camp. Despite a high-profile, four-hour closed-door meeting between President Lee Jae-myung and former party chief Jeong Cheong-rae intended to steady the ship, frustration is boiling over within the Democratic Party. Lawmakers are openly rebelling against party leader Kim Min-seok, pointing to his unvarnished hardline rhetoric and an obsession with launching dozens of toothless, showy task forces that yield zero tangible policy results. Compounding the misery is a lingering personnel crisis: weeks after a series of appointment scandals forced the president's chief secretary to offer his resignation, the administration has failed to deliver the promised cabinet refresh.

The core of the crisis, however, goes deeper than administrative sluggishness—it is about a leadership style out of touch with public sentiment. Critics and commentators argue that President Lee and his allies view political dissent entirely through a transactional lens, dismissing honest internal criticism and public unease as mere self-interest or political posturing. At the same time, controversial overhauls to the prosecution system are being driven through with partisan motives, prompting sharp warnings that these very tools could easily backfire if power ever returns to the conservative People Power Party. Rather than listening to the rising wave of discontent, the leadership risks alienating the vital middle ground that brought them to power.

This drop to 35% is not a routine fluctuation; it is a structural warning light from an electorate that feels let down. When leaders retreat into defensive factionalism and dismiss public skepticism as bad faith, they trade away their mandate for reform. Unless the ruling camp abandons its fixation on narrow political calculations and meets the public with genuine humility, this cooling-off period among voters risks hardening into permanent alienation.
