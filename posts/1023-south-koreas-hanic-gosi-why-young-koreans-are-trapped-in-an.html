South Korea’s "Hanic-gosi": Why Young Koreans Are Trapped in an Endless Cycle of Job Exams

October 12, 2026

South Korea’s youth are putting their lives on hold, spending years studying and cycling through multiple entry-level jobs just for a shot at working for tech giants like SK Hynix, South Korea's second-largest memory chipmaker.

  • What happened: SK Hynix recruitment events are drawing massive crowds of desperate job seekers, fueled by rumors of multi-million-dollar performance bonuses (worth roughly $350,000 to $450,000, or up to 600 million won).
  • What it changes: Securing an entry-level job at a top-tier chaebol (conglomerate) has become known as the "Hanic-gosi"—a pun comparing the company's name to the grueling state exams once required for elite government service. Even experienced workers are quitting their jobs to restart as corporate rookies.
  • Who is affected: Young job seekers, university students, and workers at small-to-medium enterprises face crushing inequality, with the wage gap between large corporations and smaller suppliers creating a desperate scramble for survival.

The Illusion of the Golden Ticket

The obsession with landing a spot at a tech titan like SK Hynix highlights a deeply fractured labor market where your first job dictates your entire standard of living. Data shows a massive gulf in South Korea: workers at smaller companies earn a fraction of what major conglomerate employees make, and the gap widens dramatically when massive performance bonuses are factored in. For young Koreans, missing out on a top-tier job isn't just about a lower paycheck—it means being locked out of the middle class entirely.

This winner-take-all structure has turned the education and hiring ecosystem into a high-stakes obstacle course. Affluent families pour millions of won into specialized cram schools and early tutoring for "semiconductor contract departments"—university programs designed to funnel graduates directly into tech giants. Meanwhile, ordinary applicants cycle through multiple unpaid internships, expensive clean-room training courses, and years of repeated job attempts, trapped in a relentless "N-su" (repeating exams) culture.

A Structural Crisis Disguised as Personal Failure

Society often blames young people for having unrealistic expectations or being too picky, but the data tells a different story. When the wage and benefit gap between primary conglomerates and subcontractors becomes an unbridgeable chasm, chasing a top-tier job is a rational survival strategy, not a luxury.

Until structural reforms tackle the stark inequalities between large enterprises and smaller suppliers, South Korea's job market will remain an exhausting exam room. For now, millions of young people continue to gamble their youth on a lottery-like corporate audition, trapped in a system where falling behind once means staying behind forever.
