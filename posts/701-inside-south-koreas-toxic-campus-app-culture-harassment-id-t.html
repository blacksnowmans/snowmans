Inside South Korea's Toxic Campus App Culture: Harassment, ID Trading, and Political Manipulation

October 04, 2026

South Korea’s dominant university student community app, Everytime, has devolved from a protected campus forum into a toxic breeding ground for sexual harassment, dating violence, and coordinated political astroturfing.

  • What happened:
    •  Investigative reports and public scrutiny have exposed widespread trading of verified student IDs on Everytime, allowing outsiders and political actors to infiltrate the platform under the guise of genuine student discourse.
  • What it changes:
    •  The revelation shatters the illusion that anonymous campus apps reflect the authentic voice of South Korea's younger generation, calling into question how mainstream media and politicians interpret online youth sentiment.
  • Who is affected:
    •  University students whose reputations and personal lives are targeted by harassment, as well as the broader public, who are routinely misled by manufactured political narratives disguised as organic youth opinion.

The Illusions of Strict Verification and Complete Anonymity

Everytime operates on the premise of a closed, highly secure ecosystem requiring university enrollment verification to participate. In practice, however, active user accounts are openly bought and sold, enabling anyone willing to pay to masquerade as a university student. This structural vulnerability strips away the platform's credibility, transforming a supposed sanctuary of higher education into a marketplace for malicious actors seeking to manipulate public opinion or push specific political agendas.

Behind the shield of anonymity, the platform frequently hosts degrading content, including the sharing of private personal details, sexual harassment, and the trivialization of dating violence. Rather than functioning as a peer support network, these spaces often reduce human suffering and private trauma to clickbait or targets for vicious cyberbullying. Mainstream media outlets frequently compound the issue by uncritically lifting inflammatory, anonymous posts to paint broad strokes of generational angst, effectively laundering orchestrated political interference as authentic youth sentiment.

Beyond Campuses: The Broader Crisis of Anonymous Public Forums

The decay seen on Everytime is not an isolated incident; it mirrors a wider systemic rot across South Korea's digital landscape, from the workplace-focused app Blind to various chat rooms and streaming platforms. These digital town squares have increasingly mutated into partisan battlegrounds where accountability is entirely absent. When anonymity becomes a license for unchecked aggression and manipulation, the public sphere ceases to function as a place for debate and instead becomes an echo chamber of manufactured outrage.

Ultimately, treating these digital spaces as harmless outlets for stressed youth ignores the real damage they inflict on public discourse. Until platforms abandon the fiction of self-regulation and face demands for real accountability, South Korea's online public square will remain vulnerable to manipulation, distortion, and deep-seated misogyny.
