South Korea to Cut Greenbelt for 77,000 New Homes Near Seoul

October 30, 2026

The Lee Jae-myung administration has announced a major housing expansion in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (SMA) by lifting Greenbelt environmental protections to build 76,700 new homes across six strategic locations.

  • The Plan: The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport will develop massive public housing districts in Gyeonggi Province, specifically in areas bordering Seoul such as Gimpo, Guri, and Hanam.
  • The Scale: Gimpo Ara will see the largest concentration with 37,000 units, while Hanam’s Misa and Gamil districts will receive 18,500 units.
  • The Timeline: Despite the "speed-war" rhetoric from officials, the first move-ins are not projected until at least 2033, nearly a decade away.
  • The Backlash: Local mayors and residents are protesting the lack of accompanying infrastructure, fearing the new developments will turn their cities into "transportation hell" and "bed towns."

Trading Environment for Supply

The decision to lift Greenbelt restrictions—environmentally protected zones where development is strictly prohibited to prevent urban sprawl—is a drastic measure last seen two years ago. By targeting land right on the edge of the capital, the government, led by the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's ruling liberal party), is attempting to signal a massive increase in supply to cool soaring apartment prices. However, the exclusion of Greenbelt areas within Seoul's actual city limits has raised questions about the policy's ultimate effectiveness in meeting demand where it is highest.

In my view, this policy is a double-edged sword. While the 76,700-unit figure looks impressive on paper, it relies on sacrificing "the lungs of the city" for future housing that won't be available for another ten years. This move also highlights a persistent structural flaw in South Korean urban planning: the "Seoul-centric" expansion. By pouring resources into the capital's outskirts, the government risks further neglecting the provinces, where many cities are already facing population decline and housing vacancies. Without a broader national balance, this expansion could simply pull more people into an already overcrowded capital region.

Infrastructure vs. "Bed Towns"

The most vocal opposition comes from local leaders like the Mayor of Gimpo, who argues that adding 37,000 homes without a guaranteed plan for subways and highways is "irresponsible." Gimpo is already home to the notorious "Gold Line," a light rail system so overcrowded that commuters frequently report breathing difficulties during rush hour. Critics argue that the government is repeating the failures of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s term, during which real estate policies were pushed through without sufficient local coordination, leading to compensation disputes and prolonged delays.

For these new districts to be more than just "concrete boxes" for sleeping, they must include self-sufficiency measures—meaning local jobs, schools, and cultural infrastructure. If the administration fails to uphold the "transportation first, housing later" principle, they aren't building communities; they are building a "gigantic prison" of commuters. For the frustrated 30-somethings currently struggling to pay rent, a 2033 move-in date feels like a "hope torture" that does nothing to alleviate the immediate crisis of asset inequality.
