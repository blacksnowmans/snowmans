Choo Kyung-ho's Insurrection Trial Tests Judicial Standards and Market Nerve

November 18, 2026

The legal fate of Choo Kyung-ho, a lawmaker and former floor leader of South Korea's conservative main opposition People Power Party, hinges on whether prosecutors can prove he intentionally obstructed lawmakers from voting to lift martial law, according to panelists on the political YouTube program "Kim Eo-jun's Humility Is Hard News Factory."

  • The 95-minute timeline: Investigative journalist Han Sang-jin of Newstapa detailed how, after Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol declared emergency martial law at 10:28 p.m. on the 3rd, Choo changed his party's caucus venue three times between the National Assembly and party headquarters—including a switch shortly after a roughly one-minute phone call with Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol at 11:22 p.m.
  • What it changes legally: Demonstrating physical confusion or reduced turnout is not enough for a conviction; the lower court must decide whether the evidence proves deliberate intent to block the parliamentary vote beyond a reasonable doubt.
  • Judiciary under scrutiny: Ahead of a verdict for former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, journalist Joo Jin-woo and attorney Park Pan-gyu questioned consistency across benches under Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, while Han Sang-jin stressed that martial law cases must rest strictly on law and evidence.
  • Market and policy fallout: Journalist Bong Ji-wook likened recent stock market losses to wiping out more than $1.4 trillion (2,000 trillion won)—roughly the combined value of all apartments in Seoul—while Rebuilding Korea Party lawmaker Kim Hyeong-yeon and Joo criticized mixed messaging from President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

Proving Intent in the Courtroom

The debate among the panelists highlights the narrow legal gap between political suspicion and criminal proof. Citing another reporter's assessment, Han Sang-jin suggested the repeated venue changes may have been aimed at drawing conservative lawmakers out of the National Assembly compound. Yet defense arguments attributing the shifts to chaotic access restrictions around parliament force the court to weigh competing readings of the exact same phone and text records.

That tension feeds directly into broader skepticism about South Korea's courts. Park Pan-gyu's blunt observation that a judge's disposition can outweigh even extensive special prosecutor evidence—alongside Joo Jin-woo's comparison with past election-law rulings involving President Lee Jae-myung and Presiding Judge Han Sung-jin—underscores why public trust in upcoming verdicts depends on transparent, consistent legal reasoning rather than partisan alignment.

Economic Signals Under Political Strain

These courtroom battles are unfolding alongside acute financial anxiety that tests the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and President Lee Jae-myung's administration. Panelists noted that uncoordinated remarks by cabinet officials and an apparent reluctance to label the stock downturn an emergency risk deepening investor uncertainty, even when the prime minister attempts to calm markets—though Joo added that swift, proactive policy announcements could still turn sentiment around, leaving institutional credibility in both the courts and economic ministries as South Korea's scarcest asset.
