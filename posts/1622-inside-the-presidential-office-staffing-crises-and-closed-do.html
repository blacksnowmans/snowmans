Inside the Presidential Office: Staffing Crises and Closed Doors in the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 28, 2026

South Korea's presidential office is facing a severe staffing crisis, with nearly 20% of its secretariat positions sitting vacant 17 months into President Lee Jae-myung's term—even as its personnel budget surges by over 20%.

    Staff shortages at the top: Out of 445 authorized secretariat positions, only 357 are filled, with working-level administrative officers seeing vacancies as high as 27.5%. The budget paradox: Despite a chronic lack of personnel, next year's presidential office personnel budget jumped 21.6% to about $40 million (54.5 billion won). Gatekeeping accusations: Critics and ruling party insiders allege that appointments are being tightly controlled by a faction of close aides centered around Chief Secretary for the First Presidential Secretariat Kim Hyun-ji, bypassing official HR channels. Political fallout: The leadership vacuum contributed to the resignation of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-shik following a string of troubled ministerial nominations, leaving the administration's approval ratings stuck in the low 30s.

A Hollowed-Out Office Run by an Inner Circle

The numbers coming out of the presidential office point to a dysfunctional administration rather than a simple labor shortage. While official desks gather dust, critics argue that the vacancies are not accidental. Instead, a tight-knit network tied to President Lee’s earlier days in Seongnam—often dubbed the "Gyeonggi-Seongnam line"—is allegedly locking out outsiders and independent-minded professionals. Even senior presidential secretaries have reportedly lost the authority to bring in their own staff, reducing official HR mechanisms to rubber stamps.

This closed-door management style has sidelined official channels and centralized de facto personnel power in figures like Kim Hyun-ji, a long-time associate of the president. When the gatekeepers prioritize personal loyalty and historical ties over professional merit, the administration ends up with bloated budgets to maintain its inner circle while essential working-level slots remain empty. The resulting infighting and public policy stumbles—particularly on sensitive issues like real estate—risk eroding the public trust that the Democratic Party of Korea needs to maintain momentum.
