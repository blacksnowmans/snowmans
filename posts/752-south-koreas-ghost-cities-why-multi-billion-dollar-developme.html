South Korea’s "Ghost Cities": Why Multi-Billion Dollar Developments Are Turning Into Ruins

September 14, 2026

  • The Crisis: Newly developed urban centers like Siheung’s Turtle Island and Incheon’s Midan City are suffering from vacancy rates as high as 87%, leaving once-hyped projects as derelict "ghost cities."
  • The Cause: Local governments and developers prioritized short-term profits through excessive, poorly planned commercial land sales, ignoring the lack of actual resident populations.
  • The Human Cost: Small investors and business owners who bought into the "new city" dream are now facing financial ruin, with many trapped in areas where basic amenities and transportation links remain incomplete.

South Korea’s obsession with "new city" development has hit a wall of reality. In areas like Turtle Island—a high-profile marine leisure complex launched in 2018—the contrast between glossy brochures and the current state of decay is jarring. As of late 2025, the vacancy rate for commercial storefronts hit 87%. Instead of bustling cafes, visitors find mold-covered floors, overgrown weeds, and shuttered buildings. The problem stems from a fundamental imbalance: local planners designated nearly 50% of the area for commercial use, far exceeding typical urban ratios, to maximize early-stage development fees. When the anticipated foot traffic failed to materialize, these areas were left with massive, unusable retail space.

The situation at Incheon’s Midan City is even more dire. A $1.1 billion (1.5 trillion KRW) casino resort project has been left as a concrete skeleton for years, stalled by a combination of geopolitical tensions, regulatory hurdles, and shifting economic tides. This reflects a recurring pattern in South Korean governance: the "development-first" mindset. Local authorities often use large-scale construction as a political tool to show progress, only to abandon the residents and investors once the initial land auctions are complete. Relying on "white elephant" projects like casinos or niche tourism hubs without establishing fundamental infrastructure—such as reliable highway access—has turned these areas into economic dead zones.

The Lesson for Urban Policy

The decay of these zones serves as a cautionary tale for the limits of top-down planning. Even long-established commercial districts, like the Lafesta mall in Ilsan—a first-generation new city—are struggling as demographics shift and consumption moves online. However, the difference is that Ilsan once thrived as a community. The current wave of "ghost cities" were fundamentally flawed from their inception, designed as speculative assets rather than functional living spaces. Until local governments stop prioritizing quick land-sale revenue over sustainable urban integration, these ruins will remain a painful monument to a failed development era.
