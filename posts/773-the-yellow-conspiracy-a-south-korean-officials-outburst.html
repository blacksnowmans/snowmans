The "Yellow" Conspiracy: A South Korean Official’s Outburst

September 16, 2026

Kim Jin-ae, the chair of the Presidential Committee on Architecture Policy—a government advisory body—is facing backlash after baselessly linking political supporters to a controversial religious group and dismissing election results as "abnormal."

  • What happened: Kim claimed that supporters of lawmaker Jung Chung-rae wearing yellow—a color historically associated with the late President Roh Moo-hyun and his support group, the Roh-sa-mo—were actually linked to the Shincheonji Church, a secretive religious sect often viewed with suspicion in South Korea.
  • Dismissing democracy: When Jung’s approval ratings reached 40%, Kim dismissed the figure as "abnormal" or "organized," suggesting only her expected 20% was legitimate.
  • Political fallout: Critics argue that Kim’s rhetoric weaponizes "color theory"—a common South Korean political tactic of labeling opponents as subversive—to delegitimize grassroots support.
  • The impact: The incident has reignited debates about the conduct of public officials and the rising trend of polarization within the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s main liberal opposition party.

The Perils of "Color-Coded" Politics

Kim’s comments represent a troubling trend where public officials use conspiracy-style logic to explain away political realities that don't fit their personal narratives. By labeling citizens wearing yellow as potential members of Shincheonji, Kim effectively transformed harmless political expression into a "potential criminal" investigation. There is zero evidence connecting these supporters to the religious group; the accusation is built entirely on the discomfort of a public official who cannot fathom why support for a specific politician has surged.

The most egregious part of this rhetoric is the "hit-and-run" nature of the accusations. Kim made a definitive claim, yet when the public backlash grew, she retreated into vague rumors, adopting the persona of a detached observer rather than a government official. In the arena of South Korean politics, this is a dangerous form of "shadow boxing"—setting up a fictional enemy to strike down, thereby avoiding the need to engage with actual voter sentiment or logical critique.

"Normalcy" as a Tool for Exclusion

Kim’s obsession with a "normal" 20% approval rating for Jung Chung-rae exposes a deep-seated arrogance that threatens democratic processes. In a healthy democracy, election results and polling are objective data points; they are not "abnormal" simply because they contradict an expert’s personal calculations. By framing 40% support as evidence of "organized, illicit manipulation," Kim essentially argues that any opinion outside her own is illegitimate.

As the head of an agency tasked with "spatial democracy"—the idea that urban planning should reflect and serve a democratic society—Kim’s behavior is deeply hypocritical. A leader tasked with creating public spaces that foster coexistence is currently fueling a climate of exclusion and suspicion. If she cannot accept that citizens hold diverse, unpredictable views, she is failing at the most basic duty of a public servant: acknowledging the legitimacy of the people she serves.

True political "authenticity," a concept Kim has often championed in her architectural work, requires the courage to admit when one's own assumptions are wrong. Instead, she has chosen the path of a conspiracy theorist, prioritizing the preservation of her worldview over the reality of the electorate. In an era where the public is increasingly tired of unsubstantiated accusations and emotional scapegoating, this type of governance is not just ineffective—it is an affront to the democratic values she is supposed to uphold.
