South Korea's New Investigative Agency Faces Backlash Over Pro-Yoon Appointments

September 30, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's upcoming Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA)—designed to strip direct investigative powers from prosecutors—is drawing fierce criticism for staffing key leadership roles with former prosecutors loyal to former President Yoon Suk Yeol.
  • Who is affected: The ruling liberal camp, progressive supporters, and the broader credibility of South Korea's long-sought prosecutorial reforms are taking a direct hit.
  • What it changes: Critics argue that filling a purportedly independent anti-corruption body with members of the old prosecution network turns a promised reform into a toothless continuation of the past.

South Korea’s political landscape is fracturing over the imminent launch of the Major Crime Investigation Agency, an institution originally billed as the crown jewel of prosecutorial reform. Instead of breaking free from the past, the agency's emerging lineup of officials—such as former senior prosecutor Kim Ji-yong and former public-security prosecutor Lim Hyun, both tied to the previous Yoon administration—has exposed a glaring contradiction. Rather than dismantling the entrenched power of the prosecution, critics contend the government is simply recycling old elites under a new banner.

The backlash reached a boiling point when Rep. Park Eun-jung, a prominent figure in the progressive reform movement, launched a blistering public critique. On a popular political broadcast, Park demanded that leadership drop the hypocrisy and simply declare that they intend to govern alongside pro-Yoon prosecutors. Her warning highlights a profound sense of betrayal among core supporters who spent years resisting what they viewed as prosecutorial overreach, only to watch those same figures absorb key positions in the new administration.

Reform in Name Only: The Politics of Pragmatism or Betrayal?

The controversy goes beyond standard personnel disputes, touching on a deeper ideological rift within South Korea's liberal and progressive coalitions. Analysts argue that President Lee Jae-myung’s camp is quietly sidelining traditional loyalists tied to former Presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in in favor of building a broader, more malleable political majority. By co-opting or accommodating figures from the old investigative apparatus, the administration appears to be prioritizing political survival and leverage over foundational democratic principles.

This strategy has left progressive commentators and online supporters wrestling with severe cognitive dissonance. When high-profile figures who once targeted liberals are handed leadership roles in a supposedly reformed agency, it forces a hard question: Is this pragmatic institution-building, or is it a calculated abandonment of the reform agenda? As the MCIA's October launch approaches, the growing disillusionment suggests that sweeping institutional changes mean little when the people steering the ship remain unchanged.
