Cracks in the Base: South Korea’s Progressive Coalition Turns on Itself

October 08, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s honeymoon phase is officially over, as falling approval ratings and a deepening revolt among his core progressive base expose severe cracks within the ruling Democratic Party.

  • What happened: President Lee’s approval rating has slipped into the mid-30% range, driven by sharp defections among key voters in their 40s and 50s and fierce internal backlash over tax and reform policies.
  • Who is affected: The progressive coalition, particularly core supporters who feel betrayed by compromises on key reforms like prosecutorial overhauls and real estate tax hikes.
  • What it changes: Payouts and policies aimed at courting moderate voters are backfiring, raising early fears of a lame-duck presidency as the administration alienates its staunchest defenders.

Recent polling reveals a striking shift in political loyalty. Veteran progressive commentator Rhyu Si-min notched a 29.8% trust rating in a Media Tomato survey—outranking high-profile politicians including Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok and former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Among core progressive voters, Rhyu's trust rating approaches a staggering 90%. Analysts point to this phenomenon as a symptom of a broader malaise: progressives are deeply anxious about the current direction of the Lee administration.

The friction largely stems from a classic political trap—trying to woo moderate voters by undercutting the base. Controversial tax policies, such as tightening rules on single homeowners, have directly hit the wallets of the 40-to-50-year-old demographic that served as the backbone of the progressive movement. Rhyu has famously diagnosed the administration's strategy as a botched remodeling job: promising to build an extension onto a sturdy house while accidentally tearing down its load-bearing pillars.

Ultimately, chasing the center at the expense of loyalists risks plunging the administration into a political death spiral. Governments in Seoul cannot govern effectively without their core defenders to push back against conservative opposition and media scrutiny. If President Lee and his staff refuse to heed these warning signs and clear out the internal "stones in their shoes," they may soon find themselves isolated, holding neither the base nor the middle.
