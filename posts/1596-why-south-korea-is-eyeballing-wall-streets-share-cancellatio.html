Why South Korea Is Eyeballing Wall Street's Share Cancellation Playbook

October 28, 2026

While Wall Street titans like Apple drive earnings-per-share growth by systematically buying and retiring their own stock, South Korean companies have historically hoarded treasury shares to entrench family control—a structural flaw now coming under intense reform pressure under President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

  • What happened: Top South Korean conglomerates, including semiconductor giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, are gradually shifting toward actual share retirements rather than simple buybacks following investor demands and regulatory pressure.
  • What it changes: Canceling shares permanently reduces stock supply, drives up earnings per share (EPS), and prevents founding families from using treasury stock to defend management rights or dilute minority investors.
  • Who is affected: South Korea’s 14 million retail equity investors gain stronger shareholder protections, while major business groups must adjust to international standards of corporate governance.

Share Retirement versus Treasury Hoarding

The long-term outperformance of major U.S. stock indices is built largely on earnings-per-share expansion driven by share retirement. Apple alone bought and destroyed over 800 trillion won (approximately $580 billion) worth of its own stock over the past decade, retiring more than 30% of its floating shares. This relentless reduction in total share count ensures that even modest earnings growth translates into significant gains per share, creating a self-sustaining floor for equity valuations.

In contrast, South Korean corporate groups (chaebols) have traditionally bought back stock only to stash it away on company balance sheets. Instead of boosting value for retail investors, these uncancelled shares remain an overhang on the market. Worse, chaebols often deploy treasury stock during corporate split-offs to boost controlling families' voting power without spending additional capital. While giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are beginning to adopt genuine share retirement, the broader Korean market has long suffered from this gap in governance quality.

Reforming Corporate Governance Under President Lee

Fixing this structural gap is now central to South Korea's financial agenda. President Lee Jae-myung's administration is pushing legal and regulatory reforms aimed at enforcing fair play in capital markets, expanding board duty to all shareholders, and curbing the misuse of treasury shares. This marks a clear pivot from the period during Yoon Suk-yeol's term, when market opacity and unfair corporate practices went largely unchecked, alienating retail investors.

For South Korea to resolve its chronic market undervaluation—the "Korea Discount"—regulators must ensure that treasury stock cannot be repurposed as a piggy bank for controlling shareholders. Enforcing global standards where buybacks lead directly to stock cancellation is the most critical step toward building long-term investor trust in the domestic stock market.
