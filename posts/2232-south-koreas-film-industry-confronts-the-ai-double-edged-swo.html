South Korea's Film Industry Confronts the AI Double-Edged Sword

November 11, 2026

South Korean filmmakers are moving to establish collective safeguards and an industry-led AI film festival, warning that rapid adoption of generative tools could soon leave independent creators beholden to Big Tech platforms.

  • What happened: Acclaimed director Lee Myung-se completed a 10-minute short film in just two weeks using generative AI—a project that normally takes three months—while warning that the technology cannot replace foundational screenwriting and directing.
  • What it changes: Rather than letting tech vendors dictate the narrative, Korean film professionals are launching an AI film festival next January to test the technology strictly as a cost-cutting assistive tool for cash-strapped productions.
  • Who is affected: Local directors, production crews, and public bodies like the Korea Artist Welfare Foundation, which is now wrestling with an influx of AI-assisted filmmakers seeking official artist certification and government subsidies.

Efficiency Gains and the Threat of Platform Lock-In

Lee's experiment illustrates the immediate upside of generative video: drastically reducing the barrier to high-budget visual effects and complex action sequences for low-budget cinema. Yet the industry's veteran directors caution against the illusion of effortless creation. Polished visual effects generated by software mean little if the narrative lacks emotional resonance, an element that remains strictly dependent on human scriptwriting and acting.

The more existential threat is economic. Industry insiders warn that tech giants are offering cheap computing and accessible software today solely to build dependence. Once production pipelines are hooked on proprietary generative systems, platforms can dramatically hike compute fees and licensing costs, skimming off profits while creative labor takes on the financial risk.

Heeding the Lessons of the Portal Era

This anxiety is shaped by South Korea’s recent media history. Two decades ago, domestic newspaper publishers handed over their reporting to giant internet portals for nominal gains, only to lose distribution control and find themselves structurally subservient to platform algorithms. Film professionals fear the same dynamic will play out if Big Tech captures the creative workflow from pre-production to screen.

To avoid that outcome, directors' and producers' guilds are urging collective bargaining to set standard contracts and fair usage frameworks before the market hardens. Meanwhile, state cultural institutions must clarify whether AI-driven output qualifies for public arts grants and how intellectual property rights will be protected. Without proactive guardrails from both guilds and regulators, the cost savings offered by AI may ultimately cost filmmakers their independence.
