South Korean Top Judges Under Fire Over Secret Cash Envelopes as Legal Aid Funds Dry Up

October 16, 2026

South Korea’s Supreme Court justices pocketed nearly $1 million in off-the-books cash allowances earmarked under the guise of staff welfare, even as the judiciary accumulated roughly $22 million in unpaid fees owed to public defense attorneys representing low-income defendants.

  • The revelation: During a parliamentary audit by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, Oh Min-seok, chief judge of the Seoul Central District Court, admitted that frontline research staff rarely received the monthly cash “encouragement funds” of 2 million to 3 million won (roughly $1,500 to $2,200) disbursed to individual Supreme Court justices, having received roughly 1 million won ($730) only twice over his years heading the research staff.
  • The paper trail: State audit data shows Supreme Court justices collected 1.3 billion won (about $950,000) in untraceable, receipt-free cash between 2022 and September 2025 under vague operational budget items, contradicting judicial leadership's claims that justices served merely as pass-through couriers for overworked staff.
  • The contrast: Overdue pay for state-appointed public defenders—who earn just $300 to $370 per case—reached 30 billion won ($22 million), while court administrative staff ran so short on operating funds that late-night meal stipends were suspended.
  • Transparency pushback: Lawmakers also grilled court leadership for appealing an administrative ruling that ordered the court to release unredacted copies of the first-instance verdict in the treason case against Yoon Suk-yeol, who was ousted and imprisoned following his martial law declaration.

A Rebranded Judicial Slush Fund

The scandal exposes how South Korea’s judicial elite quietly preserved opaque slush funds after promising reform. In 2018, following widespread public outrage over judicial influence-peddling under previous administrations, the courts pledged to abolish untraceable "special activity funds." Instead, leadership merely shifted the cash into general operational expenses, distributing envelopes of cash without requiring receipts or expense reporting. While Supreme Court justices drew annual salaries topping $150,000 alongside fixed monthly expense accounts, junior researchers drafting complex appellate opinions saw basic amenities—including late-night meal allowances and office coffee supplies—abruptly halted due to supposed budget deficits.

Representative Kim Yong-min of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea accused the National Court Administration—the judiciary’s powerful administrative body—of delivering false testimony to parliament. For years, court administrators insisted the cash went directly to working-level research judges supporting Supreme Court panels. Testimony from senior judges now confirms that the money functioned primarily as tax-free personal allowances for the justices themselves.

Defending Privilege Over Principle

The controversy underscores an entrenched institutional arrogance at the highest levels of the judiciary. While court leadership pleaded poverty to justify delaying statutory payments to young lawyers defending the indigent, it spared no expense in funding executive perks and litigating to conceal public information. The Seoul Central District Court's decision to appeal a court order demanding public disclosure of the names in Yoon Suk-yeol’s treason ruling illustrates an instinct toward institutional secrecy, shielding political and judicial figures behind overly broad interpretations of privacy.

External oversight of the judiciary remains notoriously weak in South Korea due to constitutional protections surrounding judicial independence. However, using public funds without receipts or legal statutory grounding is a direct violation of state accounting standards. With parliamentary scrutiny mounting and public trust damaged, pressure will grow on judicial leadership to submit its administrative budgets to the same rigorous oversight required of every other branch of government.
