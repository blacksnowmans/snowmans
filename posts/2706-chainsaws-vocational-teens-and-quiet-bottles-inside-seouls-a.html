Chainsaws, Vocational Teens, and Quiet Bottles: Inside Seoul’s Autumn Cultural Slate

November 24, 2026

Seoul’s autumn lineup of exhibitions and books shifts the spotlight toward grit, labor, and decades-long devotion, pushing back against a cultural scene often driven by fast-moving trends.

  • Kim Yun-shin’s solo show: At 91, the Korean sculptor nicknamed the “grandmother with a chainsaw” is presenting “New Continuity” free of charge at Lehmann Maupin, an international gallery in Seoul’s Hannam-dong district, marking a belated homecoming after gaining earlier recognition—and her own namesake museum—in Argentina.
  • Giorgio Morandi in Yeouido: ALT.1, an exhibition space inside The Hyundai Seoul department store in the capital’s financial district, is pairing the 20th-century Italian painter’s quiet still lifes with the actual everyday bottles and bowls he used.
  • A decade on the margins: Sociologist Yeon Hye-won’s book “Technical High School Boys” draws on 10 years of close interviews with 30 vocational high school students to examine how South Korea’s school system shapes teens into “male laborers.”
  • Anne Carson’s latest release: The acclaimed poet, essayist, and classicist—who began boxing at 76 and keeps writing despite Parkinson’s disease—is drawing attention with “The Beauty of the Husband,” a “fictional essay” exploring infidelity, deception, and enduring attachment.

Rediscovery and class scrutiny

The pairing of Kim Yun-shin’s retrospective and Yeon Hye-won’s sociology highlights two persistent blind spots in South Korea’s cultural conversation. Kim left for Argentina in her 40s, captivated by the wood she found there, and spent decades carving with a heavy chainsaw. Seoul’s art world is only now catching up to work that South America recognized years ago.

Yeon’s book targets a domestic divide that is easy to overlook. In South Korea, students at industrial and vocational high schools are often dismissed simply as future blue-collar workers. By following 30 of these boys over a decade, her reporting forces readers to confront the discrimination, inequality, and rigid social norms built into their coming-of-age.

Slowing down a trend-driven culture

Putting these four works side by side makes a quiet argument against treating art and books as disposable social-media fodder. Exhibitions in Seoul are frequently consumed as photo ops to prove one was there, and reading lists often chase whatever happens to be trending online.

Neither Morandi’s muted studies of dusty bottles nor Carson’s tangled dissection of a failing marriage offers quick spectacle. Together with Kim’s lifelong physical labor and Yeon’s ten-year field research, this season’s standout works reward readers and gallerygoers willing to slow down and sit with uncomfortable, unhurried truths.
