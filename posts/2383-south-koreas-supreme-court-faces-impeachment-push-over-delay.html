South Korea’s Supreme Court Faces Impeachment Push Over Delayed Insurrection Trial

November 16, 2026

According to a commentary on the Korean political blog Blacksnowman, South Korea’s Supreme Court has held former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo’s insurrection case for more than six months without a verdict, prompting calls from minor progressive lawmakers to impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae before Han’s detention expires.

  • A leaked press memo: Journalist Joo Jin-woo disclosed a Supreme Court public affairs office message asking reporters to refrain from definitive coverage claiming a ruling cannot be issued before November 20, 2026—a timeframe critics associate with Han’s potential release upon reaching his detention limit.
  • Ties to Yoon Suk-yeol’s case: Han is being tried as a key alleged accomplice in the insurrection case that resulted in Yoon Suk-yeol’s removal from office and imprisonment; critics cited in the commentary warn that releasing Han without a final verdict would undermine legal accountability.
  • Dissent inside the judiciary: The post cites a sitting presiding judge who publicly criticized court leadership, alleging that the judiciary has been reduced to a tool for defending Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae’s mistakes.
  • Political standoff at the National Assembly: Lawmakers from the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive opposition party, including Shin Jang-sik, have staged an outdoor tent sit-in demanding Jo’s impeachment ahead of a December deadline, while leadership in the ruling Democratic Party of Korea remains cautious about political backlash despite support for impeachment from party figures such as regional chair Park Kyu-hwan.

A Clash Between Judicial Caution and Political Urgency

The dispute underscores a sharp divide over whether the Supreme Court’s prolonged silence reflects standard legal deliberation or intentional stalling. While the Blacksnowman author and Rebuilding Korea Party protesters characterize the six-month delay as a calculated effort to protect establishment figures, the court’s memo to journalists indicates concern over media speculation before a ruling is finalized.

For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the approaching detention deadline presents a difficult strategic dilemma. Pursuing the impeachment of a sitting Chief Justice carries substantial institutional and political risks, yet continued caution threatens to frustrate grassroots supporters and political allies who argue that lawmakers will lose their leverage once Han’s detention period ends.

Beyond the immediate outcome of Han’s trial, public criticism emerging from within the judiciary points to deeper institutional strain. When sitting judges openly question the motives of court leadership, the Supreme Court faces a test of public credibility that extends well beyond a single verdict.
