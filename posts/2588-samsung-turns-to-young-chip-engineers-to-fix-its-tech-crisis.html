Samsung Turns to Young Chip Engineers to Fix Its Tech Crisis

November 21, 2026

Samsung Electronics is stripping day-to-day division duties from its two top executives and promoting three 1970s-born chipmakers to president in a bid to reclaim its lost technological edge.

  • An all-semiconductor promotion slate: In its November 20 executive reshuffle, Samsung promoted three executives to president—Hwang Sang-jun (head of the Memory Business), Park Jung-hee (semiconductor management support), and Kang Sang-hun (global manufacturing and infrastructure)—all from its Device Solutions chip division and all born in the 1970s.
  • Top bosses drop their dual roles: Semiconductor chief Jun Young-hyun and consumer-and-mobile chief Roh Tae-moon remain Samsung’s two co-CEOs, but both are stepping back from daily unit operations to focus on long-term strategy, including contract chipmaking and robotics.
  • New day-to-day commanders: Process design veteran Hwang takes charge of memory chips to restore Samsung’s standing in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), the specialized chips essential for AI servers, while mobile strategist Choi Won-jun takes over the Mobile eXperience (MX) division behind Galaxy smartphones.
  • Targeting key rivals: The overhaul aims to narrow the contract chipmaking (foundry) gap with Taiwan’s TSMC, win back AI memory leadership with next-generation HBM4, and defend Galaxy’s profitability against Apple and Chinese smartphone rivals.

A Bet on Engineering Over Bureaucracy

Chairman Lee Jae-yong’s diagnosis is clear: Samsung’s slump is managerial as much as cyclical. Critics have argued that a rigid, management-heavy bureaucracy dulled the engineering culture that once gave Samsung its "super-gap"—an overwhelming technological lead over competitors. Filling every new president slot from the semiconductor ranks sends an unmistakable message about where the company’s priorities and rewards now lie.

Separating long-term strategy from daily execution also makes sense on paper. Asking Jun Young-hyun to oversee daily memory operations while simultaneously chasing down TSMC in contract manufacturing stretched leadership too thin. Freeing Jun and Roh to focus five to ten years ahead could speed up major investments or acquisitions in foundry and robotics, though it also risks adding another layer to Samsung’s already hierarchical decision-making.

Still, putting younger, field-tested engineers in charge will not fix Samsung’s culture on its own. The real test is whether Hwang and Choi get genuine authority to break through stiff reporting lines and prioritize product breakthroughs over short-term optics. If they deliver on HBM4 and AI-driven Galaxy phones, this reshuffle will look like a turning point; if not, it will look like rearranging seats while rivals pull further ahead.
