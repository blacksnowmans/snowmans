South Korea’s Industrial Pivot: "Physical AI" Moves from Screens to the Streets

November 07, 2026

South Korea is rapidly shifting its artificial intelligence focus from generative chatbots to "Physical AI"—the integration of intelligence into robotic hardware designed to perform labor in factories, power plants, and farms.

  • The public and private sectors are launching massive infrastructure projects, including a 300 billion won (about $215 million) "Robot Farm Factory" in Saemangeum and a 150 billion won (about $107 million) national logistics testing center in Namwon.
  • Tech giants and specialized manufacturers, including Kakao Mobility (the transit arm of the tech giant Kakao) and motor supplier CNM, are pivoting to build the platforms and "muscles" for autonomous robotic systems.
  • The Lee Jae-myung administration is positioning Physical AI as a solution to South Korea's demographic crisis, aiming to replace shrinking labor pools in dangerous or labor-intensive industries.
  • Local "fabless" semiconductor firms, such as FuriosaAI, are developing specialized AI chips (NPUs) to power these robots, offering a more energy-efficient alternative to standard industry hardware.

A Strategy for National Survival

Physical AI represents a fundamental paradigm shift. While the early AI boom was defined by software that could write text or generate images, South Korea is now focusing on the "body"—the robotic joints, sensors, and processors that allow AI to interact with the physical world. This is not merely a technological trend; it is a survival strategy. Facing a severe labor shortage due to an aging population, the country is betting that autonomous systems can sustain its manufacturing and agricultural output.

The current push is notably decentralized, with major clusters forming in North Jeolla Province. By establishing "robot farms" and logistics hubs in rural areas, the government is attempting to tie high-tech innovation to regional economic revival. This proactive industrial policy stands in contrast to the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration was frequently criticized by the tech sector for cutting R&D budgets and maintaining rigid regulations that hindered field testing.

The Hardware Advantage

South Korea’s competitive edge in the global AI race may lie in its existing manufacturing DNA. Companies like CNM, which holds a 1.5 trillion won (about $1.07 billion) order backlog for automotive motors, are now repurposing their expertise for robotic actuators. Similarly, the collaboration between domestic chip designers and robotics firms suggests a move toward a "K-Physical AI" ecosystem that reduces reliance on global giants like Nvidia.

However, the transition is not without hurdles. For Physical AI to succeed, it must overcome "edge cases"—unpredictable real-world obstacles that digital AI never encounters. The Lee government’s move to propose a "Physical AI Special Act" is a critical step in providing the legal framework for robots to operate on public roads and in complex industrial sites. If the legislative support matches the current pace of investment, South Korea could transform from a software follower into a leader of the physical autonomous era.
