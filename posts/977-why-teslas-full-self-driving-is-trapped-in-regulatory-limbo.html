Why Tesla's Full Self-Driving is Trapped in Regulatory Limbo in South Korea

October 11, 2026

Tesla's advanced driver-assistance software is logging millions of kilometers on South Korean roads without official approval, exposing a major clash between rapid tech innovation and outdated bureaucratic rules.

  • What happened: A recent parliamentary audit revealed that while roughly 30,000 Teslas equipped with Full Self-Driving (FSD) have driven over 92 million kilometers in South Korea, the automaker has never formally applied for regulatory testing with the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport.
  • Who is affected: South Korean consumers who have paid thousands of dollars for software they cannot legally use, as well as drivers and pedestrians navigating roads where some users have illegally bypassed safety locks.
  • What it changes: Pressure is mounting on both Tesla Korea and transport authorities to move past a standoff of informal talks versus official filings, forcing regulators to finally draft proper safety guidelines for autonomous driving.

The Blame Game Between Regulators and Tesla Korea

During the recent parliamentary audit, executives from Tesla Korea and South Korea's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport pointed fingers at one another. Lawmakers criticized Tesla for failing to submit formal paperwork, while Tesla argued that the government failed to provide an administrative window for proper cooperation. Current international standards—such as UN R171 DCAS (Driver Control Assistance Systems)—fail to accommodate Tesla's FSD city-driving features because Tesla insists that human drivers retain ultimate legal responsibility, creating a mismatch with advanced automation frameworks.

Despite existing legal pathways like Article 114-2 of the Automobile Rules—which allows the transport minister to grant exceptions for superior new technologies—neither side has properly utilized them. Tesla has leaned on informal discussions rather than submitting hard safety data, while regulators have been slow to adapt. Meanwhile, the discovery of 93 vehicles using illegal aftermarket devices to force-enable FSD highlights just how far technology has outpaced local legislation.

Gamified Safety and the Road Ahead

Tesla's recent rollout of a "no-intervention counter" feature, which tracks and gamifies miles driven without driver intervention, adds a dangerous incentive for motorists to ignore potential hazards. Experts warn that treating safety milestones like a video game streak could delay crucial split-second human takeovers when the system fails.

Innovation cannot safely outpace the rules designed to protect the public. If South Korea is to embrace the future of mobility under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, both government regulators and automakers must stop hiding behind administrative loopholes. Regulators need to establish clear, data-driven safety standards, and Tesla must submit to transparent, formal verification rather than pushing unfinished software onto public streets.
