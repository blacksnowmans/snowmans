South Korea’s Leadership Crisis: Why Online Feuds are Fueling a Disastrous Approval Slide

September 03, 2026

South Korea’s current political landscape is defined by a leadership that relies on reactionary social media outbursts rather than coherent governance, driving approval ratings toward dangerous lows and alienating the crucial 2030 demographic.

  • Policy Inconsistency: High-profile flip-flops—such as the recent retreat on real estate tax reforms under pressure from the Democratic Party (the main liberal opposition)—have destroyed public faith in the administration’s competence.
  • The "Blame Game" Strategy: Instead of addressing policy failures, leadership is increasingly using social media to attack critics, creating a perception of fragility and defensiveness.
  • Demographic Abandonment: Disapproval among people in their 20s and 30s has surged above 70%, signaling a profound loss of hope in the country's future direction.
  • Governance Vacuum: Serious allegations, including a sexual harassment controversy involving lawmaker Jang Kyung-tae (a member of the Democratic Party), are being met with obfuscation rather than transparency, further eroding public trust.

The High Cost of Digital Petty Politics

The administration’s tendency to treat social media as a venting platform rather than a communication channel has backfired. When a leader consistently frames every policy setback as the fault of an "external enemy," they signal their own inability to command the state. This "dopamine-driven" politics might provide temporary satisfaction to a core base of supporters, but it is eroding the gravitas required to govern. As disapproval ratings climb above 60% nationally, the administration is finding itself increasingly isolated, struggling to maintain control as its traditional support pillars begin to crack.

The core issue is that voters are no longer swayed by political rhetoric; they are focused on the tangible impact of erratic policy shifts. When a government changes its tax or housing policies with little warning, it forces citizens to question whether there is anyone actually steering the ship. The current government’s tendency to bow to political pressure rather than adhering to stated principles makes it appear not just flexible, but strategically incompetent.

Leadership as a Reflection of Accountability

The handling of the scandal surrounding Jang Kyung-tae illustrates a broader culture of evasion in Korean politics. When officials prioritize silence or denial over clear, fact-based accountability, they essentially dare the public to lose faith in the system. The emergence of evidence—such as group chat transcripts—long after the fact suggests that the political class is far more adept at covering up misconduct than at addressing it.

Ultimately, the administration’s focus on "winning" petty online arguments instead of building consensus is a losing strategy. A leader who spends their time responding to every critic on the internet reveals that they are in survival mode, not governing mode. For the average citizen, this constant noise is exhausting. Unless there is a pivot toward consistency, silence, and genuine introspection, the current slide in public approval is unlikely to bottom out. The numbers in the latest polls are not just data; they are a formal declaration of lost confidence from the very generation that defines the nation’s future.
