How YouTube's Algorithm Turned South Korea's Pro-Lee Jae-myung Faction Against Itself

October 10, 2026

South Korea’s progressive political landscape is being hollowed out from the inside as high-profile YouTube commentators and hyper-partisan online communities turn their fire on each other in a desperate scramble for views and ad revenue.

  • The Factional Civil War: Pro-Lee Jae-myung online forums have banned attacks on ruling party figures like Representative Kim Min-seok, attempting to clamp down on self-destructive infighting among pro-government internet commentators.
  • The Clickbait Economy: Pundits and YouTubers are increasingly cannibalizing each other's audiences—squabbling over time slots and ad revenue rather than engaging in substantive political debate.
  • Algorithmic Echo Chambers: South Korea's massive reliance on video platforms—where YouTube dominates monthly screen time—traps supporters in radicalized filter bubbles that treat any internal dissent as treason.
  • The Broader Cost: Lawmakers have warned that this algorithm-driven political warfare threatens the foundation of deliberative democracy by drowning out moderate voices and policy discussion.

The Degeneration of "Cider Politics"

In South Korea, political commentary that offers instant, satisfying verbal attacks on opponents is colloquially known as "cider"—refreshing, sharp, and immediate. But this demand for constant outrage has created a trap. Pundits who fail to deliver aggressive attacks quickly lose their audience, while those who do are forced to continually escalate their rhetoric. Over time, the target has inevitably shifted inward. Commentators who once united behind the broader progressive cause have begun feuding over audience share, attacking fellow pro-government figures, and tearing apart their own base for clicks and superchats.

This dynamic has reduced a major political movement into a gated community of niche internet bubbles. Rather than expanding their reach to the broader public, these fringe commentators rely on 2000s-style internet forum tactics and aggressive name-calling, alienating mainstream voters and marginalizing themselves. By retreating into self-imposed echo chambers and treating every minor disagreement as an existential betrayal, these online factions are trading long-term political impact for short-term digital validation.
