South Korea’s Power Vacuum: The Collapse of the Presidential Office

October 03, 2026

Less than 18 months into his term, President Lee Jae-myung is facing a humiliating political crisis: his top aides are quitting, and nobody wants to take their place. The vacancy of the Chief of Staff—the most powerful gatekeeper role in the Blue House—has become a clear signal that the administration is losing its grip on power.

  • The Vacuum: Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik resigned on the day the President departed for a high-profile overseas trip, a breach of protocol that signifies total internal collapse.
  • The "Poisoned Chalice": Potential successors are publicly and aggressively rejecting the role, fearing that association with this sinking administration will destroy their own political careers.
  • Structural Failure: The administration’s reliance on informal "shadow" power structures (the so-called "Seongnam Line") has rendered the official cabinet and staff positions powerless and prone to scandal.
  • The Consequence: With the administration trapped in an "echo chamber" of loyalists, policy-making has stalled, and the government is spiraling into a classic "lame duck" phase.

A Government Running on Empty

The refusal of prominent politicians—such as lawmaker Han Jun-ho—to accept the Chief of Staff position is not just a scheduling conflict; it is a calculated survival strategy. In South Korean politics, the Chief of Staff is the "captain" of the presidential ship. When prospective candidates scramble to deny their appointment, they are effectively telling the public that they believe the ship is already underwater.

The administration’s problems are compounded by its rigid, exclusionary leadership style. By prioritizing blind loyalty over competence and surrounding the President with "yes-men" who fear providing honest counsel, the office has created a toxic environment. This has alienated even the President’s own party members, who are now distancing themselves to focus on their own survival in upcoming electoral cycles.

The "Shadow" Power Problem

The crisis is rooted in a fundamental disconnect between formal authority and actual influence. While the Chief of Staff is theoretically the highest-ranking advisor, the real power—the "Seongnam Line" of presidential associates—operates behind the scenes, bypassing official channels. This creates a "hollowed-out" administration where ministers and staff take the blame for failures while having zero control over the decision-making process.

President Lee’s decision to force through controversial appointments, such as Kim Ji-yong as head of the National Investigation Agency, reflects a refusal to adapt. Rather than seeking national consensus or reforming his inner circle, the President appears driven by a personal grudge against his political rivals. This stubbornness has turned the Blue House into an isolated fortress, leaving the administration unable to recruit the talent needed to steer the country through its current economic and social challenges.
