Kospi Plunges to 6,800 as Foreign Investors Dump Billions

October 08, 2026

South Korea's stock market suffered a brutal correction on October 7, with the benchmark Kospi tumbling from its recent highs above 7,000 down to the 6,800 range as foreign investors pulled out up to 3.1 trillion won (about $2.2 billion) in a single day.

  • What happened: Heavy foreign selling dragged down large-cap anchors like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, while the Japanese yen hit historic lows against the won, squeezing Korean automakers' export competitiveness.
  • What it changes: The rout exposes vulnerabilities in leveraged small-to-mid-sized businesses and highlights growing pressure on household debt amid high interest rates.
  • Who is affected: Retail investors caught at market peaks, heavily indebted households, and export-driven manufacturers facing currency headwinds.

Foreign Capital Flight and Currency Squeeze

The core driver of the sell-off is a relentless exodus of foreign capital, with cumulative net selling by foreigners topping 220 trillion won (about $160 billion) this year alone. As global interest rate pressures persist, overseas investors are pulling hard cash out of Korean equities. Compounding the pain, the Japanese yen has weakened dramatically to under 845 won per 100 yen—levels not seen since before the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis—giving Japanese rivals a severe price advantage that battered Korean auto stocks.

Amid the carnage, structural bright spots remain. While households struggle with debt accumulated during past real estate buying binges, South Korea's major corporations are sitting on robust cash reserves. Samsung Electronics also cleared a major hurdle by passing Nvidia's quality tests for its HBM4E memory chips, signaling strong future demand in the AI hardware space. However, high valuations across popular sectors like defense and shipbuilding mean investors must remain disciplined.

AI Infrastructure Bottlenecks and Market Realities

The broader tech boom is also hitting physical walls. Massive AI data centers face severe power grid delays—exemplified by Oracle's recent project stalls—raising fresh fears of an AI bubble fueled by high-interest debt, as warned by investor Ray Dalio. For Korean markets, the lesson is clear: when sentiment turns, weaker small-cap companies and over-leveraged firms break first, while those with solid fundamentals survive.
