Decoding Boys: Expert Tips on Parenting, Focus, and Managing Parental Burnout

October 11, 2026

Raising a son often feels like navigating unchartered territory, but two South Korean parenting experts argue that the key to avoiding burnout lies in separating a child's emotions from their actions while modeling emotional regulation yourself.

  • What happened: Parenting experts Choi Min-jun and Won Min-woo appeared on a local talk show to discuss the realities of raising boys, handling parental powerlessness, and fostering deep focus in children.
  • The core principle: Parents are urged to adopt a simple rule—validate a child's underlying feelings completely, but set firm boundaries against destructive behaviors.
  • The takeaway: High-achieving children share a common trait of deep immersion in a single interest; parents should protect this focus rather than forcing rote study.
  • The context: The discussion touches on broader national conversations about child psychology and emotional regulation, even as political shifts under President Lee Jae-myung bring new attention to family support policies.

The Anatomy of Parental Burnout

Every parent knows the sinking feeling of helplessness when a child throws a tantrum in public or a safety rule is broken. Choi Min-jun admitted to losing his temper while driving when his son dangerously opened a window—a moment of lost control that many parents face. Experts note that this frustration rarely stems from the child's disobedience alone, but from the terrifying realization that parents cannot completely control every situation.

The solution starts with self-regulation. When parents step back, calm down, and later apologize for losing their tempers ("It was wrong of me to attack who you are"), they teach children a vital lesson: adults make mistakes, but relationships can be repaired. Parenting is not about being a flawless machine, but about growing alongside your child.

Igniting Intelligence Through Deep Focus

Children who excel academically often share a history of deep, uninterrupted immersion in a specific hobby, no matter how niche. Whether a child is obsessed with bus schedule numbers or toy cars, parents should expand on these interests rather than shutting them down to study. Denying children "appropriate deprivation"—giving them everything instantly—robs them of the chance to build internal motivation.

Ultimately, emotional regulation is caught, not taught. When parents step away to take a deep breath before reacting to stress, they provide the ultimate model for their children. As public policies increasingly emphasize mental health and emotional education, the most powerful change still happens at home, one warm conversation at a time.
