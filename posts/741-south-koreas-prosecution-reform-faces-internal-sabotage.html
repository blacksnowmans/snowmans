South Korea’s Prosecution Reform Faces Internal Sabotage

September 14, 2026

  • The controversy: The nomination of a former prosecutor to head the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA) has sparked intense backlash, with critics labeling it a betrayal of the goal to separate investigative and prosecutorial powers.
  • The "pro-Yoon" influence: Lawmakers allege that the presidential office's personnel system is dominated by loyalists to President Yoon Suk Yeol, effectively allowing the prosecution to maintain control over the very agencies meant to limit its power.
  • Public distrust: Recent polling shows that over 53% of South Koreans view the appointment of former prosecutors to lead investigative bodies as inappropriate, reflecting deep-seated skepticism toward the prosecution's self-reform efforts.
  • Strategic regression: Critics argue that legislative loopholes—specifically clauses allowing personnel to opt out of transitions—are being weaponized to preserve the prosecution’s institutional influence.

A Reform in Name Only

The proposed leadership for the SCIA—a body originally conceived to strip the prosecution of its investigative overreach—has become a flashpoint for critics who see it as a "reward" for those who previously resisted reform. Lawmakers like Park Eun-jung of the Rebuilding Korea Party have pointed to the nominee’s history, arguing that figures involved in past controversial cases—such as the suppression of investigations into media-prosecution collusion—are now being positioned to lead the nation's new investigative landscape. This effectively places the "fox in charge of the henhouse," reversing the intent of the 4-year-old push for reform. The situation is exacerbated by the composition of the nomination committee, which currently lacks police representation and is heavily skewed toward former prosecutors. This has created an environment where a "pro-Yoon" clique effectively selects its own successors, ensuring that the investigative power remains within a familiar, friendly ecosystem.

The Illusion of Personnel Changes

The core of the issue is not just individual appointments, but a failure of systemic clearing. Even when new laws are passed to redistribute power, they are often rendered toothless by administrative "poison pill" clauses—such as requirements that personnel must consent to transfers, which the prosecution is now using to block meaningful change. My reading of the situation is that this is not a series of administrative errors, but a calculated strategy of resistance. By filling the presidential office’s civil affairs line and key supervisory roles with former prosecutors, the current administration has created a buffer that shields the institution from genuine reform. As long as the personnel remains unchanged, no amount of institutional restructuring will yield results. True reform requires more than just new office plaques; it requires the removal of the very power structures that have spent years fighting the concept of accountability.
