South Korea’s Conservative Opposition Takes to the Streets Over Special Counsel Bill

November 15, 2026

South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), has launched an aggressive nationwide street campaign to demand a presidential veto against a contentious special prosecutor bill pushed through by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

  • Mass rally in Busan: On November 14, PPP leaders and rank-and-file members rallied in the rain at Busan Station Plaza, denouncing the legislation as an unconstitutional "jailbreak bill" designed to shield President Lee Jae-myung from pending legal trials.
  • Escalating rhetoric: PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok called for President Lee to step down, demanded the immediate resumption of the president's stalled criminal trials, and warned that cabinet members could face prison time if the measure is executed.
  • Budget session neglected: The ruling liberal Democratic Party blasted the rallies, accusing the opposition of sabotaging vital parliamentary reviews of the upcoming national budget to stage street protests and mock the president.
  • A nationwide roadmap: The opposition plans to sustain pressure by taking its protests to traditional conservative strongholds, scheduling rallies in Daegu on November 21 and Daejeon on November 28.

Abandoning the Assembly During Crucial Budget Talks

The opposition's decision to leave parliament and occupy the streets comes at a critical juncture, as the National Assembly sits in review of the upcoming national budget. Passing the government's spending plan is the legislature’s most pressing constitutional duty. By trading parliamentary committee chambers for public squares, the PPP has effectively frozen bipartisan dialogue at a time when economic headwinds demand legislative focus.

The dispute centers on how each side views the special counsel legislation. While the ruling Democratic Party maintains that the bill is a legitimate correction of prosecutorial overreach and politically motivated indictments, the PPP argues it is a partisan maneuver to halt five ongoing trials involving President Lee. With both camps framing the fight as a defense of the constitutional order, routine governance has taken a back seat to reciprocal hostility.

Rallying the Hardline Base Amid Internal Cracks

Taking politics to the pavement is a calculated risk by PPP leadership to consolidate the conservative base. By taking the fight to southeastern battlegrounds such as Busan and Daegu, the party aims to build outside pressure against the administration and give cover for a potential presidential veto. Yet beneath the public display of strength, fissures inside the opposition are already emerging.

Despite mandatory mobilization directives, several PPP lawmakers skipped the Busan rally, exposing quiet apprehension within party ranks about an open-ended, extra-parliamentary campaign during budget season. While street rallies energize hardline partisans, they risk alienating centrist voters who favor institutional compromise. For now, institutional politics in Seoul has ground to a halt, replaced once again by the volatile theater of street protests.
