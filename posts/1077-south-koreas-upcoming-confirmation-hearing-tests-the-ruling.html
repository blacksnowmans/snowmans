South Korea's Upcoming Confirmation Hearing Tests the Ruling Party Amid 97% Public Disapproval

October 13, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea faces a high-stakes confirmation hearing for nominee Kim Ji-yong under President Lee Jae-myung's administration, overshadowed by a poll showing 97% of respondents view the appointment as inappropriate.
  • What it changes: The hearing puts heavy pressure on the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, which faces criticism for rubber-stamping appointments and internal disarray following recent parliamentary audits involving Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae.
  • Who is affected: President Lee's administration, the ruling Democratic Party, and voters demanding greater accountability in government personnel appointments.

A Rubber-Stamp Hearing or a Real Test?

Confirmation hearings are meant to serve as a public window into whether a nominee is fit for high office. But the run-up to Kim Ji-yong's hearing looks less like a rigorous vetting process and more like a foregone conclusion. Critics argue that the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party, is treating the procedure as a mere formality rather than a substantive examination of the nominee's qualifications.

Compounding the frustration is an astonishing 97% negative rating in recent polling on Kim's appointment. For an administration that came to power promising reform, ignoring such overwhelming public skepticism risks alienating voters. Compounding the political turbulence, recent parliamentary audits of Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae exposed bizarre infighting within the Democratic Party itself, as lawmakers from the ruling party launched aggressive attacks against their own governor, only for Choo to deftly fend them off.

The Ruling Party on Trial

The upcoming hearing is shaping up to be a referendum not just on Kim Ji-yong, but on the competence and discipline of the Democratic Party lawmakers tasked with questioning him. Instead of acting as rigorous arbiters of public standards, ruling party members face accusations of selective silence and putting political calculations ahead of public welfare.

Public memory is long, and voters are watching closely to see if the ruling party will break free from old, insular political habits. If the government pushes through a deeply unpopular appointment in the face of near-unanimous public opposition, the political fallout could deal a severe blow to the Lee Jae-myung administration's credibility just as it attempts to solidify its reform agenda.
