Why South Korea's Prosecution Reform Crisis is Splitting the Progressive Base

September 24, 2026

South Korea's progressive ruling camp is facing an acute internal crisis after veteran political commentator Rhyu Si-min accused President Lee Jae-myung of misleading the public over the true scope of promised prosecution reforms.

  • What happened: President Lee stated in a recent press conference that the absolute limit of prosecution reform is simply dividing the existing Justice Ministry into separate investigative and indicting units—falling far short of the complete separation demanded by the progressive base.
  • The fallout: Rhyu slammed the administration's stance as a "deception," arguing that a series of government-drafted bills over the past year have merely rebranded old prosecutorial powers under new agency names rather than dismantling them.
  • Civil society pushback: Major civic groups, including People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy (PSPD) and Minbyun (Lawyers for a Democratic Society), have echoed these criticisms, calling the government's plans a superficial "signboard change."
  • Who is affected: The widening rift exposes deep tensions between the presidential office, the ruling Democratic Party, and loyal supporters who feel foundational reform promises are being quietly abandoned for bureaucratic convenience.

A Chasm Between Rhetoric and Reality

The core grievance driving this firestorm is a perceived betrayal of trust. For years, the progressive bloc mobilized millions under the banner of stripping the powerful prosecution service of its unchecked authority and placing law enforcement under civilian democratic control. Yet, as the timeline of legislation unfolded—from government reorganization acts to prime ministerial task forces—critics watched policy drift away from public pledges. Rhyu's public critique broke an unwritten rule of intra-party solidarity, prompting predictable accusations from party loyalists that he is stoking needless division.

Rather than engaging with the timeline of policy retreats or the specific structural flaws in the newly proposed investigative agencies, defenders of the administration have largely resorted to attacking the messengers. This reflex to frame substantive policy critiques as acts of disloyalty highlights a dangerous trap within Korean factional politics: prioritizing the preservation of power over the actual values that brought the movement to office in the first place.

The Danger of Sweeping Wounds Under the Rug

Political survival often tempts leaders to paper over internal dissent in the name of unity, dismissing warnings as collateral damage. But when critical allies like civic watchdogs and veteran commentators begin to walk away, it signals that the disconnect between the leadership and its base has moved past simple policy disagreements. If the administration continues to suppress debate and substitute genuine institutional overhaul with bureaucratic rebranding, it risks alienating the very voters who built its mandate—turning political apathy into a permanent breach.
