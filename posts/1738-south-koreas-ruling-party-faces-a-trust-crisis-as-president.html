South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces a Trust Crisis as President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Stalls

October 30, 2026

Public support for South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is slipping as eroded personal trust, policy friction, and unrefined presidential messaging undermine the ruling Democratic Party of Korea’s political momentum.

  • Dropping Approval: Polling indicates positive ratings for President Lee have fallen from early highs near 70% to 47.8%, with negative sentiment closely tailing at 46.8% amid youth alienation and real-economy anxieties.
  • Policy Disconnects: Local friction over major regional infrastructure commitments, such as the stalled Gwangju military airport relocation, has fueled public skepticism regarding the administration's execution.
  • Social and Judicial Frustration: Controversial court acquittals in high-profile corruption cases, paired with unscripted presidential social media posts, have heightened concerns over governance style and rule of law.
  • Push for Electoral Reform: Minor progressive coalition partners are urging the ruling party to adopt runoff voting and lower the parliamentary threshold for official negotiating groups from 20 seats to 10 seats.

Why Trust Matters More Than Tactics

The core problem confronting President Lee Jae-myung is not merely policy mechanics, but a breakdown in foundational public trust. Unlike former President Moon Jae-in, who maintained a remarkably resilient 45% approval rating at the end of his term because voters believed in his moral intentions despite policy missteps, President Lee’s administration is struggling to project that same integrity. When basic trust vanishes, government concessions and policy incentives are no longer viewed as goodwill—voters view them as calculated political distractions.

This credibility deficit is exacerbated by erratic presidential communication and lingering legal friction. Unfiltered social media posts risk reducing national governance to daily political bickering, alienating traditional supporters while failing to capture swing voters. Meanwhile, operational misalignments—such as mixed signals sent to local communities regarding regional development and airport relocation projects—reinforce the perception that executive promises lack deliberate follow-through.

Breaking the Two-Party Cartel

Beyond executive messaging, current tensions highlight deep structural flaws in South Korea’s political system. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) faces growing strain with smaller progressive allies, including the Rebuilding Korea Party and the Social Democratic Party. While the DPK has floated unity initiatives, smaller parties accuse the ruling majority of seeking tactical absorption rather than genuine coalition-building.

To end the destructive cycle of hyper-polarization between the DPK and the main opposition conservative People Power Party, progressive reformers are demanding institutional changes. Lowering the requirement to form a recognized parliamentary negotiating group from 20 seats down to 10 seats would grant smaller parties legislative leverage and allow moderate factions space to act independently. Combined with a presidential runoff voting system, such reforms could curb forced tactical voting and compel future administrations to govern through true broad-based consensus.
