Backlash Grows on South Korea’s Left as Activists Accuse President Lee’s Faction of Betrayal

November 07, 2026

Grassroots progressive activists and traditional party loyalists are mounting an open revolt against President Lee Jae-myung and his inner circle, alleging that the ruling Democratic Party of Korea has abandoned its founding welfare principles in favor of corporate tax breaks and factional self-preservation.

  • Fiscal shift: The Lee administration’s proposed budget expands total government expenditure by 12.8% to 820.9 trillion won (about $615 billion), yet health and welfare spending rises by only 8.2% to roughly 148.7 trillion won (about $111 billion), drawing fierce criticism from welfare advocates who argue low-income safety nets are being sidelined.
  • Tax policy reversal: Disaffected party members accuse President Lee of mimicking conservative trickle-down economics by championing corporate tax breaks for large conglomerates in strategic sectors like semiconductors and artificial intelligence, rather than supporting small enterprises and working-class families.
  • Internal power struggle: Outraged progressive commentators have branded the ascendant "New Lee Jae-myung" faction a "Blue People Power Party," accusing loyalist lawmakers of treating the party as a vehicle for personal power while ditching the political heritage of former presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in.
  • Threat of a 2028 split: Accusing the leadership of misusing internal member databases to favor insiders, disaffected party members are threatening a rectification campaign and warning they will field rival candidates or launch an alternative party ahead of the 2028 legislative elections.

Corporate Tilts and Fractured Loyalties

The rift exposes a widening ideological fissure inside South Korea’s ruling camp. President Lee took office backed by a broad coalition expecting sweeping progressive reforms, but his governing agenda has increasingly prioritized industrial competitiveness and corporate incentives over redistributive spending. For core activists who took to the streets during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s term before his impeachment, seeing the new Democratic Party administration champion big-business tax relief while holding back social safety-net growth feels like outright political betrayal.

Compounding this frustration is the administration’s rhetoric on issues such as foreign laborers and multicultural policies, which critics argue borrows talking points previously confined to the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition. Disillusioned party members argue that despite holding commanding poll numbers, the administration is taking its traditional base for granted while playing to conservative swing voters.

A Brewing Civil War Ahead of 2028

Inside the Democratic Party, the dispute is rapidly becoming an institutional battle over ballot access and party governance. Critics argue that Lee's loyalists are engineering an intentional shift in the party's voter base, sidelining committed party regulars while using procedural maneuvers and toothless parliamentary inquiries to deflect hard political decisions.

Unless the leadership addresses allegations of factional favoritism and database leaks, South Korea's ruling party faces an agonizing intra-camp war. Grassroots groups are already openly discussing local candidate challenges for the 2028 general elections, suggesting that the most volatile threat to the Lee administration’s mandate may ultimately come from its own disillusioned base rather than the conservative opposition.
