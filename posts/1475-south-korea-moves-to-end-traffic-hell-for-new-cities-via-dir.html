South Korea Moves to End "Traffic Hell" for New Cities via Direct Licensing

October 26, 2026

The South Korean government is taking direct control of road construction permits for the "3rd Generation New Towns" to ensure residents have functional transportation infrastructure the moment they move into their new homes.

  • Bypassing Bureaucracy: The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) and its Metropolitan Transport Commission (MTC) will now use "direct licensing" to override delays caused by negotiations between local municipalities.
  • Expanded Scope: The government has increased the number of prioritized road projects under this fast-track scheme from five to seven, adding two critical routes serving the Incheon Gyeyang district.
  • Shortened Timelines: By centralizing the approval process, the government expects to cut administrative delays by one to two years, addressing the chronic "move in first, build roads later" problem.
  • Infrastructure First: This policy shift aims to fulfill the "transportation before move-in" principle, a direct response to the failures of previous housing developments where residents were stranded without adequate transit for years.

Bypassing the Local Government Bottleneck

For decades, South Korea’s massive satellite city projects have followed a grim pattern: luxury apartment complexes rise in the middle of empty fields, while the roads and subways promised to residents remain stuck in administrative limbo. These delays often stem from local governments bickering over route maps, cost-sharing, and intersection designs. In the past, a single disagreement between neighboring cities could freeze a road project for years, leaving commuters trapped in overcrowded buses and unpaved shortcuts.

The introduction of direct licensing by the Metropolitan Transport Commission (MTC)—a central agency under the land ministry—is a decisive move to break this cycle. By allowing the central government to approve and manage these cross-jurisdictional roads, the Lee Jae-myung administration is effectively stripping local bureaucrats of their "veto power" over national infrastructure. This is particularly vital for the Incheon Gyeyang district, a key housing hub in the western capital region that faces severe congestion if its connecting roads to Seoul are not completed by the time the first residents arrive.

Learning from the Failures of the Past

This aggressive administrative push is a corrective measure against the "market-first" approach seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure. Under the previous administration, critics argue that housing policy focused too heavily on supply numbers and deregulation while neglecting the state's responsibility to build the underlying infrastructure. Residents in "2nd Generation New Towns" like Dongtan and Unjeong are still dealing with the fallout, having paid billions of won in transportation levies only to wait a decade for promised train lines and highways.

The current government’s pivot suggests a realization that a house is not just a "concrete box," but part of a living ecosystem that requires mobility. While direct licensing may ruffle the feathers of local politicians who lose their leverage, it prioritizes the rights of the tens of thousands of young professionals and families who have leveraged their futures to buy homes in these new districts. For these residents, a one-to-two-year reduction in construction time isn't just an administrative metric—it is the difference between a functional life and a daily three-hour battle with traffic.
