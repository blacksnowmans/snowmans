South Korea’s Year-End Shopping Season Heats Up as Live Commerce Goes Global

November 17, 2026

South Korea's year-end retail season has officially shifted into high gear as major food brands and e-commerce giants launch high-stakes "limited edition" and Black Friday campaigns driven by live streaming.

  • Haitai Confectionery, a leading South Korean food company, increased production by 67% for its snack-based Advent Calendar after the product sold out in just 10 minutes last year.
  • Coupang, South Korea’s dominant e-commerce platform, launched a two-week "Digital Black Friday" event featuring exclusive live-stream discounts and prize draws.
  • Traditional markets in central Seoul and rural farming districts like Bonghwa are bypassing physical foot traffic by selling directly to national audiences via Naver Shopping Live, the streaming arm of the country’s top search portal.
  • K-beauty exports are seeing explosive growth on TikTok Shop, with some Korean cosmetic brands reporting a fivefold increase in global sales through short-form video commerce.

The Power of Scarcity and Storytelling

The headline-grabbing success of Haitai’s Advent Calendar—a 5,000-unit limited run—highlights a shift in how Korean brands approach the holidays. Rather than simple price cuts, companies are leaning into "experience-based" products. The calendar isn't just a box of snacks; the packaging is designed to be folded into a "cookie house" that functions as a mood lamp. This visual appeal is specifically engineered for the "three-second rule" of mobile commerce, where a product must grab a scrolling user's attention instantly.

This strategy is proving essential as the market moves away from static web pages toward live video. By creating "must-have" items that look good on camera, brands are driving urgency. The 10-minute sell-out of previous years suggests that for Korean consumers, the "thrill of the find" in a live broadcast is becoming as important as the product itself.

Digital Lifelines for Traditional Sellers

While giants like Coupang—often called the Amazon of Korea—are using Black Friday to move high-end electronics, a more quiet revolution is happening in the country’s traditional markets. In Jung-gu, the historic heart of Seoul, vendors from the famous Namdaemun Market are now appearing on Naver Shopping Live to sell everything from handmade accessories to seaweed. For these aging markets, live commerce is no longer a luxury but a survival tool to reach younger, mobile-savvy shoppers who rarely visit physical stalls.

This digital transition is extending to rural areas as well. Bonghwa-gun, a mountainous county known for its agriculture, has moved beyond one-off broadcasts to "encore" sales events. By using the data gathered during initial live streams to target interested buyers later, these local governments are building a sophisticated e-commerce infrastructure that reduces the influence of traditional middlemen and puts more profit directly into the hands of farmers.

Global Expansion and AI Integration

The reach of Korean live commerce is now extending far beyond the peninsula. On TikTok Shop, the combination of 15-second tutorials and real-time streaming has allowed K-beauty brands to bypass traditional export hurdles. One medical beauty brand, The Mellow, reportedly generated nearly 1 billion won (about $720,000) during a single Chinese shopping festival broadcast, proving that visual demonstration is a universal language that scales globally.

Looking ahead, the industry is betting on artificial intelligence to maintain this momentum. Hyundai Department Store has recently integrated ChatGPT-based shopping assistants to handle customer inquiries, while other marketing firms are signing deals to automate the "matchmaking" process between influencers and products. As the line between entertainment and shopping continues to blur, the winners in the Korean market will be those who can combine high-tech AI logistics with the high-touch human connection of a live broadcast.
