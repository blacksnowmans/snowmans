Why South Korea’s Youth Are Choosing "Dating Strikes"

October 06, 2026

A growing number of South Korean young people are opting out of dating and marriage, viewing these traditional milestones as high-risk, low-reward social burdens rather than essential parts of life. This phenomenon, increasingly dubbed a "dating strike," reflects a profound disillusionment with a society that demands infinite competition but offers little in the way of stable futures.

  • Structural Despair: High housing costs and extreme job market polarization have made the traditional path to family life financially unattainable for many.
  • The Death of Deep Thought: Over-reliance on algorithm-driven short-form content and digital convenience is eroding the capacity for critical thinking and genuine human connection.
  • Generational Conflict: The older generation’s tendency to label these choices as "selfish" or "unproductive" only deepens the divide and ignores the systemic reality young people face.

The Erosion of Thinking in the AI Era

Physics professor Kim Sang-wook recently warned that the current obsession with convenience and short-form content is fundamentally rewiring how society functions. As we outsource our decision-making to algorithms, we lose the ability to engage in complex reasoning—a cornerstone of democracy. When text consumption drops to record lows, as reported by government statistics, the ability to read between the lines and engage in rational debate disappears, leaving a population vulnerable to manipulation.

This is further exacerbated by the "palli-palli" (hurry-hurry) culture. The unchecked rollout of kiosks and automated systems in restaurants and cafes has alienated the elderly and the digitally vulnerable. While businesses benefit from lower labor costs, the social safety net for those left behind—or those struggling to keep up with the breakneck pace of technological change—is non-existent. This efficiency-first capitalistic framework is squeezing the empathy out of daily life.

The "Frustrated Elite" and the Structural Cliff

South Korea’s youth are not choosing to stay single because they lack romantic drive; they are reacting to a structural wall. Despite having one of the highest university attendance rates in the world, graduates find that the "promise" of a stable life after graduation has been broken. The extreme wage gap between conglomerates and small-to-medium enterprises makes asset accumulation nearly impossible for those without family wealth.

History-dynamics research suggests that this creates a dangerous class: "frustrated elites." These are highly educated young people who followed the social script of success but found the doors closed to them. When this group realizes the "meritocracy" is a myth, their latent frustration can destabilize the entire system. Instead of lecturing the youth on "hunger spirits" or national duty, the current administration and society must address the foundational failures that have made marriage and child-rearing feel like a punishment rather than a life choice.

Ultimately, the choice to remain single is a rational survival strategy. Until the system shifts to provide genuine support and equity, these "dating strikes" will likely continue. For the youth, the path forward isn't found in conforming to 20th-century expectations, but in redefining happiness on their own terms, far from the toxic comparisons encouraged by social media and a broken economic ladder.
