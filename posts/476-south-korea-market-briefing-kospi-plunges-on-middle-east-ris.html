South Korea Market Briefing: Kospi Plunges on Middle East Risks and Foreign Sell-Offs

September 08, 2026

    What happened: The Kospi, South Korea's benchmark stock index, plunged sharply in afternoon trading on Middle East geopolitical tensions, while retail investors aggressively bet against the market by pouring about $2.2 billion (3 trillion won) into inverse products.
  • What it changes: Surging oil prices—with Brent crude nearing $100 a barrel—are reigniting inflation and interest rate fears, dragging down high-flying tech and semiconductor stocks despite underlying supply shortages.
  • Who is affected: Retail traders facing severe account losses from volatile derivatives, major tech exporters navigating soaring memory demand, and Korean policymakers balancing robust GDP growth metrics against tightening monetary pressures.

South Korea's financial markets suffered a sharp reality check as afternoon trading wiped out morning gains, driven by a missile attack by Yemen's Houthi rebels on Saudi civilian infrastructure that injured at least 73 people. The resulting spike in oil prices pushed global bond yields higher and triggered a broad sell-off across Asian equities, including Japan's Nikkei. While big institutional players trimmed risk, South Korean retail investors piled heavily into inverse exchange-traded funds (ETFs) to bet on further market declines—a high-risk strategy that highlights widespread anxiety among mom-and-pop traders.

Beneath the daily market turbulence, structural shifts are reshaping Korean industries. Apple has abandoned its short-term pricing leverage, locking in multi-year, long-term supply agreements for NAND flash memory to survive extreme component shortages driven by hyperscale AI data center investments. Meanwhile, SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won has proposed a bold economic partnership with Japan to jointly purchase and stockpile energy, aiming to build a massive $6 trillion bloc to navigate US-China supply chain fragmentation. These moves signal that while short-term sentiment remains fragile, the underlying industrial chess board is being redrawn.

High-Yield ETF Frenzy Faces a Reality Check

The explosive growth of covered-call ETFs in South Korea has turned into a double-edged sword for retail investors. The market has ballooned to over $19 billion (26 trillion won) across 62 products, fueled by eye-catching annual dividend yields ranging from 10% to over 22% in popular funds like KODEX 200 Target Weekly Covered Call and TIGER Dividend Covered Call Flavor.

While these high-yield products provide steady monthly payouts during sideways markets, financial experts warn of hidden risks. Covered-call strategies capture option premiums during stable periods, but a sharp market downturn can quickly erode the principal, wiping out months of accumulated dividends. Furthermore, holding these aggressive dividend-paying funds in standard taxable accounts exposes investors to steep comprehensive financial income taxes, making tax-advantaged accounts like Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs) essential for risk-conscious participants.
