South Korea Targets AI Cheating in College Entrance Exam with Drastic Penalties

November 18, 2026

Following a flurry of political activity on November 17, South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party (DP) has moved to implement severe punishments for students caught using artificial intelligence to cheat on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), including a potential ten-year ban on future exam attempts.

  • New Task Force: The DP officially launched a specialized education committee to oversee AI-related academic integrity, with plans to enact a "Special Act on AI Cheating Prevention."
  • Harsher Penalties: The party is pushing to extend the current one-year ban on retaking the CSAT to up to 10 years for those caught using AI tools, aiming to apply these measures starting with this year's exam.
  • Political Backlash: A proposal by committee chair Kim Moon-soo to have students monitor and report each other was retracted within hours following intense public and opposition criticism.
  • Heightened Response: At the ruling party's request, the government has upgraded the official response level, with the Prime Minister—rather than the Minister of Education—expected to lead the public address on the issue.

A Scramble for Control in the AI Era

The Democratic Party’s sudden legislative offensive highlights the growing anxiety surrounding "AI glasses" and other wearable tech that could undermine the integrity of the CSAT, a high-stakes exam that effectively dictates the academic and professional futures of Korean students. By pushing for a 10-year ban, the government is signaling that it intends to treat AI-assisted cheating not as a minor breach of conduct, but as a severe offense requiring a deterrent of equal weight.

However, the rapid rollout of these measures—marked by the swift abandonment of the "student mutual surveillance" proposal—suggests that the administration is struggling to balance public demand for "fairness" with practical, enforceable policy. The move to have the Prime Minister take the lead on the issue shows that the Lee Jae-myung administration is keen to demonstrate proactive governance, though critics argue the speed of the legislative race could lead to rushed, poorly considered policy.

Legislative Competition or Strategic Posturing?

As the ruling DP and the main opposition People Power Party (the conservative opposition) both scramble to propose tougher laws, the central challenge remains execution. While there is broad consensus on the need to protect exam integrity, the fine print of these bills—specifically how to fairly differentiate between malicious AI usage and simple rule violations—will be the true test for the National Assembly.

For the average student, the situation is one of mounting uncertainty. While the goal is a "fair exam," the threat of a decade-long ban being applied retroactively or in a hurry creates a high-pressure environment. Whether these measures can be implemented before this year's exam depends entirely on whether the two parties can move beyond their current competition for optics and reach a viable, consensus-based legal framework.
