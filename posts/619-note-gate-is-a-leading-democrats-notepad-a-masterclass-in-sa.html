"Note-Gate": Is a Leading Democrat’s Notepad a Masterclass in Sabotage?

September 10, 2026

  • What happened: During a parliamentary plenary session, Kim Min-seok, a senior leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK, the main liberal opposition), had his personal notepad captured by press cameras. It featured notes labeling political rivals “out” and listed potential candidates for the Seoul mayoral race.
  • The "Slip": Critics—including from the rival People Power Party (PPP, the ruling conservative party)—argue the exposure was intentional, designed to publicly pressure opponents and signal a shift in party dynamics.
  • The Fallout: The notes effectively outed a plan to push Rep. Jung Chung-rae, a powerful internal rival, into the nearly unwinnable Seoul mayoral race, sparking a fierce, public backlash from within the party.

For observers of South Korean politics, the "notepad incident" is being viewed less as a clumsy accident and more as a calculated performance. In the high-stakes environment of the National Assembly, leaving a memo exposed to cameras—specifically one containing high-profile names and blunt political directives—is widely seen as a tactical maneuver to control the narrative. By positioning the notepad, Kim Min-seok appears to have attempted an "all-in-one" strategy: rallying his base by attacking the government and simultaneously neutralizing a formidable internal rival.

The core of the controversy lies in the mention of Rep. Jung Chung-rae as a potential candidate for mayor of Seoul. With the DPK facing an uphill battle in the capital, nominating a major figure like Jung is seen by many analysts not as a genuine attempt to win, but as a "poisoned chalice." The theory is that Kim sought to leverage the "party-first" (seondanghusa) rhetoric to force Jung out of his safe legislative district, clearing the way for Kim’s own allies to take control of Jung's power base.

A Backfire in the Making

The reaction from Jung Chung-rae was immediate and scathing. Publicly rebuking the party leadership on social media, Jung characterized the maneuver as "cruel" and a blatant attempt to sideline him. This outburst has laid bare the deep, toxic divisions within the Democratic Party. Instead of a masterstroke of political engineering, the incident has morphed into a self-inflicted wound, exposing the party leadership’s obsession with internal power struggles rather than coherent policy governance.

The scandal has also drawn attention to broader efforts to purge the party of dissenters. The recent appointment of former lawmaker Choi Jae-sung—a figure associated with previous election failures—to a senior advisory role is being interpreted as part of a wider effort to consolidate power around the current leadership. Critics argue that behind the facade of "party reform," the current leadership is aggressively clearing the board to ensure personal loyalty, a practice that risks alienating the party’s core supporters and damaging its credibility ahead of future elections.

Ultimately, this "notepad diplomacy" highlights a depressing reality of contemporary Korean politics: the subordination of legislative duty to Machiavellian power games. If the party continues to prioritize the removal of rivals over genuine political vision, the voters—who are increasingly wary of these transparent ploys—are likely to provide a harsh verdict when they next head to the polls.
