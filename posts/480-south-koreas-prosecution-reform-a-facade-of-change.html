South Korea's Prosecution Reform: A Facade of Change?

September 09, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea is set to dissolve the 77-year-old Supreme Prosecutors' Office, splitting investigation and indictment powers under a new Government Organization Act.
  • The catch: Despite the new "Prosecution Office" (Gongso-cheong) branding, critics point out that detective units, digital forensics departments, and the exact headcount of 2,292 prosecutors remain entirely untouched.
  • Who is affected: Ordinary citizens funding bloated state bureaucracies, and the ruling Democratic Party, which faces backlash for pushing a rushed legislative timeline without cutting core prosecutorial privileges.

Same prosecutors, new nameplates

For decades, South Korea's prosecution service has wielded near-absolute power at the top of the country's judicial system. Now, as the National Assembly prepares to pass sweeping government reorganization bills, that name is technically leaving the statute books. The administration frames this as a historic triumph for democracy—dividing investigative duties from indictments to return power to the public.

Look past the political rhetoric, however, and the reality looks suspiciously like a re-branding exercise. Key investigative divisions within the top prosecutor's office have simply been moved wholesale under the new indictment-focused agency. Out of eight senior prosecutor posts, five remain dedicated to investigation units under slightly different titles. Meanwhile, the number of prosecutors remains locked at 2,292—not a single desk has been cut. Instead, reductions fell entirely on administrative and support staff, leaving the legal elite's core infrastructure fully intact.

Forensics, bloated ranks, and political failure

The survival of the prosecution's digital forensics and advanced science units adds insult to injury. Despite police forces maintaining their own capable tech-investigation arms, the prosecution retained these powerful evidence-analysis tools, effectively positioning itself to audit and second-guess police findings. Critics argue this preserves a formidable apparatus previously used to shape narratives and protect political interests, all funded by taxpayers.

Compounding the problem is the bizarre three-tier structure of South Korea's prosecution system—combining local offices, high courts, and the supreme office in a setup without global parallel. Senior prosecutors unable to secure promotions routinely sit in high-prosecutor positions with minimal heavy caseloads, functioning as an expensive holding pen. Observers note that while the ruling Democratic Party rushed to meet legislative deadlines, its failure to amend the separate Prosecutor Act—which mandates prosecutor staffing levels—left the door wide open for bureaucrats to draft loopholes that preserve their old fiefdoms.
