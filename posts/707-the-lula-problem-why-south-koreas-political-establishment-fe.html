The "Lula" Problem: Why South Korea’s Political Establishment Fears a Populist Revival

October 05, 2026

The political future of Lee Jae-myung, leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal opposition), is being compared to Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, sparking intense debate over whether South Korea’s rigid power structure will allow another "outsider" to rise for the next decade.

  • Parallel Lives: Both Lee and Lula rose from extreme poverty—Lee as a child laborer in a factory and Lula as a shoeshine boy and union leader—to become central political figures representing the working class.
  • The "Outsider" Threat: Both men are viewed by their respective countries' elites as volatile populists who threaten established norms and institutional power.
  • The Legal Battleground: Just as Lula survived imprisonment and political exile to return to power, Lee faces relentless legal scrutiny, which supporters view as a targeted attempt by the establishment to extinguish his political career.
  • Deep Polarization: The obsession with these figures has turned Korean politics into a zero-sum game, fueling fanatical support from base voters and extreme hostility from opponents.

The "Dirt Spoon" Narrative and the Fear of Disruption

Lee Jae-myung’s rise is rooted in a brutal childhood. Born to a family of slash-and-burn farmers in Andong, he worked in a Seongnam industrial complex as a young boy, suffering a permanent arm disability from a factory accident. This trajectory—from a laborer without formal schooling to a lawyer and presidential candidate—defies the traditional South Korean elite path, which usually requires credentials from top-tier universities and legal or bureaucratic circles.

To his supporters, Lee is more than a politician; he is a proxy for their own struggles and "han" (a uniquely Korean sentiment of deep-seated sorrow and resentment). To his critics, however, this "outsider" persona is a dangerous liability. His tendency to bypass established political protocols is seen by the establishment as a direct threat to the status quo, prompting a visceral reaction that mirrors the opposition Lula faced in Brazil.

The Structural Wall: Can Korea Produce Another Lula?

The common refrain among local political observers is that "because of Lee Jae-myung, we won’t see another leader like Brazil’s Lula for at least 10 years." This is not necessarily a comment on Lee’s specific policies, but rather an admission of how deeply "anti-outsider" the Korean system has become. While Lula’s return was made possible by Brazil’s relative democratic elasticity, South Korea’s political environment—dominated by a tightly knit "cartel" of corporate, media, and legal elites—is far less forgiving.

The current state of Korean politics has devolved into a wearying, endless legal battle. Parliament has ceased to be a place for debating livelihood issues and has instead become a courtroom where legal defense and political offense are the only currencies. This environment does more than just hurt Lee; it stifles the emergence of new, grassroots leaders who might otherwise challenge the status quo. By turning politics into a trap of mutual annihilation, the establishment has ensured that only those groomed within the "greenhouse" of the two major parties can survive.

The tragedy of the current climate is not just about one man’s legal risks. It is the realization that Korea’s political structure has become a closed loop, where the very act of challenging the elite invites a level of social and legal destruction that makes genuine reform nearly impossible.

Ultimately, the "Lula comparison" serves as a mirror for South Korean society. If the country cannot tolerate an outsider who forces the system to confront its own class inequities, it may find itself permanently locked into a stagnant, polarized stalemate, unable to produce the transformative leadership it claims to seek.
