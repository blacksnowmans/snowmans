South Korea’s Housing and Political Crisis: A Reality Check

October 03, 2026

  • Housing Mirage: The administration’s pledge to supply 1.19 million homes in the Seoul metropolitan area is being dismissed by critics as a "paper plan" that ignores current construction financing freezes and realistic site limitations.
  • Infrastructure Deadlock: New satellite cities (the "3rd generation new towns") are facing critical delays, as residents move in without the promised transit infrastructure—a problem exacerbated by slow, mandatory feasibility studies.
  • Political Erosion: President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has hit a record low of 36.5%, with his party facing the unprecedented collapse of its traditional stronghold in the Honam (Southwest) region.

The Housing Supply Fantasy

The government’s plan to construct 1.19 million homes across the Seoul metropolitan area has drawn sharp criticism for its lack of physical or economic grounding. In the dense urban core of Seoul, finding sufficient land for such massive volume is effectively impossible. Furthermore, the local construction industry is currently paralyzed; developers are unable to secure bridge loans for Project Financing (PF), and sky-high land prices have rendered many projects unviable.

Critics argue that the administration is prioritizing "desk-side" statistics over the reality on the ground. By focusing on lofty numbers to stabilize housing prices, the government is sending misleading signals to a market that is already suffering from a lack of liquidity. Without addressing the underlying financial crisis for developers, these supply goals remain purely aspirational.

Infrastructure and the "Ghost Town" Risk

The situation is equally dire in the 3rd generation new towns—such as Wangsuk, Daejang, Changrung, Gyeyang, and Gyosan. While these projects were marketed as major residential hubs, they are being built without adequate transit connectivity to Seoul. The current bureaucracy requires lengthy "preliminary feasibility studies" (Ye-ta) for subways, which can delay infrastructure by up to a decade.

There is growing pressure on the Korea Land and Housing Corporation (LH)—the state agency responsible for land development—to waive these studies or expedite them alongside construction. Without such bold legislative intervention, these new towns risk becoming isolated "bed-towns" or ghost cities rather than functional residential hubs.

The Honam Defection

Perhaps the most seismic shift is the cooling of support in the Honam region, a historic power base for the Democratic Party. Recent polling showing support for President Lee Jae-myung dipping to 36.5% signals that the traditional base is no longer offering reflexive support. Observers note that when a party’s core constituency begins to feel "looted" or ignored by their own representatives, the political fallout is usually terminal.

The current administration’s tendency to label dissenters as "an evil minority" rather than reflecting on policy failures has only deepened this divide. As the government struggles to secure critical infrastructure like semiconductor fabrication equipment, the internal political friction is preventing the country from managing its key economic systems. For the administration to recover, it must move past bureaucratic posturing and address the cold reality of its crumbling public support and stagnant real estate market.
