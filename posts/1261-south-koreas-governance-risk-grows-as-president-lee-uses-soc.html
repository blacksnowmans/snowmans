South Korea’s Governance Risk Grows as President Lee Uses Social Media to Attack Internal Critics

October 18, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s unfiltered social media outbursts and relentless targeting of internal critics have sparked a major governance crisis, replacing formal statecraft with digital intimidation.

  • What happened: President Lee has repeatedly used platforms like X (formerly Twitter) to vent personal frustration, implicitly signaling his online supporters to target scholars, allies, and lawmakers who offer constructive criticism.
  • What it changes: Bypassing official government spokesperson channels, executive power is being deployed as a tool for personal retaliation, triggering swarms of online harassment against dissenters.
  • Key appointment: The recent naming of a former National Tax Service chief as Presidential Chief of Staff signals a dangerous pivot toward audit-driven political control rather than democratic compromise.
  • Who is affected: Internal reformers—including members of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the minor liberal Rebuilding Korea Party—and policy intellectuals whose OECD-aligned proposals are dismissed as "radical."

Digital Retaliation Replaces Democratic Debate

The presidency in South Korea is a constitutional institution designed to unify and safeguard the state, yet President Lee Jae-myung’s recent behavior reduces executive authority to impulsive emotional posts. Rather than conducting policy debates through formal press briefings, raw statements accusing critics of seeking favors or pushing "radical" agendas are posted online. This acts as a signal for aggressive online supporters to launch coordinated cyberattacks against anyone voicing dissent, effectively replacing democratic debate with digital intimidation.

This weaponized rhetoric targets not just opposition figures, but core allies who supported the administration's rise—including former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae and lawmakers from the minor liberal Rebuilding Korea Party. By smearing reformers as self-serving elites simply for recommending policy course corrections, the presidency seeks to destroy the personal credibility of its critics. While similar polarising tactics were visible during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure through far-right online channels, seeing the sitting president directly coordinate targets from his smartphone represents a severe breakdown of institutional governance.

A Dangerous Pivot Toward State Intimidation

Equally alarming is the administration's decision to appoint a former head of the National Tax Service as Presidential Chief of Staff. In South Korean political history, tax audits have frequently been weaponized to suppress critical media, businesses, and dissidents. Placing a top tax official at the center of presidential operations sends a clear message: the administration prefers administrative pressure and investigation over political negotiation.

Dismissing data-backed critiques from academics—such as calls to fix South Korea's low tax redistribution levels based on OECD statistics—as "radical" shows a presidency isolating itself from objective facts. When a government silences constructive criticism with ideological labels and surrounds itself with audit authorities, it forfeits its moral authority. Leaders who trade systemic governance for personal grievance and digital harassment inevitably alienate their own base and accelerate their political isolation.
