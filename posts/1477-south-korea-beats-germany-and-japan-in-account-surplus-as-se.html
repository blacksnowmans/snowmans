South Korea Beats Germany and Japan in Account Surplus as Semiconductor Boom Surges

October 26, 2026

Driven by an unprecedented artificial intelligence chip boom, South Korea recorded a record-breaking $116.6 billion current account surplus in the second quarter, making it the world’s second-largest surplus nation and surpassing Germany and Japan combined.

  • What happened: South Korean exports hit an all-time high of $121.1 billion in September, while chipmakers Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are projected to post a combined Q3 operating profit of up to 185 trillion won (about $135 billion).
  • What it changes: Aggressive deregulation under President Lee Jae-myung—including slashing 13 major administrative hurdles—is speeding up infrastructure projects like the Yongin semiconductor cluster to lock in Korea's technological lead.
  • Who is affected: Global hardware makers are feeling the squeeze as memory chip supply shifts heavily to AI servers, driving up component costs and causing global PC shipments to plunge 21% year-over-year.

A Polarized Tech Boom and Corporate Resurgence

The record surplus highlights how deeply South Korea’s economic fortunes are tied to global AI demand. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the country's two memory giants, are projecting astonishing operating profit margins near 80%, fueled by overwhelming orders for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). Corporate momentum is further bolstered by Samsung’s next-generation Exynos 2700 mobile processor beating Qualcomm’s latest chip in internal benchmarks, signaling a potential rebound for its system chip division. Meanwhile, Samsung regained the top spot in India's smartphone market after nine quarters, demonstrating commercial strength despite a broader regional slowdown.

Structural Risks Behind the Record Numbers

Yet, this single-engine growth conceals real economic vulnerabilities. While semiconductors soar, other core Korean manufacturing sectors such as automobiles and petrochemicals remain bogged down by weak global demand. Furthermore, the extreme diversion of memory manufacturing toward AI servers has spiked prices for standard DRAM and NAND chips. Finished-goods makers unable to absorb these costs cut back production, driving global third-quarter PC shipments down 21% to 57 million units—a clear sign that an unbalanced supply chain could eventually backfire on chip demand.

Domestically, the Lee Jae-myung administration's decisive policy support—a stark contrast to the policy stagnation experienced during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure—has provided long-awaited relief to high-tech manufacturers. However, South Korean policymakers now face a double challenge: spreading this semiconductor windfall into local consumer spending while ensuring that state-backed infrastructure development reaches beyond the capital region to southern hubs like Honam.
