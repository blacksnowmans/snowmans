President Lee Jae-myung’s Risky Pivot: Abandoning Progressive Core for Conservative Elites

November 01, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is risking his political foundation by distancing himself from his core progressive base in an attempt to build a broad "structural majority" through compromises with conservative elites.

  • The Pivot: President Lee has begun recruiting conservative figures, including former government officials Lee Seok-yeon and Kim Sung-sik, to push his approval rating toward a target of 60% to 70%.
  • The Rift: Grassroots supporters who defended Lee against political prosecutions during the tenure of Mr. Yun Suk-yeol now feel sidelined, as Lee frames their demands for sweeping reform as radical self-interest.
  • The Strategy: The administration is attempting an approach similar to Japan’s ruling Liberal Democratic Party (LDP), seeking long-term power through accommodation with conservative establishments.
  • The Consequence: By alienating his primary base, President Lee has dismantled his organic public shield, leaving his administration politically isolated and vulnerable to external attacks.

The Illusion of a "Structural Majority"

President Lee Jae-myung’s strategy hinges on the belief that the liberal bloc—led by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—remains a "structural minority" unable to sustain long-term governance alone. During an October 19 press conference at the presidential press center, Lee signaled a shift toward moderate and conservative voters. However, by framing his key supporters' calls for thorough reform as narrow factionalism, Lee is sacrificing the grassroots engine that brought his party to power.

To construct this broader coalition, Lee has brought in several conservative figures, including Lee Seok-yeon, Lee Byung-tae, Kim Sung-sik, and Lee Hae-woon. Rather than expanding his reach, this policy has alienated traditional supporters who protested to defend him during the administration of Mr. Yun Suk-yeol, the impeached leader currently in prison. By putting key promises—such as prosecutorial reform—on the back burner, Lee risks eroding his political legitimacy.

Grassroots Shield Replaced by Isolation

Historically, liberal South Korean presidents relied heavily on enthusiastic, voluntary public defense during political crises. Under Lee, however, this organic shield has mostly dissolved into cynicism. In its place, the administration has come to rely on friendly political commentators and online influencers who lack genuine persuasive power, leaving the presidency exposed whenever controversies erupt.

This isolation is already taking a political toll. Facing growing pressure and lacking a passionate base to defend his policy decisions, President Lee recently lashed out at critics during an overseas trip, declaring that "to pig eyes, everything looks like a pig." Seeking long-term dominance by allying with conservative establishments overlooks the dynamic nature of South Korea's democracy, which has historically punished leaders who abandon their reform promises.
