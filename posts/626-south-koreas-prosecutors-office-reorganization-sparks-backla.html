South Korea's Prosecutors' Office Reorganization Sparks Backlash Amid Deepening Inequality

September 10, 2026

South Korea’s political opposition is crying foul over a sudden government push to reorganize the prosecution service, warning that reforms are being watered down into a mere rebranding exercise while severe economic inequality goes unaddressed.

  • The Issue: The Ministry of Justice has introduced a revised plan for the new Public Prosecutors' Office—intended to replace the traditional prosecution service—that keeps prosecutor staffing levels virtually unchanged despite promises to strip away direct investigative powers.
  • The Political Fallout: Shin Chang-sik, leader of the minor opposition Rebuilding Korea Party, slammed both the government and the ruling Democratic Party for losing their reformist edge and shifting too far to the right.
  • The Economic Contrast: While macro indicators flash green—with August exports surging nearly 70% to about $98 billion—micro realities show youth unemployment hitting 6.8% and elderly poverty at a staggering 39.7%, the highest in the OECD.
  • The Response: Critics are demanding immediate action on inequality, including a proposed "Workers' Basic Law" to protect some 8 million platform and freelance workers currently left outside traditional labor protections.

A Rebranded Prosecution Service or Retained Power?

The core dispute centers on the Ministry of Justice's newly proposed organizational structure. While the envisioned Public Prosecutors' Office is billed as a step toward separating investigation from indictment, it retains a massive headcount of over 2,200 prosecutors. Critics argue that creating a new "judicial control department" within local offices simply hands prosecutors a backdoor method to oversee police investigations, betraying the original intent of prosecutorial reform.

The ruling Democratic Party has also drawn heavy fire for sitting on its hands and moderating its stance in pursuit of conservative and moderate swing voters. Observers note that by prioritizing tax compromises and softening reform pledges to win elections, the ruling bloc risks alienating the progressive base that brought them to power. The Rebuilding Korea Party warns that it will not act as a silent partner if the ruling party continues to backpedal on systemic reforms.

Glittering Exports vs. A Faltering Safety Net

Beneath South Korea's impressive headline economic numbers—such as a projected 3.3% GDP growth rate and soaring semiconductor-driven exports—lies a punishing reality for ordinary citizens. Small business survival rates remain low, and marginalized workers face precarious conditions without adequate safety nets. As debate mounts over whether policymakers are too focused on high-altitude economic metrics while ordinary households struggle on the ground, pressure is growing in the National Assembly to establish a dedicated special committee on inequality to tackle disparities in income, housing, and labor head-on.
