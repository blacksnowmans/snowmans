South Korea Confronts the Forgotten 1946 October Uprising on its 80th Anniversary

September 24, 2026

Eighty years after some 2.3 million South Koreans rose up against hunger, police brutality, and the US military administration in the country's first major post-liberation mass protest, the event remains largely absent from official textbooks and is being commemorated entirely through grassroots crowdfunding.

  • What happened: The October Uprising began in Daegu on October 1, 1946, drawing an estimated 2.3 million participants—more than 10% of the Korean Peninsula's population at the time—amid severe food shortages and crackdowns.
  • The human cost: Mostly young protesters and their families were branded as communists (a dangerous "red" label under state authorities), resulting in decades of social stigma, state violence, and suppressed trauma.
  • Current situation: The 80th-anniversary event committee is organizing a memorial ceremony and a concert in October entirely through public donations, having received zero financial support from local governments.
  • Who is affected: Aging bereaved families, who are making a final plea for historical truth and official recognition before they pass away.

Unearthing a Buried Chapter of Modern Korean History

To outside observers, South Korea’s modern political history typically starts with the Korean War or later democratization movements like the 1980 Gwangju Uprising. Yet the chaos following liberation from Japanese colonial rule in 1945 birthed an earlier, massive wave of popular resistance. Sparked by starvation, the return of collaborators, and the vacuum left by the US military government, ordinary citizens poured into the streets in late 1946. Organizers like executive committee member Park Seok-jun emphasize that this was no localized riot, but a nationwide cry for basic human survival and dignity.

Despite its scale, the uprising was long marginalized by state narratives and anti-communist crackdowns. Bereaved family association head Chae Young-hee recalls how young men in their twenties and their equally young wives lost everything, subsequently spending their lives trapped under the lifelong scarlet letter of being "communist sympathizers." State violence did not end with the killings; it systematically destroyed survivors' lives through guilt-by-association laws and persistent surveillance, silencing generations of families.

Grassroots Remembrance vs. Institutional Silence

As the 80th anniversary arrives, the contrast between the historical weight of the event and its contemporary official neglect is stark. With local governments offering neither budgets nor basic attention, organizers have raised roughly 40 million won (about $30,000) through citizen crowdfunding to fund the upcoming memorial and an arts festival titled "Ullim"—a regional word carrying the dual meaning of a desperate cry and a spreading echo.

This reliance on ordinary people's pockets rather than state funds highlights a persistent gap in South Korea's historical accountability. When power and institutional media look away, preserving the truth falls entirely to the public. For survivors like Chae, whose final wish is simply for the truth of their loved ones' deaths to be accurately recorded before they die, this anniversary represents a crucial, fleeting window to reclaim a buried past.
