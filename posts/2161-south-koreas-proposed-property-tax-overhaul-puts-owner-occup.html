South Korea's Proposed Property Tax Overhaul Puts Owner-Occupants First

November 10, 2026

South Korea is locked in a fierce debate over a sweeping property tax overhaul proposed by the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, designed to heavily reward actual homeowners while clamping down on speculators.

  • What happened: The government is pushing to raise the comprehensive real estate holding tax exemption for primary homeowners living in their properties to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million), while keeping the cap at 900 million won (about $650,000) for non-resident property owners.
  • What it changes: The policy aims to widen the tax burden between people who actually live in their homes and those who hoard properties purely for investment, fundamentally treating housing as a place to live rather than a speculative asset.
  • Who is affected: Middle-class single-home owners stand to get significant tax relief, while multiple-home owners and absentee landlords face stricter penalties and higher social costs.

At the heart of this debate is a glaring absurdity in South Korea's current tax code: a person driving a mid-sized sedan with a 3,000cc engine can easily pay more in annual car tax (about $580) than an absentee owner pays in property tax on a 2-billion-won (about $1.45 million) luxury home after various deductions. Critics rightly point out that when multi-billion-won assets are taxed more leniently than automobiles, the system is fundamentally broken. The previous administration under impeached former leader Yoon Seok-yeol unspooled these safeguards, leaving ordinary citizens struggling with housing costs while asset-holders caught a break.

President Lee's ruling Democratic Party faces a delicate political balancing act. Some lawmakers warn against overly timid "dry" policymaking that avoids conflict, arguing that meaningful tax reform requires the political courage to stare down conservative opposition and wealthy vested interests. If the administration wants voters to feel the true impact of political change, it must push past pushback from pro-landlord commentators crying "punitive taxation" and restore basic tax fairness so that society's heaviest assets carry a fair share of the load.
