South Korea’s New Investigative Agency Faces "Prosecutorial Cartel" Backlash

September 29, 2026

South Korea’s plan to launch the Major Crime Investigative Agency (MCIA)—a new body designed to strip the prosecution of its investigative powers—is being overshadowed by controversy over the appointment of its inaugural chief.

  • The Contender: Reports, including a widely discussed exclusive from the Dong-A Ilbo, suggest Lim Hyun, a prosecutor known for his close ties to the "pro-Yoon" (loyalists to former President Yoon Suk Yeol) faction, is the leading candidate.
  • The Core Conflict: Critics argue that filling the new agency with veterans of the existing prosecution service—specifically those from the elite Special Investigation Department—will lead to the "old guard" simply operating under a new name.
  • The Risk: Skeptics fear a repeat of the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO), which promised reform but was widely criticized for failing to break away from internal bureaucratic inertia.

The "Prosecutor Cartel" Fears

The core of the anxiety lies in the professional DNA of the people being floated for leadership. The names appearing in the press, including Lim Hyun, Kim Yu-cheol, and Yang Seok-jo, are familiar figures from the era of intense friction between then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol and the Ministry of Justice. For many observers, these individuals represent the very "prosecutorial supremacy" that the new agency was created to dismantle.

The concern is not just about the top post, but the entire organizational structure. If the MCIA is staffed by people who spent decades mastering the "Special Investigation" playbook—a system that often relied on leverage and intimidation—the institution’s structural independence won't matter. History shows that when a new agency is staffed with 80% or more of personnel from the old guard, the organization reverts to its former behavior within three years.

Reform or Rebranding?

The debate highlights a fundamental divide: Is the MCIA a blank slate, or is it destined to be a "Special Investigation Department 2.0"? Defenders of the current appointment strategy argue that "prosecutorial expertise" is essential for tackling high-level corruption and that the institution’s design—the separation of investigation and prosecution—is what truly matters, not the backgrounds of the individuals in charge.

However, that argument rings hollow for many citizens who feel "burned" by previous reform efforts. The trauma of seeing past reform agencies become extensions of the prosecution’s power remains deep. When individuals who have spent their careers prioritizing the "logic of the organization" over personal principles are placed in charge, the public is rightfully skeptical. If these leaders are chosen simply because they are "stable" or "experienced," the government risks gutting the reform before it even begins, proving that without a radical change in personnel and philosophy, no amount of institutional restructuring can fix a broken culture.
