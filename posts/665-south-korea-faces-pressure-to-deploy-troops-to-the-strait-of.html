South Korea Faces Pressure to Deploy Troops to the Strait of Hormuz

September 11, 2026

South Korea is currently grappling with mounting pressure from the United States to deploy military forces to the Strait of Hormuz by November 1 and to increase its share of defense spending, sparking intense domestic debate over sovereignty and national interest.

  • The Demand: The U.S. has intensified calls for South Korean military participation in the Strait of Hormuz, a critical maritime chokepoint, and requested an increase in the “defense cost-sharing” (the funds Seoul pays to support the presence of U.S. troops in South Korea).
  • The Security Risk: Critics, including retired four-star general and current lawmaker Kim Byung-joo, argue that deployment would make South Korean troops and oil tankers prime targets for Iranian retaliation.
  • Strategic Dilemma: The government faces a choice between maintaining traditional alliance expectations and asserting the "strategic autonomy" necessary to avoid being treated as a subordinate partner.
  • Public Opposition: There is growing consensus among experts and the public that the government must resist these demands to avoid being drawn into a conflict without clear strategic justification.

The Risks of "Mercenary" Diplomacy

The argument for deployment—often framed as a matter of energy security—is increasingly dismissed by security analysts as a dangerous fallacy. Critics argue that sending troops into a volatile region where the U.S. lacks a clear roadmap for conflict resolution would turn South Korea into a high-priority target. As the global order shifts away from U.S. hegemony, experts contend that "blind loyalty" is no longer a viable security strategy.

There is also deep skepticism regarding the U.S. demand for increased defense cost-sharing. Observers note that the administration of Donald Trump tends to view alliances through a purely transactional lens. The consensus among critics is that yielding to these financial demands will not satisfy the U.S., but rather embolden it to seek further concessions. The prevailing sentiment is that South Korea must shift from a vertical, subservient alliance to a more balanced, equitable partnership.

Shifting the Paradigm of the U.S.-ROK Alliance

The current debate highlights a broader, uncomfortable truth: the era of total reliance on the U.S. is closing. With South Korea now boasting one of the world's top five military powers, proponents of a new approach argue it is time to leverage that strength. Rather than fearing abandonment, the South Korean government is being urged to use its strategic importance as a bargaining chip to secure autonomy.

Ultimately, the decision on the Strait of Hormuz represents a fork in the road for Seoul. Whether the government chooses to remain a "piece in the pocket" of U.S. policy or assert its own sovereign interests will likely define the future of the U.S.-ROK relationship for years to come. For many, the message is clear: true allies are partners who speak as equals, not subordinates who are exploited for the sake of fading hegemonic interests.
