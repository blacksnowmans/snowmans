South Korean Politics Descends into Bitter Rhetorical Warfare

November 01, 2026

South Korea’s political sphere plunged into crude partisan vitriol this week as President Lee Jae-myung and the conservative opposition traded explosive insults over presidential diplomacy, family leisure, and institutional integrity.

  • A vulgar clash over diplomacy: Defending his frequent overseas trips against accusations of wasteful travel, President Lee sparked public fury by posting on social media that "pigs only see pigs," prompting the conservative opposition to accuse him of degrading the dignity of the presidency as his approval ratings dropped back into the 30% range.
  • The "nuisance golf" offensive: Jang Dong-hyeok, head of the main conservative People Power Party (PPP), escalated attacks on the first family, labeling their outings "nuisance golf" and ridiculing the president’s claims of exhaustion from summitry.
  • A diplomatic flap over a U.S. visa: When ruling Democratic Party lawmaker Jin Sung-joon faced bureaucratic delays that held up his U.S. entry visa until the eve of his departure, the PPP branded the incident an alliance disaster, while Democrats accused the opposition of self-destructive sycophancy toward Washington.
  • Spillover into the courts and elections: The confrontation broadened as Democrats pushed for a parliamentary probe into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, while the PPP demanded the preservation of ballots stored at Seoul’s Olympic Park amid claims of electoral fraud.

Rhetorical Escalation Replaces Governance

The sudden escalation in harsh language reflects a zero-sum struggle that has largely bypassed policy and public welfare. For the ruling Democratic Party, President Lee's choice to abandon refined presidential messaging in favor of raw online retorts has backfired. While intended to signal fierce dedication to the national interest abroad, words like "pigs" inevitably drag statecraft into the gutter. Coupled with ongoing controversies surrounding his comments on foreign labor, this combative posture has driven moderate voters away, leaving the administration bogged down in self-inflicted communication crises.

At the same time, the conservative PPP is leaning heavily into emotional wedge issues. Having delivered a lackluster performance during the recently concluded annual parliamentary audit, the party leadership under Jang Dong-hyeok has substituted structural policy critiques with personal attacks on presidential ethics. Seizing on keywords like "golf" during a period of economic strain allows the opposition to tap into public grievance, even if it offers no coherent governance alternative.

A Deepening Crisis of Institutional Trust

The most alarming facet of this deadlock is how easily fundamental institutions are dragged into the feud. By framing a routine administrative visa delay as proof of a broken security alliance, the political class is treating delicate foreign relations as cheap ammunition. Worse, the simultaneous fights over the judiciary and election management threaten the foundations of Korean democracy. While the ruling party seeks to pressure the top court through legislative inquiries, the conservative opposition is flirting with fringe election-fraud narratives over ballot preservation at Olympic Park.

South Korea has seen where institutional overreach leads, particularly during the turbulence of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term. When political parties treat the courts, election bodies, and diplomacy merely as battlegrounds to protect partisan interests, the rule of law inevitably deteriorates. Until both camps retreat from the politics of contempt, the public is left watching a governance vacuum where real economic solutions ought to be.
