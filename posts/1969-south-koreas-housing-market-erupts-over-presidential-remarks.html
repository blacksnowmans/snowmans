South Korea's Housing Market Erupts Over Presidential Remarks

November 05, 2026

South Korea's political and real estate spheres have been thrown into turmoil following President Lee Jae-myung’s recent comments suggesting that home prices in key areas are seeing sharp drops, triggering swift denials from the presidential office and fierce pushback from opposition politicians and local government heads.

  • What happened: President Lee remarked that property prices in major areas are dropping significantly, which media outlets reported as a city-wide plunge in Seoul. The presidential office quickly pushed back, labeling the broader reports as "fake news" and clarifying that the remarks referred strictly to ultra-high-end districts in southern Seoul like Gangnam.
  • Political fallout: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon and the conservative opposition People Power Party fired back, accusing the government of being out of touch with reality as official data shows Seoul home prices continuing a prolonged upward streak.
  • Who is affected: Homebuyers and tenants across South Korea caught between conflicting statistics and soaring housing costs, as political infighting overshadows actual affordability concerns.
  • Market impact: The clash highlights a deep disconnect between government housing indicators and the lived reality of everyday citizens struggling with high mortgages and tight credit controls.

A Dangerous Gap Between Statistics and Street-Level Reality

The core of the controversy lies in how cherry-picked numbers are weaponized in Korean politics. While the presidential office leans on selective data points showing slight corrections in ultra-luxury pockets of Seoul—such as the affluent Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa districts—the vast majority of working-class families and first-time homebuyers face a very different reality. In neighborhoods outside these elite enclaves, prices and jeonse (lump-sum housing deposits) remain stubbornly high, leaving prospective buyers alienated and exhausted.

By lashing out at media outlets as spreaders of "fake news" rather than addressing the root causes of housing anxiety, the administration risks looking defensive and detached. Markets do not move on presidential press briefings or semantic corrections; they move on supply, credit availability, and realistic financing options. When leaders spend their energy arguing over terminology while ordinary citizens struggle to secure a lease, trust in housing policy erodes rapidly.

Politics Over Policy: The Endless Blame Game

Predictably, the opposition was quick to capitalize on the misstep. Mayor Oh Se-hoon publicly rebuked the administration, asking if the president was living in a parallel universe given that much of Seoul has seen consecutive weeks of price gains. Conservative lawmakers piled on, framing the remarks as yet another tone-deaf attempt by the government to spin the housing crisis in its favor while failing to deliver meaningful stabilization measures.

Ultimately, this latest squabble reveals a frustrating truth about South Korea's housing debate: it is treated primarily as a political battlefield rather than a socioeconomic emergency. While the ruling party and the opposition trade insults over who owns the narrative, the heavy burden of high living costs and shrinking housing accessibility remains firmly on the shoulders of the public. Until politicians stop hiding behind statistical technicalities and start addressing the structural drivers of housing inequality, public frustration will only continue to mount.
