South Korea and Japan Shore Up Ties Ahead of Major Power Summit

November 18, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung and Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi met in Manila on Sunday to solidify security and energy cooperation, signaling a united front just two days before a historic summit between the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia.

  • Shuttle Diplomacy Resumed: The two leaders agreed to continue their "shuttle diplomacy"—the practice of regular reciprocal visits—with a fifth summit officially scheduled for January in Japan.
  • Major Power Hedge: The meeting served as a strategic alignment session ahead of the first-ever U.S.-China-Russia summit, aimed at ensuring Seoul and Tokyo are not sidelined by shifts in the global order.
  • Energy Security Pact: Both nations pledged joint responses to energy supply risks stemming from instability in the Middle East, a critical vulnerability for both resource-poor economies.
  • Lingering Friction: Despite President Lee noting that mutual public favorability has reached "historic highs," the summit took place against a backdrop of diplomatic tension following Japan's refusal to grant entry to Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party.

Strategic Alignment in a Shifting Landscape

The fourth meeting between President Lee and Prime Minister Takaichi underscores a rapid stabilization of ties under the Lee Jae-myung administration and his Democratic Party of Korea. By securing a visit to Japan in January, Lee is signaling to domestic and international audiences that the "shuttle diplomacy" initiated to repair the damage of the Yoon Suk-yeol years is now a permanent fixture of regional politics. This consistency is particularly vital as the region braces for the potential of renewed direct talks between the U.S. and North Korea under a second Trump administration.

The timing of this summit—exactly 48 hours before the major powers meet—is no coincidence. Both South Korea and Japan are navigating an increasingly complex security environment where the interests of Washington, Beijing, and Moscow are in flux. By reaffirming their partnership now, Lee and Takaichi are attempting to create a "middle-power" buffer, ensuring that the security concerns of the two primary U.S. allies in Northeast Asia are accounted for in any grand bargains between the superpowers.

Energy Resilience and Persistent Friction

Beyond high-level security, the summit focused on the "bread and butter" issue of energy security. The leaders discussed the concept of an "Asian version of the IEA" (International Energy Agency) and potential cooperation regarding the CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership), a massive regional trade pact. For South Korea, aligning with Japan on energy procurement and supply chain diversification is a pragmatic necessity to protect industrial costs and inflation from Middle Eastern volatility.

However, the briefing was not without its shadows. Even as President Lee spoke of record-high mutual favorability among citizens, he acknowledged that "difficult issues" still require progress. The same day, news broke that Japan had denied entry to Cho Kuk, a prominent opposition figure, highlighting that the path to full normalization remains cluttered with political and historical landmines. While the top-level relationship is warmer than it has been in decades, these diplomatic hiccups suggest that the "shuttle diplomacy" scheduled for January will need to move beyond symbolic gestures to tackle more sensitive bilateral grievances.
