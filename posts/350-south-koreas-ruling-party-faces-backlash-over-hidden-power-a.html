South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Backlash Over Hidden Power and Broken Personnel Systems

September 07, 2026

  • What happened: Leaked text messages and abrupt high-level firings have triggered allegations that a shadowy, unelected figure named Kim Hyun-ji is running South Korea's official personnel appointments behind the scenes.
  • What it changes: Critics argue that the official hiring infrastructure under President Lee Jae-myung has broken down, replaced by informal personal networks and absolute loyalty over merit.
  • Who is affected: The administration's democratic credibility is taking a heavy hit, leaving key government posts vacant for months and demoralizing the civil service.

The Shadowy Reality of Informal Power

In South Korea's high-stakes political arena, official titles often matter less than proximity to the leader. Recent leaks—most notably from a text exchange involving former lawmaker Kim Nam-guk—exposed how elite government positions are allegedly being carved up through informal back-channel discussions rather than transparent bureaucratic checks. While the presidential office maintains an official personnel secretary, insiders complain that the post has been sidelined into irrelevance.

This dynamic reflects a broader governance style adopted by President Lee, whose background as a hands-on local administrator in Seongnam relies on direct control rather than institutional checks and balances. By keeping critical security and law enforcement posts vacant for months—or cycling through acting deputies—the administration has turned state governance into a fragile "emergency committee" model. When high-ranking officials like former Finance Minister Koo Yun-cheol can be summarily fired at an airport without warning or proper protocol, it sends a clear, chilling message to the bureaucracy: principles don't matter, and pleasing the inner circle is everything.

A Dangerous Reliance on Personal Control

The core issue is not merely office gossip, but a fundamental erosion of democratic procedure. Lee built his political brand on a reputation for ruthless efficiency and getting things done. However, treating a sovereign nation like a personal startup—where systems are viewed as tedious bureaucratic red tape—inevitably breeds institutional failure. When leaders refuse to trust independent structures, the resulting vacuum is quickly filled by unelected power brokers.

History shows that administrations built purely around a leader's personal grip on power ultimately suffer from severe isolation and early lame-duck paralysis. South Korea's progressive movement spent decades fighting to build a transparent state apparatus governed by law rather than individual personalities. If this administration continues to hollow out those institutions in favor of private loyalty networks, it risks squandering its mandate and inviting deep political instability.
