Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Meltdown: Power Struggles, Leaked Notes, and Plunging Approval Ratings

September 09, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party, which swept into power under President Lee Jae-myung, is tearing itself apart from the inside through factional backstabbing, arrogant governance, and a severe disconnect from its base.

  • The Leaked Memo: A notebook belonging to senior Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Min-seok was caught on camera at the National Assembly, displaying hostile notes such as "Chung Cheong-rae: Seoul mayoral candidate, OUT"—exposing internal scheming against fellow party members.
  • Plunging Support: Recent polling shows President Lee's approval rating stagnating in the mid-30s, with negative sentiment inside the Democratic Party itself surging to 36.7%—matching the bleakest periods of the previous conservative administration.
  • Tone-Deaf Culture: The party's official YouTube channel faced fierce backlash for using content produced by an online creator whose profile featured derogatory slurs targeting former President Moon Jae-in, which officials defended rather than removed.
  • The Cost of Arrogance: Core progressive supporters and housing experts who helped elect the government are walking away in frustration, alienated by dismissive leadership and shifting policies.

Factional Paranoia and the Politics of Exclusion

What was supposed to be a united progressive government has devolved into an atmosphere of intense internal policing. Ever since the party leadership shift, anyone expressing independent views or failing to toe the line is quickly branded as "anti-Lee" or a "rebel." The leaked notebook incident—where a top lawmaker's notes explicitly plotted against former party chief Chung Cheong-rae's potential mayoral ambitions—proved that even after winning elections, the party's leadership remains obsessed with purging internal rivals rather than governing.

This toxic culture of exclusion has trickled down to the party's grassroots. Traditional supporters who once formed an impenetrable wall of defense are now being alienated by a leadership that mistakes arrogance for strength. When criticism arises from the public or the party's own base, officials brush it off instead of listening, creating a feedback loop of cynicism and anger.

Ebbing Trust and Dangerous Parallels to the Past

The political cost of this insular behavior is showing up clearly in the data. Recent surveys put President Lee's approval ratings neck-and-neck with the lowest points of his conservative predecessor, Yoon Suk Yeol, at a comparable point in their terms. Token cabinet reshuffles will not fix a crisis rooted in fundamental governance failures.

To reverse this downward spiral, the administration must abandon its defensive bunker mentality. President Lee needs to decisively quash persistent rumors regarding term-extension schemes, and the ruling party must stop treating moderate critics and the general public like political enemies. If the leadership continues to value online echo chambers over common-sense governance, the progressive bloc risks squandering its political mandate far faster than it won it.
