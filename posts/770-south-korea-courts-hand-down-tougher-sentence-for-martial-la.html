South Korea Courts Hand Down Tougher Sentence for Martial Law Minister

September 15, 2026

A South Korean appellate court has increased a former government minister's prison sentence to nine years, ruling that the original punishment was too lenient for an official who actively enabled an unconstitutional martial law attempt.

  • What happened: The Seoul High Court sentenced a former Interior Minister to nine years in prison, overturning a lighter lower-court sentence for their role in the short-lived December 3 emergency martial law decree.
  • The charges: The court highlighted the minister's role in enforcing unconstitutional measures, including attempting to shut down critical media outlets by ordering utility cut-offs and deploying emergency personnel.
  • Broader fallout: The ruling reinforces the judiciary's hardline stance on the December 3 crisis, following South Korea's Supreme Court upholding a seven-year sentence for former President Yoon Suk Yeol and confirming the anti-corruption agency's authority to investigate insurrection charges.
  • Who is affected: Former high-ranking officials facing prosecution for the martial law attempt, as the judiciary signals that executive orders and presidential immunity do not shield individuals from treason charges.

Constitutional Violations Overshadow Excuses

The appellate court dismantled the defense's argument that the minister played a secondary role without prior conspiracy. While the defendant claimed they were merely following administrative orders, the court emphasized that holding the office of Interior Minister carries a fundamental duty to uphold the constitution, not execute illegal directives that paralyze democratic institutions. By attempting to silence the press through physical coercion—using firefighters to cut off utilities to news organizations—the accused weaponized public safety systems against the public.

Furthermore, the court punished the defendant's lack of remorse. Throughout the 583-day legal proceedings, the former minister defended the martial law declaration and attempted to cover up actions rather than cooperate with investigators. By increasing the sentence to nine years, the judiciary sent an unambiguous message: high-ranking officials who help dismantle constitutional order will face severe penalties, and presidential immunity is no blanket pardon for insurrection.

A Test of Democratic Resilience

This ruling marks a critical chapter in South Korea's reckoning with the December 3 crisis. The attempt to use emergency powers to prop up fading executive authority exposed vulnerabilities in the country's democratic foundations. Yet, the fact that the legislature, investigative bodies, and the courts ultimately functioned as intended demonstrates the resilience of South Korea's constitutional safeguards.

For international observers watching South Korean politics, the takeaway is clear: the country's legal and democratic systems possess the institutional grit to hold the highest offices accountable. As these landmark trials conclude, the focus now shifts to institutional reforms—such as tightening oversight on emergency decrees—to ensure that the events of December 3 remain a painful historical aberration rather than a repeatable precedent.
