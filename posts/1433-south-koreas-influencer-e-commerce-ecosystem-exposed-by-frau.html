South Korea’s Influencer E-Commerce Ecosystem Exposed by Fraud, Bot Farms, and Fake Reviews

October 24, 2026

A sprawling shadow economy of manipulated social media metrics and unregulated influencer group-buying schemes has left thousands of Korean students and small-business owners facing steep financial losses while organizers evade legal accountability.

  • What happened: An investigation aired by public broadcaster KBS revealed how social media influencers took commissions of up to 18 percent selling items through ghost suppliers that never fulfilled orders, backed by coordinated review-rigging and click farms.
  • Who is affected: More than 1,000 consumers—predominantly young students lured into wire-transfer purchases of around 129,000 won (about $95)—and desperate mom-and-pop shop owners who lost millions of won to predatory marketing agencies.
  • What it changes: The findings have sparked demands for Korean regulators and courts to apply the Electronic Commerce Act strictly, treating influencers who actively orchestrate sales not as passive advertisers, but as liable commercial vendors.

The Anatomy of a Social Media Trap

The mechanics behind these group-buying operations rely on manufactured intimacy and algorithmic deception. In one highlighted scheme, an Instagram influencer repeatedly opened preorder runs for vintage iPhones, drawing hundreds of buyers through cash-only bank transfers. When deliveries stalled, the promoter launched subsequent sales rounds before blocking frustrated customers. Behind the campaign was a shell company registered at a shared workspace with no staff, operated by individuals who repeatedly swapped corporate registrations between family members to dodge sanctions from South Korea's Fair Trade Commission.

The credibility fueling these sales is shockingly cheap to manufacture. Investigative tests showed that buying 100,000 video views costs as little as 10,000 won (about $7.50). Using automated click farms and overseas proxy servers, agencies artificially inflated view counts within minutes. On major retail platforms—including Naver and Coupang—agencies rigged customer reviews through "empty box" scams: paid participants bought products on paper, received empty packages in the mail to clear automated platform tracking, and published glowing five-star ratings in exchange for product refunds and small commissions.

Small Merchants Caught in the Algorithmic Drain

While young shoppers lose monthly living allowances to phantom merchandise, small-business owners already battered by economic headwinds are suffering even deeper damage. Marketing brokers routinely cold-call restaurateurs and repair shops, promising top-tier placement on search engines and social feeds for nominal monthly rates, only to bill them several million won (typically $1,700 to $2,800) under unauthorized multi-year contracts.

The traffic delivered by these agencies is entirely hollow. Account audits revealed that viral clips boasting tens of thousands of views were watched primarily by automated bots cycling from Brazil, India, and the United States, producing zero actual diners or paying customers. When merchants demanded contract cancellations, agencies weaponized extortionate termination penalties before shutting down phone lines and vanishing.

For South Korea's e-commerce landscape, the scandal exposes an unsustainable regulatory gray zone. Influencers reap massive cuts from commercial volumes while ducking liability behind shell suppliers. Until enforcement treats high-profile online intermediaries as formal telecommunication sellers and penalizes traffic manipulation, the burden of digital fraud will continue to fall squarely on the most vulnerable consumers and neighborhood businesses.
