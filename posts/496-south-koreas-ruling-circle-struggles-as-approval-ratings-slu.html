South Korea's Ruling Circle Struggles as Approval Ratings Slump

September 09, 2026

  • The core group: Members of the "Group of 7," a veteran faction of close aides to South Korean President Lee Jae-myung, held a private meeting following a cabinet reshuffle to discuss plummeting approval ratings.
  • The admission: Participants reportedly conceded they had "no silver bullet" or clear strategy to reverse the political downturn, with some even questioning the need for the faction's continued existence.
  • The defense mechanism: Pundits and loyalists defending the administration have dismissed public criticism as bad-faith attacks driven by self-interest, while drawing contested comparisons to past administrations.

Inside the Aides' Secret Meeting: Diagnosis Without a Cure

When an administration hits a political rough patch, the familiar ritual in South Korean politics is for close circles to huddle behind closed doors. For President Lee Jae-myung, that meant a recent gathering of the "Group of 7"—lawmakers and former politicians who have backed him long before he consolidated national power. Their conclusion after reviewing the slump in presidential support? A blunt resignation: there are no easy answers.

The problem is not merely a lack of ideas, but a fundamental misdiagnosis of the ailment. By viewing falling approval numbers as an external nuisance or the result of bad-faith actors rather than a reflection of policy missteps, the inner circle traps itself in an echo chamber. Past administrations, under both conservative and progressive banners, weathered similar 30% approval floors not by dismissing critics, but by shifting course and acknowledging public fatigue.

Good Intentions Are Not a Substitute for Results

A recurring trap in the current political discourse is what can be described as mind-reading: automatically attributing benevolent motives to the president while painting any critic or dissenter as self-serving. Under this logic, if the leader's intentions are inherently good, policy failures or plunging polls cannot possibly be his fault. Critics are written off as partisan saboteurs.

This insular logic creates a dangerous shield. Supporters often point out that past progressive leaders—such as former President Moon Jae-in—faced intense demands for apologies during scandals, arguing that today's critics apply a double standard. Yet historical record shows that Moon did, in fact, issue direct public apologies during critical junctures of political friction. Comparing current demands to past precedents while ignoring actual presidential apologies distorts the facts to shield power from accountability.

Ultimately, a government is judged by the tangible impact of its choices on citizens' lives, not the purity of its intentions. When an administration's inner circle meets only to express helplessness while brushing off dissenting voices as enemies, it risks isolating itself entirely from the public it serves.
