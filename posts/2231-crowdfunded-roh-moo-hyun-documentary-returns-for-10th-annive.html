Crowdfunded Roh Moo-hyun Documentary Returns for 10th Anniversary Amid Rising Political Polarization

November 11, 2026

A landmark 2016 independent documentary chronicling the late South Korean President Roh Moo-hyun's uphill battle against entrenched regionalism is returning to theaters in a restored director's cut after easily surpassing its crowdfunding target.

  • The project: Director Jeon In-hwan and producer Cho Eun-sung launched a campaign on Korean crowdfunding platform Tumblbug to fund a 10th-anniversary re-release of Moo-hyun: The Story of Two Cities, swiftly passing their initial 20 million won (about $15,000) goal and topping 30 million won (about $22,000).
  • What’s new: The director's cut adds unreleased archival footage, most notably scenes of Roh breaking down in tears behind the scenes during his doomed parliamentary run in Busan's conservative Buk-Gangseo district.
  • An indie phenomenon: First launched on a shoestring budget of just 3 million won (about $2,200) during the 2016 candlelight protests, the original release drew roughly 200,000 admissions—a box-office miracle in South Korea equivalent to millions of tickets for a commercial film.
  • The political spark: The filmmakers say the re-release is a direct response to intensifying online mockery and disparaging rhetoric against the late progressive leader by figures in the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition party.

A Battle Against Regionalism That Never Ended

At its core, Moo-hyun: The Story of Two Cities is an examination of South Korea's most persistent political fault line: regionalism. The film interweaves Roh's quixotic campaigns in conservative Yeongnam (the southeast) with a parallel race in liberal Honam (the southwest), showing candidates from opposing strongholds deliberately choosing unwinnable races to bridge geographic divides. A decade after the documentary first debuted, that geographic factionalism remains a defining feature of the country's legislative map.

The filmmakers had to overcome deep hurdles both then and now. In 2016, the documentary faced deliberate online review bombing, organized protests, and a chronic lack of commercial multiplex distribution. Yet word-of-mouth organizing and group ticket-buying turned it into a cultural touchstone. The newly restored version aims to push those themes further, using Roh's raw campaign moments to critique the transactional pragmatism that dominates contemporary electioneering.

Why Roh's Legacy Remains a Flashpoint

Fifteen years after his tragic death, Roh Moo-hyun remains the emotional bedrock of South Korea's progressive movement—and a primary target of conservative scorn. Online fringe spaces and partisan commentators have increasingly weaponized caricature and satire to erode his historical standing, prompting pushback from supporters who view his stubborn adherence to principle as an antidote to today's toxic political climate.

The rapid success of the crowdfunding drive—which aims to reach 50 million won (about $37,000) to secure nationwide distribution and host audience discussions—proves that grassroots civic mobilization remains potent. For many South Korean moviegoers, backing the re-release is less an exercise in political nostalgia than an active vote against political cynicism and factional division.
