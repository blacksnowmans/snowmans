South Korea’s Rebuilding Left: Why the Rebuilding Korea Party is Surging

November 02, 2026

The Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive minor party, has reached an 8.1% approval rating—its highest level in two years—signaling a sharp warning from core supporters of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) regarding the pace of current reforms.

  • Warning shot: Over 90% of those who identify as Rebuilding Korea Party supporters currently hold a negative view of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.
  • Internal friction: Public infighting between DPK members, particularly regarding responsibility for past election losses, is alienating the party's base.
  • Shift in strategy: Supporters are moving away from the "big tent" strategy of the DPK, opting for smaller, more ideologically aggressive parties to force faster reform.

A Warning to the Democratic Party

The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party is not a simple shift in political affiliation; it is a tactical expression of frustration. The DPK—the ruling party currently headed by President Lee—is struggling to break through the 40% approval ceiling. Supporters who feel the government is moving too slowly or acting with "lethargy" are using their support for the Rebuilding Korea Party as a blunt instrument to demand more aggressive action.

This is a paradox for the DPK. While the party often fears that bolder actions will trigger a conservative backlash, their failure to act is causing their own base to seek alternatives. As a result, the Rebuilding Korea Party is effectively acting as a "catfish"—an outside force meant to stir up and revitalize the lethargic main party.

Internal Infighting and Strategic Failures

The DPK's internal cohesion is fraying. Recently, DPK lawmaker Lee Hae-sik publicly criticized party leadership over merger negotiations with the Rebuilding Korea Party, even attempting to shift blame for past election losses onto former leadership. This "blame game" is viewed by many as a cowardly attempt to cover up personal strategic failures, particularly since Lee served as the head of the campaign committee during the party's defeat in the Seoul mayoral race.

Furthermore, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration remains shadowed by the lingering effects of the "demonization frame" employed by prosecutors and conservative media during his campaign. Despite the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, the persistent media hostility continues to suppress the current administration’s ability to broaden its support among moderates. If the DPK continues to prioritize internal finger-pointing over a cohesive, aggressive reform agenda, they risk losing the very foundation that brought them to power.
