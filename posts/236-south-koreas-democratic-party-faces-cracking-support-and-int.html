South Korea's Democratic Party Faces Cracking Support and Internal Turmoil

September 07, 2026

  • South Korea's ruling liberal Democratic Party is grappling with a mid-30s approval rating, driven by a sharp 14% drop in its stronghold of the Honam region (Gwangju, Jeolla provinces).
  • Critics and disgruntled supporters point to internal factionalism, loyalty-driven yes-men, and polarizing messaging on official party media channels as core drivers of voter alienation.
  • Lawmaker Kim Min-seok's controversial approach to key real estate legislation and an ongoing party list data-leak scandal have further deepened mistrust ahead of the upcoming local elections.

The Democratic Party, which anchors South Korea's liberal political camp under President Lee Jae-myung, is learning a harsh lesson: when a progressive party loses its moral high ground on procedural fairness, its core base does not argue—it simply walks away. A double-digit plunge in the party's traditional southern heartland is not a routine mid-term slump; it is a red alert signaling that the party's leadership has lost touch with the very people who put them in power.

Much of the self-inflicted damage stems from a leadership vacuum and a toxic culture of internal conformity. Lawmakers bite their tongues to stay in the party leader's good graces, while official communication channels often function more as echo chambers for factional purity tests than tools for public persuasion. Meanwhile, high-profile figures like Kim Min-seok are pushing polarizing stances on critical issues like real estate reform—ostensibly in the name of political expansion, but effectively fracturing the party's foundational unity before it can even secure its base.

Erosion of trust is rarely about just one poll number or a single policy dispute; it is about a growing perception that expediency has replaced principle. Whether dealing with alleged data leaks or controversial voting procedures at recent party conventions, the party's reflexive defensiveness only deepens voter cynicism. If the leadership wants to arrest its slide, it needs to stop looking for external scapegoats and summon the courage to face its own internal dysfunction head-on.
