South Korea’s New Investigative Agency Stirs Backlash Over Prosecutor Appointments

September 30, 2026

  • What happened:
    •  South Korea's planned launch of new investigative agencies—designed to strip prosecutors of direct investigative powers—is facing severe backlash over reports that veteran prosecutors are being tapped to head them.
  • Who is affected:
    •  The ruling administration under President Lee Jae-myung, whose core supporters feel betrayed by the apparent compromise with the very prosecutorial establishment the reforms aim to curb.
  • What it changes:
    •  Critics argue that filling top posts with career prosecutors turns the new bodies into mere replicas of the old prosecution service, risking the entire reform agenda.

South Korea’s push to overhaul its powerful criminal justice system is hitting a wall of internal contradiction. As the country prepares to launch the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency and a separate Prosecution Service—reforms meant to separate investigative and indicting powers—the reported nominees for top leadership roles are career prosecutors. Proponents of the appointments argue that former prosecutors are needed for a "smooth transition" and organizational stability. But for a government that came to power promising to dismantle entrenched privileges, handing the reins back to the legal establishment looks less like reform and more like capitulation.

The controversy deepened with reports that Lim Hyun, a former prosecutor linked to past political battles during the Yoon Suk Yeol administration, is being considered for a deputy role. Critics point out that the logic of needing prosecutors because "only they know how to investigate" echoes the historical justification used to spare collaborators after liberation from colonial rule. With the presidential office's senior civil affairs secretary escaping scrutiny for the vetting failures, supporters are left wondering whether the administration has the political will to see its own reforms through.
