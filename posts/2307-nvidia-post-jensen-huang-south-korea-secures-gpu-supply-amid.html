Nvidia Post-Jensen Huang: South Korea Secures GPU Supply Amid Global Turmoil

November 14, 2026

The sudden resignation of Nvidia founder Jensen Huang has sent shockwaves through the global semiconductor market, prompting the South Korean government to hold an emergency session to ensure the country’s critical artificial intelligence infrastructure remains on track.

  • The National AI Strategy Committee—a high-level government body coordinating South Korea's AI policy—confirmed that Nvidia’s commitment to supply 260,000 GPUs to the country remains intact despite the leadership vacuum.
  • Nvidia shares plummeted 11% following the news, dragging down the KOSPI (South Korea’s benchmark stock index) and highlighting the "Nvidia Risk" inherent in the global tech ecosystem’s over-reliance on a single firm.
  • Interim CEO Colette Kress faces an immediate crisis as competitors like AMD and Broadcom form new alliances, and regulators investigate allegations of chip diversions to China through Southeast Asian subsidiaries.
  • The leadership shakeup offers a mixed outlook for South Korean giants: while SK Hynix faces uncertainty due to its tight integration with Nvidia’s supply chain, Samsung Electronics is being viewed as a potential beneficiary of market efforts to diversify chip sources.

A Leadership Vacuum in the "Imperial" Tech Era

The departure of Jensen Huang is more than a standard corporate transition; it is a structural shock to the "imperial" founder-led model that has defined the AI era. With Huang’s exit mired in controversy—including allegations ranging from personal scandals to involvement in U.S.-China intelligence friction—the "Nvidia Risk" has become a tangible reality for investors. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, which has prioritized AI as a cornerstone of national growth, the emergency meeting of the National AI Strategy Committee reflects deep-seated anxiety over whether a leaderless Nvidia can maintain its aggressive R&D and supply commitments.

Internal instability at Nvidia’s Silicon Valley headquarters is already showing. Reports of key engineers being headhunted by OpenAI and Google suggest that the company's technical moat is under siege. While Colette Kress has stepped in as interim CEO, her background as a CFO suggests a defensive, finance-first posture that may struggle to replicate Huang’s visionary—and often high-risk—leadership. For South Korea, the confirmation of the 260,000 GPU delivery is a short-term relief, but the long-term sustainability of the AI data center rollout remains precarious as global supply balances shift.

The Shift Toward Software and Strategic Autonomy

As the hardware monopoly led by Nvidia shows cracks, the market is already rotating toward generative AI software and alternative architectures. The massive interest in the Anthropic IPO—which saw orders exceed supply by six times with a valuation nearing $1.75 trillion—indicates where the next wave of capital is flowing. This shift serves as a warning for South Korean manufacturers like SK Hynix, whose fortunes are currently tethered to Nvidia’s High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) demand. If the "Nvidia Era" cools, the memory market could face a significant correction.

Ultimately, this crisis underscores the danger of South Korea’s dependence on a single foreign entity for its national AI backbone. While the government has secured immediate supply promises, the volatility of the past week proves that technological sovereignty cannot be outsourced. The focus must now shift from merely securing shipments of Nvidia chips to accelerating domestic chip design and diversifying partnerships with the emerging AMD-Broadcom-Google alliance. In a world where tech leadership is increasingly entangled with national security and "spy-game" geopolitics, the Lee Jae-myung government must treat semiconductor supply chains as a matter of urgent national defense.
