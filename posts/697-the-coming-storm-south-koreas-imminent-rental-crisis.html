The Coming Storm: South Korea’s Imminent Rental Crisis

October 04, 2026

South Korea’s rental market is heading toward a critical "explosion" in 2027 and 2028, driven by structural failures that threaten to displace non-homeowners and send monthly rents soaring.

  • The Forecast: Experts warn of a severe rental shortage peaking in 2027, as current housing policies fail to address the fundamental supply-demand gap.
  • The "Monthly" Shift: High property taxes on owners are increasingly being passed on to tenants, forcing a transition from jeonse (a unique Korean system of large-sum deposit leases) to high-cost monthly rent.
  • Strategic Pivot: For those unable to afford homes in Seoul, the only viable survival strategy is relocating to the outskirts of Gyeonggi or Incheon, specifically near major transport hubs like the new GTX (Great Train Express) high-speed rail lines.
  • Policy Failure: The current government's focus on "non-apartment" supply (villas and studio-type housing) is being ignored by real-world buyers who demand apartment-style living, further worsening the supply crunch.

The End of Affordable Housing in Seoul

Real estate analyst Kim Hak-ryul, widely followed for his market insights, argues that the era of hoping for a market correction in central Seoul is over. With Seoul’s jeonse inventory plummeting—down 13% in just the last year—the market has reached a point where non-homeowners must act now. For those priced out of the capital, the focus must shift to peripheral regions connected to the city via high-speed transit. This isn't merely investment advice; it is a defensive move to secure long-term residential stability.

The core of the problem lies in the government's antagonistic approach toward multi-homeowners. By labeling them as the primary cause of housing instability and imposing heavy taxes, the government has inadvertently destroyed the private rental supply. Owners of high-end properties, faced with annual comprehensive real estate taxes exceeding $30,000 (about 40 million KRW), are choosing to switch to high-cost monthly rentals to cover their tax burdens. This "tax-to-rent" transfer is creating a new, expensive reality that is rapidly spreading from the affluent Gangnam district to the rest of the capital region.

Policy Disconnect and the "Gap" Reality

Government interventions are currently hitting a wall of irony. Heavy loan regulations designed to curb speculation are instead trapping low-income buyers in the "lower-tier" market, while the ultra-wealthy in high-tier areas remain largely unaffected by interest rate hikes. This widening "parallel" between the rich and the rest is creating a market where the gap between prime locations and the outskirts grows increasingly insurmountable.

The government's persistent attempts to boost supply through "non-apartment" housing—such as small villas or office-style living spaces—are failing because they do not align with what Korean families actually want. As long as policy ignores the market's preference for apartments and continues to squeeze the private rental sector, the coming rental crisis will remain an inevitable outcome of "man-made" policy failures rather than an unavoidable natural disaster.

For the average non-homeowner, the path forward is narrow. The era of waiting for policy to "fix" the market has passed. Survival now depends on recognizing that the housing market is a cold, rational system that rewards those who adapt to reality—by prioritizing transit-linked areas and accepting the shift in market dynamics—rather than those waiting for government-subsidized solutions that have yet to materialize.
