South Korea’s Judiciary Faces Backlash Over Missing Attendance Records

September 30, 2026

South Korea’s judiciary is under fire after revelations that judges and court officials operate without a formal attendance tracking system, a significant departure from the strict time-keeping standards applied to the rest of the nation's workforce.

  • The Gap: While private-sector employees and administrative civil servants use digital systems to track their hours to the minute, the judicial branch lacks any such infrastructure.
  • The "Badge" Illusion: Staff use ID badges to enter court buildings, but these are for security access only and do not log start or end times.
  • The Accusation: Critics argue that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the judicial leadership are maintaining a "system of convenience" that borders on dereliction of duty, exempting themselves from the standards they enforce on the public.
  • The Evidence: Skeptics point out that official vehicles assigned to high-ranking judges already create an electronic log of entry and exit times, suggesting that the data exists but is not being used for accountability.

A Culture of Privilege?

The revelation has sparked public outrage, particularly because South Korea is a global leader in digital infrastructure. For a branch of government tasked with upholding the law, the lack of a basic time-tracking system—often dismissed by officials as an administrative technicality—is being viewed as a systemic failure. Critics argue that this is not a technological limitation, but a deliberate choice to avoid the scrutiny applied to the common citizen.

Under the State Public Officials Act, court staff are technically required to adhere to standard 9-to-6 working hours. By failing to track these hours, the judiciary is effectively operating in a "blind spot," shielded from the accountability that defines the lives of ordinary workers. This has fueled accusations of naeronambul—a Korean term for "if I do it, it’s romance; if others do it, it’s an affair"—highlighting the perceived double standard where the judiciary mandates strict compliance for the public while ignoring it for themselves.

The Question of Accountability

The controversy centers on whether the judicial leadership is intentionally shielding its workforce from transparency. Even if formal time clocks were absent, the existence of digital logs for government-issued vehicles means that the judiciary is already in possession of data that could verify the movements of its highest officials. Failure to utilize or acknowledge these logs raises serious questions about the integrity of internal records.

For the judiciary to regain public trust, it must move beyond claims that the system is simply "not built." In a society where every other worker is held accountable by the clock, the court’s continued silence on this issue risks undermining its moral authority. If the institution that serves as the final arbiter of justice cannot adhere to the basic rules of professional conduct, it invites deep skepticism regarding its ability to judge the rest of society fairly.
