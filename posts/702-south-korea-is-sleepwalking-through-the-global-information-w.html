South Korea Is Sleepwalking Through the Global Information War

October 04, 2026

  • What happened: While countries like the US and France implement strict bans on social media and generative AI for minors to counter foreign cognitive warfare, South Korea's political class remains dangerously indifferent.
  • What it changes: Domestic political apathy and failure to address coordinated online manipulation—such as gender and generational polarization exposed in research on portal comments—leave the country vulnerable to foreign influence operations.
  • Who is affected: South Korean internet users, whose digital environments are increasingly weaponized by bad actors while national security and intelligence agencies fail to adapt.

Global policymakers are waking up to the reality of cognitive warfare, where foreign adversaries and digital platforms directly target the public's minds. From New York City temporarily blocking YouTube in schools to European nations tightening defenses against cyber-attacks and foreign interference, governments are treating information security as national security. Yet, in South Korea, mainstream political parties and commentators treat these developments as mere punchlines, dismissing systematic online manipulation as conspiracy theories.

Local research, including studies by the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), has already shown how coordinated, anonymous accounts manipulate domestic platforms like Naver to inflame gender, generational, and regional divides. Despite these clear warnings, South Korea's major political forces remain fixated on traditional retail politics and election cycles. Notably, while international experts gather in Lithuania this week to strategize against foreign influence operations and hybrid threats, only three South Korean specialists are reportedly in attendance.

Building a Grassroots Defense When Politics Fails

After seven years of watching successive administrations—from the conservative People Power Party to liberal leadership—neglect intelligence reform and fail to counter modern cognitive threats, relying on the political establishment is a dead end. South Korea's major liberal and conservative factions are either entirely unequipped or too self-absorbed to build the technological infrastructure required to fight back against sophisticated information warfare.

Because the state apparatus and intelligence agencies continue to drop the ball, civil society must take the initiative. Observers and digital researchers are now pushing to establish independent projects like "Cyber Craft" and AI-driven information observatories. Without a decentralized, tech-literate network capable of monitoring, analyzing, and countering disinformation, South Korea risks becoming an open playground for foreign and domestic perception manipulation.
