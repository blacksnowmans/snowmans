Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Power Struggle: The Fall of Yong Hye-in

September 13, 2026

The abrupt resignation of Gender Equality and Family Minister nominee Yong Hye-in has exposed deep, festering fractures within South Korea's ruling Democratic Party, highlighting a dangerous shift toward vertical party control under pro-Seok factions.

    Yong Hye-in, a high-profile cabinet nominee, resigned under intense pressure from within the ruling party despite President Lee Jae-myung initially urging patience.
  • The move reflects a dominant pro-Seok faction tightening its grip on policy and personnel decisions, sidelining reform-minded voices and youth support.
  • Public polling showed over 60% disapproval for Yong, driven by controversy over holding dual roles as a proportional representative and a cabinet minister.
  • President Lee's recent statements cautioning against "over-speeding" in reforms have further alienated progressives who accuse the administration of diluting core promises.

The Pro-Seok Hegemony and Internal Purges

The forced exit of Yong Hye-in is not merely an isolated personnel failure; it is a clear symptom of a power struggle within the Democratic Party. While President Lee Jae-myung stated he would wait for the confirmation hearings, the party's dominant faction—often referred to as the pro-Seok group—orchestrated a relentless campaign demanding her withdrawal. Leadership figures openly intervened, making it clear that defending her amid plunging youth approval ratings and sliding poll numbers was politically untenable. This episode reveals a troubling dynamic: executive power and legislative control are increasingly concentrated in the hands of a faction willing to discipline anyone straying from their line.

Critics argue that this behavior stems from a traditional progressive political playbook of manufacturing internal divisions to secure power. Rather than targeting external opponents, the ruling faction has increasingly turned its crosshairs inward, branding reform-minded figures and critics—such as prominent commentator Rhyu Si-min and lawmaker Chung Cheong-rae—as disloyal elements. By labeling constructive internal dissent as "anti-Lee" activity, the party leadership risks alienating its core base and silencing the very voices that brought the administration to power.

President Lee's Caution Versus Progressive Frustration

Amid the chaos, President Lee's recent social media endorsements of caution have sparked intense debate. Embracing views that warn against the perils of over-zealous reforms and internal undermining, Lee emphasized that reform must be methodical, procedural, and designed to minimize resistance. While this moderate approach is understandable for a sitting president seeking stability against entrenched conservative institutions, it has deeply disappointed progressives who voted for sweeping systemic change.

For a movement built on the promise of uprooting deep-seated corruption in the judiciary, media, and bureaucracy, treating radical reform as a dangerous "revolution" sounds dangerously like compromise. As the party continues to purge its own ranks under the banner of strategic unity, it risks replacing genuine reform with a hollow maintenance of the status quo. If the administration continues to treat critical feedback from its own supporters as noise rather than a mandate, it faces a growing risk of voter cynicism and a disillusioned base.
