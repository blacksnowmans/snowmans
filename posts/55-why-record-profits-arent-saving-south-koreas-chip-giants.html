Why Record Profits Aren't Saving South Korea's Chip Giants

September 02, 2026

    Record earnings: South Korean memory giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are posting massive profits—with SK Hynix logging a staggering 557% jump in operating profit—yet their stock prices remain stuck in the red.
  • Macro headwinds: Surging oil prices, Middle East geopolitical tensions, and rising bond yields (with US 10-year yields topping 4.8%) are draining market liquidity and fueling investor anxiety.
  • The sentiment trap: The market is gripped by a fear that the current semiconductor cycle is peaking, causing investors to ignore strong fundamentals.
  • Who is affected: Retail investors in South Korea watching their portfolios turn blue while corporate earnings reports flash green.

The Clash Between Fundamentals and Macro Noise

There is a jarring disconnect in the South Korean stock market right now. Every quarter brings headlines of historic earnings for tech powerhouses like Samsung Electronics (the world's top memory chip maker) and SK Hynix (a major AI chip supplier). Yet, everyday investors checking their accounts see little to celebrate. The issue is no longer just about whether a company can turn a profit; it is about whether solid business fundamentals can survive a tidal wave of macroeconomic turbulence.

Global factors are hijacking local sentiment. Crude oil prices hovering near $90 a barrel have revived inflation fears, while climbing global interest rates are making risk-on assets like equities far less attractive. Meanwhile, persistent geopolitical risks—highlighted by recent attacks on oil tankers in the region—are freezing investment appetite. Tech stocks are suffering not because their underlying business is weak, but because the broader economic environment is choking off market liquidity and breeding widespread caution.

Riding Out the AI Infrastructure Cycle

Despite the stock market stagnation, the underlying demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure remains intact. Strong performances from global tech suppliers like Dell Technologies show that capital expenditure on data centers and AI server racks is roaring ahead. The physical buildout of the AI era has not stopped; money is still flowing heavily into the foundational layers of technology.

In our view, the current slump is a classic case of market psychology outrunning reality. Investors are so anxious about when the current upcycle will end that they are punishing stocks even as the money rolls in. The brutal divergence between stellar corporate earnings and depressed share prices will likely persist until macroeconomic panic settles and interest rates find a stable footing. Until then, investors in South Korea's chip champions face a test of patience—separating short-term market noise from the undeniable, long-term wave of AI infrastructure demand.
