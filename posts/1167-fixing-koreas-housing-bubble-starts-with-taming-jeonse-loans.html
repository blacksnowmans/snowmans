Fixing Korea's Housing Bubble Starts with Taming Jeonse Loans

October 15, 2026

Unchecked expansion of subsidized loans for jeonse—South Korea’s distinctive lease system where tenants hand over large lump-sum deposits instead of monthly rent—has functioned as a primary engine of runaway home prices, prompting calls for a phased 10-year lending cap and mandatory escrow accounts to dismantle speculative property rings.

  • The 10-year glide path: A proposed gradual policy roadmap would systematically scale down jeonse loan availability to a ceiling of 300 million won (roughly $225,000) over a decade, preventing sudden market paralysis.
  • Targeting "gap investment": Restricting cheap deposit credit directly curbs tenant borrowing power, putting downward pressure on deposit sizes and forcing overextended speculators—who buy apartments using tenants' deposits as interest-free leverage—to liquidate holdings.
  • Mandatory escrow safeguards: Where deposits or loans exceed 60% to 70% of a home’s value, excess funds would be legally locked in third-party escrow accounts so landlords cannot gamble tenant money on additional properties.
  • Ending public bailouts: The reform seeks to permanently discard the moral hazard seen during the 2022–2023 property downturn, when the state intervened with emergency "return loans" to rescue insolvent landlords trapped by falling deposit prices.

Defusing an Informal Margin-Lending Machine

Originally promoted as middle-class housing welfare, South Korea's state-backed jeonse loan framework evolved into an informal margin engine for speculative real estate. Because banks handed out deposit loans with minimal friction, landlords could demand ever-higher sums from incoming tenants. Speculators routinely practiced "gap investment," stacking multiple apartment purchases with virtually zero personal capital by relying on these bloated deposits to cover purchase costs.

This dynamic unraveled during the 2022–2023 market contraction into a nationwide "reverse jeonse" panic, where plunging prices left landlords incapable of returning deposits when contracts expired. Rather than letting the market clear out reckless leverage, authorities stepped in with publicly backed concessionary loans to help landlords refund tenants. Bailing out speculative failures with state liquidity simply shifted private debt risk onto future taxpayers while keeping systemic asset bubbles intact.

The Road to a Managed Soft Landing

A sudden credit freeze would trigger mass evictions and systemic default, which is why a multi-year exit strategy is indispensable. A 10-year timeline, paired with two-to-four-year grandfathering protections for current leaseholders, provides market participants predictable milestones to de-lever. Crucially, enforcing escrow accounts on high-ratio deposits attacks the root vice: the widespread delusion among landlords that a tenant’s refundable deposit represents private pocket money rather than a strict liability.

The primary hurdle remains political fear. Curbing popular credit lines inevitably provokes vocal backlash from both sitting tenants and asset owners, which has historically caused politicians to punt the issue down the road. But without dismantling the debt-fueled leasehold spiral, broader housing affordability pledges under the Lee Jae-myung administration will remain hostage to the very leverage fueling the fire.
