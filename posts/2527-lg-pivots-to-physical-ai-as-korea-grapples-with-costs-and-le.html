LG Pivots to "Physical AI" as Korea Grapples with Costs and Legal Gaps

November 20, 2026

Major South Korean industrial players and local governments are shifting their focus from generative AI to "Physical AI"—the integration of artificial intelligence into hardware like robots, construction machinery, and agricultural equipment.

  • LG Group promoted 132 executives in a strategic reshuffle designed to move AI from the research lab to the commercial market, focusing on robotics and data centers.
  • Despite high interest, five major listed "Physical AI" companies in Korea reported financial losses, highlighting a gap between technological hype and profitability.
  • Jeonbuk Special Self-Governing Province (North Jeolla) is seeking approximately $44 million (61.7 billion KRW) in state funding to pilot AI in the livestock and farming sectors.
  • The National Police Agency warned of a "legal vacuum," noting that while patrol robots are scheduled for deployment next year, there is no legislation governing their authority or liability.

LG’s Hard Shift to Hardware Intelligence

On November 19, LG Group, one of South Korea’s largest conglomerates, announced a massive leadership reshuffle. While keeping most veteran CEOs in place to ensure stability under the Lee Jae-myung administration’s economic climate, the group promoted 132 younger technical leaders. The goal is to accelerate the commercialization of "Physical AI"—technology that allows machines to perceive and interact with the real world rather than just processing text or images.

The reshuffle heavily favored LG Innotek, the group’s electronic component arm, and LG AI Research. LG Innotek is pivoting toward advanced semiconductor substrates required for high-performance AI sensors, while the research arm is tasked with adapting its "EXAONE" AI model for industrial robotics. This move suggests LG is finished with "demonstrations" and is now focused on the high-margin business of selling autonomous systems to factories and warehouses.

The Profitability Gap and Global Competition

While the corporate vision is grand, the current market reality is more sober. Five major listed Korean firms specializing in Physical AI recently reported losses. This includes Clobot, a prominent robotics software company that saw revenue grow but failed to turn a profit due to high implementation costs. Investors are learning that "teaching" a robot to navigate a messy, real-world factory floor is significantly more expensive than running a chatbot on a server.

However, capital continues to flow into the sector globally. The briefing noted that India’s HumynLabs recently secured about $47 million (63 billion KRW) in Series A funding to build data infrastructure for physical AI. In Korea, smaller firms like Sagak are partnering with established manufacturers to bridge the gap between AI software and industrial hardware. The consensus among market analysts is that the winners will not be those with the best demos, but those who can control the "physical data" generated by machines in the field.

Regional Ambitions and Regulatory Hurdles

Outside of Seoul, the push for AI is becoming a central pillar of regional development. Jeonbuk Province has requested the Democratic Party of Korea-led government to allocate $44 million for AI livestock centers. The province aims to use robots to solve chronic labor shortages in agriculture. Similarly, UNIST (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology) announced plans to train 2,000 specialists in "Physical AI" to support the heavy industries of the country’s southeast.

Yet, technology is outstripping the law. The National Police Agency pointed out that patrol robots are ready to hit the streets in 2025, but there are no laws defining who is responsible if a robot causes an accident or violates privacy. This legal uncertainty remains the biggest bottleneck for the 112 Korean companies currently preparing for large-scale government pilot projects next year.

"The transition from screen-based AI to physical machines is no longer a research project; it is now a matter of industrial survival and legislative urgency."
