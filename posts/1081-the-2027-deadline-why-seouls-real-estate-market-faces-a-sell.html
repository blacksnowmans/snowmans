The 2027 Deadline: Why Seoul's Real Estate Market Faces a Sell-Off

October 13, 2026

South Korea’s property market is bracing for a significant shift as government tax reforms remove long-standing incentives for investors, creating a "sell-off" deadline by the end of 2027.

  • Policy Rollback: The government is phasing out tax loopholes—such as the "Mutually Beneficial Landlord" program—that previously allowed speculative investors to bypass capital gains taxes.
  • Tightening Timelines: The grace period for temporary two-home owners to sell their original property is shrinking back from three years to two, forcing faster turnover.
  • Expiration of Benefits: Decades-old "Rental Housing Business" tax exemptions are set to vanish after December 31, 2027, pressuring landlords to offload assets to avoid heavy taxation.
  • Market Polarization: While high-end properties are seeing a cooldown, lower-end markets are experiencing severe rental price spikes, highlighting a deepening urban housing crisis.

The End of Tax-Sheltered Speculation

For years, Seoul’s high-end property market was sustained by a complex web of tax breaks and incentives. Many of these policies were established during the administration of the now-imprisoned former leader, Yoon Suk-yeol. Under his tenure, measures like the "Mutually Beneficial Landlord" system and extended disposal periods for two-home owners acted as safety nets for "gap investors"—people buying property with the intention of covering the cost through high rental deposits.

The current administration, led by President Lee Jae-myung, is systematically dismantling these frameworks. By re-tightening the rules, the government is essentially forcing a market correction. Investors who relied on these "honey pots" to avoid capital gains tax are now facing a reality where holding onto multiple high-value properties becomes financially unsustainable.

The 2027 "Liquidation" Pressure

The core of this impending shift is the sunsetting of the "Rental Housing Business" (often referred to as Ju-im-sa) incentives. Initiated years ago, these programs offered generous tax exemptions to owners who registered their units as long-term rentals. However, as these mandatory rental periods expire, owners are realizing that the generous tax shields will evaporate by December 31, 2027.

For many property owners, the math no longer adds up. Between high interest rates and the looming loss of capital gains exemptions, holding onto these assets is becoming a liability rather than an investment. We are likely to see a surge of inventory hitting the market as 2027 approaches.

While some argue that this is an overly aggressive move, it is arguably a return to normalcy. Previous administrations often used real estate "band-aid" policies to prop up the market during downturns. The Lee Jae-myung government’s current approach suggests a pivot toward reducing systemic reliance on real estate speculation. For the average buyer, this may finally offer some breathing room, but for those heavily leveraged in the high-end market, the next few years represent a high-stakes exit race.
