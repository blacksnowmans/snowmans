Crisis in Seoul: Why Lee Jae-myung’s Administration is Losing its Grip

September 11, 2026

The Lee Jae-myung administration, which took power with a mandate for systemic reform, is facing a deepening governance crisis as it struggles to decouple its political survival from the nation’s increasingly fragile economy.

  • Political-Economic Entanglement: The administration’s reliance on short-term political fixes rather than structural reform has eroded market confidence, pushing the won-dollar exchange rate to volatile highs (currently over 1,500 KRW).
  • The Trust Deficit: With approval ratings hovering in the low 40% range, the president is facing alienation from his core supporters, who view the current trajectory as a betrayal of his original reformist promise.
  • Internal Fractures: Infighting within the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal opposition party) and a drift toward "political engineering" have paralyzed the government’s ability to execute its core agenda, including promised prosecutorial reforms.

The Failure of "Reform"

The central paradox of the current administration is its failure to reconcile its self-defined identity as a "People’s Sovereignty Government" with the realities of governing. Lee, a former provincial governor and presidential candidate, campaigned on dismantling entrenched privileges and addressing the grievances of the marginalized. However, critics argue that the government mistook a temporary semiconductor-led market rally for its own success, ultimately squandering the "golden time" for genuine structural reform.

As the economic reality worsens, the administration has pivoted to political defensive maneuvers. By sidelining critical voices—including key party figures who once supported him—and retreating from the painful but necessary work of reform, the president has created a feedback loop of distrust. In Korea, this is often described through the idiom "Mushinbulrip" (無信不立)—"without trust, nothing can stand."

Stuck in a Cycle of Political Engineering

The current instability is exacerbated by the rise of what some observers call the "Myung-Sung Alliance," a term describing a controversial political circle aimed at securing the president’s position rather than serving the public interest. This inward-looking focus has alienated the administration's base, turning once-passionate supporters into cynical observers. When the president publicly framed his declining popularity as a failure to "cater to the left," he insulted the very people who supported him not for ideological rewards, but for a belief in his commitment to justice.

The administration’s refusal to tolerate "bitter medicine" from within its own ranks signals a drift toward isolation. As the president himself has reportedly acknowledged in private, the path forward is clouded by deep uncertainty. If the government continues to prioritize political survival over the difficult, "skin-peeling" work of true reform, it risks becoming yet another chapter in the cycle of political failure that has haunted South Korean presidencies in the post-democratization era.
