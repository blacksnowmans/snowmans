South Korea’s Supreme Court Splits Over Former Prime Minister’s Role in 2024 Martial Law

November 20, 2026

In a divided en banc ruling, South Korea’s Supreme Court majority moved to limit insurrection charges against former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo over his role in a pre-declaration Cabinet meeting, prompting a sharp dissent from Justice Oh Gyeong-mi that has intensified debate over official accountability.

  • The core dispute: The court majority concluded prosecutors failed to prove Han intended to subvert the Constitution when he suggested and convened a Cabinet meeting prior to Yoon Suk Yeol’s December 3, 2024 emergency martial law declaration, while Justice Oh argued that giving procedural cover to an unlawful "self-coup" amounted to active participation in insurrection.
  • The timeline at issue: Records cited in the dissent state that Han spent over an hour securing a Cabinet quorum—calling Agriculture Minister Song Mi-ryeong at 9:37 p.m. without warning her of martial law—before a two-minute session preceded the declaration, after which he sought post-hoc signatures from ministers.
  • Diverging court views: Lower divisions of the Seoul Central District Court previously issued guilty verdicts on insurrection-related charges involving Yoon, Han, and former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min, though judges differed on whether the episode reflected premeditated long-term dictatorship or an impulsive breakdown of restraint.
  • Who is affected: The Supreme Court’s standard directly shapes criminal liability for South Korea’s top appointed officials and tests where routine bureaucratic compliance ends and constitutional subversion begins.

Procedure Versus Purpose in the Courtroom

Han’s defense attorneys argued that the former prime minister—who also served as vice chair of the Cabinet—was simply carrying out statutory procedures under instructions from Yoon Suk Yeol, comparable to routine administrative work by a career civil servant. By contrast, Justice Oh’s dissent and Korean legal commentators cited in local reporting contend that a prime minister bears a distinct constitutional duty to restrain unlawful executive action rather than facilitate it.

Timeline records highlighted in the dissent indicate Han entered the executive office in Seoul’s Yongsan district twice to meet with Yoon and former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun, reporting at 9:29 p.m. that the Cabinet quorum was met. After the 10:16 p.m. Cabinet meeting ended in two minutes without substantive debate, Han allegedly stayed behind for roughly 16 minutes with Lee Sang-min to discuss contingency documents involving power and water cutoffs at major institutions. To Justice Oh, isolating the pre-declaration meeting from this broader sequence ignores how formal procedures can be used to legitimize an insurrectionary act.

The Broader Stakes for Constitutional Accountability

The split inside South Korea’s highest court reflects an enduring legal tension between strict statutory proof of criminal intent and a broader defense of democratic institutions. Requiring clear evidence that an official actively aimed to overthrow the constitutional order protects bureaucrats from sweeping guilt by association, explaining the majority’s caution regarding Han’s procedural steps.

At the same time, lower-court rulings in Seoul and comparative cases from the United States, Germany, and Brazil underscore that modern democratic crises rarely arrive as overt military coups from outside the state. Instead, they often operate through existing legal machinery. Whether South Korea’s judiciary treats procedural formalities as a legal shield for senior officials or as a potential tool of executive abuse will shape how future governments navigate constitutional emergencies.
