The Betrayal of Reform: How the Current Administration Is Preserving Prosecutor Power

October 12, 2026

More than a year into the administration of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung, critics and progressives are expressing alarm as key justice ministry roles and investigative bodies remain dominated by prosecutors—reversing long-promised reforms and outdoing the excesses of the imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol.

  • Prosecutor Monopoly in the Ministry of Justice:
    •  Fifty-six active prosecutors currently hold concurrent, senior positions inside the Ministry of Justice, including key bureau chief roles and even the human rights and inspection offices.
  • Weakening the Corruption Investigation Office (CIO):
    •  Ruling-party lawmakers are pushing to strip the CIO of its authority to investigate politicians, threatening to transfer those powers to a newly proposed investigative agency.
  • Abandoning Campaign Promises:
    •  Rather than dismantling the entrenched political-prosecutor cartel, the administration has staffed key oversight posts with figures criticized as pliant insiders.
  • Eclipsing Past Legacies:
    •  Observers note a persistent push by the current ruling camp to distance itself from the previous Moon Jae-in administration's reform efforts, often at the cost of progressive principles.

The Irony of Prosecutor-Run Oversight

The core grievance voiced by critics is simple: an administration that came to power promising to dismantle political prosecution has instead absorbed it. During the tenure of the jailed former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, the high walls of prosecutor resistance were somewhat expected. Today, however, with the reformist camp holding the presidency, those walls should have crumbled. Instead, the Ministry of Justice has transformed into a sanctuary for prosecutors, with 56 active prosecutors occupying key posts.

Most tellingly, even the ministry's Human Rights Bureau—which during the Yoon Suk-yeol administration was at least nominally led by civilian experts or lawyers—has been overrun by prosecutors. When those tasked with auditing and reforming a corrupt institution are placed in charge of it, internal self-correction becomes impossible. Critics argue that the administration has chosen to make peace with the establishment rather than fight it, settling into a comfortable partnership with the very power structures it once vowed to dismantle.

Legislative Engineering and the Fear of Accountability

Beyond the Ministry of Justice, the ruling Democratic Party faces sharp criticism for attempting to dilute the Corruption Investigation Office (CIO). Proposals floated by pro-Lee Jae-myung lawmakers aim to shift investigations of politicians away from the CIO—a watchdog originally established precisely to keep powerful officials in check—and move them to a newly proposed investigative body.

This legislative maneuver reads to many as a preemptive insurance policy. By reshaping oversight bodies and installing pliable chiefs, the administration risks replicating the worst traits of past governments under a different banner. For a voter base that flooded the streets demanding systemic reform, watching a progressive government recreate elite privilege structures from within feels less like governance and more like betrayal.
