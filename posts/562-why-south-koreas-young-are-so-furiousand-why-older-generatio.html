Why South Korea's Young Are So Furious—And Why Older Generations Don't Get It

September 10, 2026

  • The boiling point: A viral discussion on a popular Korean current-affairs show uses recent films to diagnose an exhausted, angry society shaped by brutal commutes and structural despair.
  • The generation gap: Young Koreans face soaring living costs, bleak job prospects, and widespread housing fraud—like rental scams (*jeonse* fraud)—while older generations dismiss their struggles by boasting about past hardships.
  • Pop-culture escape: Hit movies like the shark thriller Deep Water and the romance-thriller The Drama offer audiences a chance to process hidden pasts, institutional betrayal, and the urge to flee harsh realities.
  • The takeaway: Dismissing youth frustration as whining only deepens societal fractures; bridging the gap requires older generations to acknowledge that today's survival looks fundamentally different from the past.

The Roots of Youth Rage: From Hellish Commutes to Structural Despair

Ask anyone commuting into Seoul from Gimpo—a satellite city notorious for its agonizingly crowded subway lines—and they will tell you that South Korea's daily life starts with a baseline of raw irritation. This exhaustion is not just about bad traffic; it is a symptom of a deeper societal malaise. When commentators on a prominent Korean media broadcast recently dissected this anger, they pointed straight to a widening chasm between generations. For young adults, particularly those born in the late 1990s, the economic landscape feels rigged. They carry heavy psychological and financial burdens, navigating a world where traditional milestones like stable housing and secure employment have drifted out of reach.

The friction is compounded by older generations who lean on the lazy defense of "we had it harder." But comparing the material poverty of South Korea's post-IMF crisis decades to today's psychological gridlock—where young people do everything right yet cannot move forward—misses the point entirely. When films portray young protagonists lashing out in sheer frustration, that anger is less a personal character flaw and more a structural indictment of an unforgiving economy.

Escapism and the Burden of the Past

To cope with the pressure, audiences are turning to genre cinema that cuts straight to raw survival. Renny Harlin’s shark thriller Deep Water offers a pure, unfiltered adrenaline rush, giving stressed-out viewers a safe, chaotic space to forget real-world anxieties. Meanwhile, darker psychological fare like The Drama forces characters—and viewers—to confront hidden pasts and buried secrets just as they reach society's traditional milestones, like marriage. These cinematic collisions mirror real-life dilemmas: how much of our loved ones' dark histories, or our own embarrassing youth, can we truly forgive and accept?

Ultimately, these cultural touchstones suggest that South Koreans of all ages are simply swimming to avoid being eaten by their own economic waters. If older adults want to heal the social fabric, the first step is dropping the arrogance of comparative suffering and recognizing why the younger generation feels like they have nothing left to lose.
