President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Strategy Sparks Internal Party Conflict

October 03, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s recent habit of amplifying critical social media posts through his personal account has triggered a wave of "digital purges" within the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal opposition party.

  • What happened: The President is frequently "retweeting" aggressive posts targeting political rivals and dissenting party members, a phenomenon supporters have dubbed "Meme-Jae-myung."
  • What it changes: The President's online behavior is signaling to his hardline base—the so-called "New Lee Jae-myung" faction—which individuals or groups are currently out of favor.
  • Who is affected: Key party figures like former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae, Rep. Kim Yong-min, and Rep. Park Eun-jung have become targets of organized digital harassment and petitions demanding their expulsion from the party.
  • The underlying issue: Critics argue the President is framing legitimate internal policy disagreements and external criticism as "sabotage of state affairs" or profit-driven smear campaigns.

The Rise of "Retweet Politics"

President Lee has openly defied traditional norms of presidential restraint, embracing a combative online presence. By selectively boosting posts that mock and attack his own party colleagues—such as labeling certain internal critics as "insincere" or "saboteurs"—Lee is effectively deputizing his base to enforce ideological purity. This creates a feedback loop: the President validates the anger of his supporters, who then intensify their attacks on perceived enemies of the "reform" agenda.

The aggression has extended to personal lives, most notably in the case of Rep. Park Eun-jung. Supporters of the President have targeted her husband, prosecutor Lee Jong-geun, with claims that he is a "pro-Yoon" (pro-President Yoon Suk-yeol) figure. Despite evidence that Lee Jong-geun actually faced demotion during the previous administration for his reformist leanings, the false narrative persists, fueled by online campaigns that resemble the tactics of far-right "Taegukgi" groups of the past.

Shifting Power to the Party Base

This atmosphere of hostility poses a significant threat to the DPK’s internal health. By labeling any dissenter as a self-interested actor rather than a legitimate critic, the presidential office is stifling the internal checks and balances necessary for a functional party. Observers and reform-minded voices, including veteran commentator Rhyu Si-min, have suggested that the only way to counteract this factional domination is to mobilize the party's "dues-paying members" (3 million strong) to reclaim the decision-making process from the radical minority of influencers and online activists.

Ultimately, the President’s social media usage reveals a binary worldview where one is either a loyal follower or an enemy of the state. For the Democratic Party, the challenge remains clear: to move beyond the toxic dependency on a single leader’s online approval and return to the institutional, evidence-based politics that once defined its platform. Unless the party can insulate itself from this "politics of emotion," the goal of meaningful structural reform—such as the establishment of new investigative agencies—risks being overshadowed by internal polarization.
