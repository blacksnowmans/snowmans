Why South Korea's Stock Market Plunges Despite Record Profits

October 12, 2026

  • What happened: Despite companies posting record earnings, the Kospi index dropped 3.65% to 6,384 as foreign investors dumped over $1.4 billion (2 trillion won) in shares, with tech giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix plunging over 4%.
  • What it changes: Daily trading volume has evaporated by 70% from its May peak, reducing the market to a volatile playground for foreign short-term trading and draining retail investor confidence.
  • Who is affected: South Korean retail investors—known locally as "ants"—face severe losses from a predatory corporate governance structure that dilutes minority shares while controlling shareholders cash out.

The Anatomy of Korea Discount and Foreign Flight

South Korea’s capital market is trapped in a destructive paradox. Even as industrial export numbers hit historic highs, the pipeline connecting those earnings to the stock market is entirely broken. Foreign investors and global financial media like Bloomberg are increasingly writing off the massive $4.3 trillion market, pointing to low valuations, rigid exchange rules, and an over-reliance on a single memory chip sector. When foreign capital pulls back even slightly, the entire market collapses under its own weight.

The deeper rot lies in corporate governance. South Korean markets have long tolerated a predatory setup where a controlling shareholder sells their stake at a massive premium, only for the new buyer to issue heavily discounted new shares to themselves via third-party allotments. Retail investors suffer a double blow: their initial holdings are devalued by the acquisition, and then their stakes are aggressively diluted. High-profile cases involving companies like Lotte Rental and Kakao Games have shown how legal loopholes allow controlling families to line their pockets at the expense of everyday shareholders.

Fixing the Broken System: Law, Leadership, and Reform

The current administration and lawmakers face mounting pressure to deliver structural reform rather than empty slogans. Critics point out that while Tokyo overhauled the Tokyo Stock Exchange to win back foreign trust through aggressive corporate governance reform, South Korea remains stuck in the compliance lagging phase. Restoring credibility requires decisive legislative action—such as revising commercial laws to expand a director's fiduciary duty beyond the company itself to include all shareholders, and introducing mandatory tender offer rules to protect minority investors from predatory buyouts.

Ultimately, a stock market cannot survive on speculation and hollow hype alone. If South Korea wants to permanently shed the "Korea Discount," policymakers must dismantle the cozy cartels of corporate control and enforce fair play. Without legal protections that guarantee ordinary investors a fair share of corporate growth, the market will remain little more than a house of cards.
