Putin Shatters Post-Summit Calm with Largest Airstrike on Ukraine to Date

November 21, 2026

Vladimir Putin has launched a massive wave of retaliatory airstrikes across Ukraine, targeting three critical bridges in Kyiv and violating NATO airspace just hours after a trilateral summit between the U.S., China, and Russia.

  • Kyiv Bridges Targeted: Russian forces struck three primary transport arteries in the capital, severely crippling civilian movement and military logistics.
  • NATO Airspace Breach: A missile entered Polish territory during the barrage, prompting Poland to scramble fighter jets and placing the alliance on high alert.
  • Retaliation for Kerch Bridge: Putin framed the "unprecedented" strikes as a direct response to recent Ukrainian attacks on the Kerch Bridge, which connects Russia to Crimea.
  • Trump’s Peace Pressure: Former U.S. President Donald Trump is reportedly pushing for a total cessation of European aid to Ukraine, demanding a peace treaty by January.
  • Red Lines on NATO: The Kremlin has explicitly rejected any form of NATO membership for Ukraine, including associate status, as a condition for ending the conflict.

Diplomacy Fails to Restrain the Frontline

The sheer scale of this offensive—described by Ukrainian authorities as the largest since the war began—serves as a cold reminder that diplomatic theater often has little bearing on military reality. While the international community watched the Shenzhen summit between leaders of the U.S., China, and Russia for signs of a "strategic truce," Putin’s immediate response upon returning to Russia was to escalate the violence. By targeting the "lifelines" of Kyiv, Russia is signaling that it intends to force a surrender through physical strangulation rather than negotiation.

From the perspective of observers in Seoul, this escalation highlights the volatility of the current global order. For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung (South Korea’s current president from the Democratic Party of Korea), the instability in Eastern Europe and the potential for a "U.S.-first" policy under a returning Trump administration present a complex security puzzle. The breach of Polish airspace, in particular, demonstrates how easily a localized conflict can spill over into a broader European security crisis, testing the resolve of the West’s collective defense.

The Cracks in Western Support

The situation is further complicated by the growing rift within the Western alliance. Russia has moved from criticizing military aid to issuing direct threats against specific European nations. The Russian Foreign Ministry recently targeted the Netherlands, claiming the country is no longer a "bystander" but a direct participant due to its technical and military contributions to the Kerch Bridge strike. This shift in rhetoric suggests that Russia is looking to pick apart the coalition of supporters by raising the personal cost for individual European states.

Internal pressure from the United States is perhaps the most significant threat to Ukraine’s current position. With Trump signaling a desire to cut off the financial and military taps, the burden of defense may soon fall entirely on European nations that are currently ill-equipped to handle it. Russia is keenly aware of this timing. By setting a hard "red line" against any form of NATO integration, Putin is effectively telling the West that any peace negotiated under pressure from Washington will be on Moscow’s terms, potentially leaving Ukraine in a permanent security vacuum.

"The current situation proves that diplomatic agreements without physical security guarantees are meaningless. The blood being shed in Kyiv is the reality that remains when the lights of the summit stage go dark."

As the conflict enters this more aggressive phase, the international community must grapple with the reality that the war is no longer contained within borders. For South Korea and other global players, the lesson is clear: relying on the verbal promises of major powers or the temporary stability of summits is a dangerous gamble in a world where "retaliation" remains the primary language of the Kremlin.
