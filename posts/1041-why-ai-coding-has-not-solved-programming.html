Why AI Coding Has Not Solved Programming

October 13, 2026

Generative AI tools can write routine code in seconds, but as real-world development shows, the hidden costs of maintenance, cognitive load, and human accountability mean coding is far from solved.

  • The maintenance trap: AI reduces the initial cost of writing code, but it leaves the heavy lifting of maintenance, debugging, and infrastructure management untouched.
  • Productivity illusion: Studies show experienced developers using AI tools can actually take longer to complete tasks due to the cognitive load of verifying and integrating AI-generated output.
  • The ownership rule: AI is a tool of execution, but humans bear the final consequences — meaning you cannot safely use code you do not understand.
  • Shifting skills: While AI handles personal projects and proof-of-concept tests efficiently, high-stakes systems like finance and healthcare still demand rigorous human oversight.

The Hidden Costs and Cognitive Load of AI Coding

The rise of generative artificial intelligence has drastically cut the time and money required to produce source code, turning tasks that once took days into hourly chores. Yet, software veterans know that initial writing is merely the tip of the iceberg. The real financial and operational drain lies submerged: long-term maintenance, security patches, sudden server crashes at 3 AM, and traffic optimization. When production fails, developers cannot call OpenAI for answers; the burden of fixing the system falls squarely on human shoulders.

At the same time, the promise of soaring productivity often clashes with reality. Research from organizations like METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research) involving senior open-source developers found that using coding assistants actually increased task completion times by nearly 20%. Developers anticipated faster results, but editing prompts, inspecting AI-generated snippets, and aligning them with existing projects created a massive cognitive bottleneck. While AI can boost output for junior developers dealing with repetitive tasks, it also tempts less-experienced users to rely on plausible-looking code without grasping its structural implications.

Ownership, Knowledge, and the Danger of Losing Fundamentals

Legally and operationally, artificial intelligence cannot take the blame when a system breaks. Alex Ewerlöf, an SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) expert, points out a vital rule: "You cannot be responsible for what you don't understand." A developer who pushes AI-generated code to a main repository without reading or grasping it is essentially flying blind. True software ownership rests on three pillars: knowledge of how the system works, the mandate to change and deploy it, and accountability when things go wrong. Delegating the "knowledge" pillar to AI while retaining full responsibility shatters that balance.

As developers lean more heavily on AI tools, a subtler danger emerges: rusty fundamentals. When human programmers stop writing raw code and diagnosing basic syntax from scratch, their foundational instincts dull. A degraded developer struggles to spot subtle, high-impact flaws hidden inside AI-generated scripts. While AI excels at rapid prototyping, personal projects, and disposable automation scripts, critical domains like financial transactions, medical records, and payment processing demand deep architectural control, automated testing, and rigorous code reviews. Ultimately, the future belongs not to those who can type prompts the fastest, but to those who retain the engineering judgment to question, verify, and master the tools they use.
