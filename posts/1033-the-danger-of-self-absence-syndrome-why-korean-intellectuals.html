The Danger of "Self-Absence Syndrome": Why Korean Intellectuals Are Warning Against Spiritual Escapism

September 23, 2026

A growing trend in South Korean social media and online discourse is the rise of "Self-Absence Syndrome" (무아병), a form of spiritual bypassing where people dismiss their real-world struggles, financial troubles, and physical pain as mere "illusions."

  • What happened: Intellectuals and influencers are critiquing the misuse of Buddhist concepts like "non-self" (anatta) to justify cynicism and social withdrawal.
  • The core error: Adherents mistakenly believe that if the world is an illusion, their daily responsibilities and physical suffering are meaningless.
  • The takeaway: Authentic "enlightenment" is not about detaching from reality, but engaging more deeply and responsibly with one's physical existence.

The Trap of Spiritual Bypassing

In modern Korean discourse, "Self-Absence Syndrome" acts as a convenient shield for those avoiding the pressures of a high-stress society. By labeling negative emotions or personal failures as "hollow shadows," individuals distance themselves from the necessity of healing or taking action. This practice ignores the central paradox of Eastern philosophy: if everything is an illusion, then that illusion is the very substance of reality. To reject the world as "fake" is to negate the existence of the self, leading to emotional paralysis and a false sense of superiority.

Critics argue that physical reality—hunger, fatigue, and illness—is the primary antenna through which we experience the world. Using abstract concepts to ignore a hungry stomach or a broken bank account is not spiritual growth; it is self-neglect. As the ancient philosopher Laozi suggested, tending to the needs of the body is the first step toward true understanding. Ignoring physical reality in favor of intellectual detachment is, in fact, a form of self-abuse that prevents genuine engagement with life.

Real Enlightenment is in the Daily Grind

The solution to this "syndrome" is a return to grounding. In Zen tradition, a famous anecdote tells of a student who claimed everything was an illusion, only to be struck by his master. When the student cried out in pain, the master retorted, "If nothing exists, who is it that feels pain?" This serves as a harsh reminder that true wisdom lies in acknowledging our human limitations, not denying them.

The "enlightenment" needed today is not a mystical retreat from society, but a shift toward "practical ethics." A truly enlightened individual doesn't transcend mundane tasks like working a job or cleaning a room; they perform them with renewed focus and care. By accepting that our lives are fleeting—like clouds in the sky—we find that the meaningful act is not to ignore the clouds, but to appreciate their form while they last. The path forward is not to hide behind the shield of "illusion," but to embrace the reality of the present moment, acknowledging that the act of living, in all its messy complexity, is the only truth we have.
