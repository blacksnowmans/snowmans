Apple Turns to Long-Term Deals as Korean Chipmakers Seize Memory Leverage

November 18, 2026

Apple is in late-stage talks for four-to-five-year DRAM supply contracts with South Korea’s Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, signaling that pricing power in global memory chips has decisively shifted from tech buyers to Korean manufacturers.

  • Big Tech locks in supply: With memory prices surging nearly 270% and now accounting for 40% of smartphone component costs, Apple is abandoning short-term buying to secure volume and price floors—leaving smaller device makers scrambling for leftover inventory.
  • Record war chests and sold-out lines: Samsung and SK Hynix have spent over 140 trillion won (about $100 billion) on R&D and facilities this year, backed by 329 trillion won (about $235 billion) in combined cash reserves, while SK Hynix says its DRAM and high-bandwidth memory (HBM) output is effectively sold out through 2029.
  • Rival stumble and foundry pivot: U.S. rival Micron Technology faces a nearly 36% stock pullback and safety fallout after a fatal contractor accident at its Taichung, Taiwan site, while Samsung’s contract chipmaking unit is phasing out older 8-inch lines to focus on 2-nanometer turnkey packages for AI clients like Meta.
  • Stock jitters vs. consumer pain: Ahead of Nvidia’s earnings and amid NAND price warnings, SK Hynix shares dipped below 1.1 million won (about $785) even as U.S. fund Dragoneer bought 2.4 trillion won (about $1.7 billion) in stock—while memory inflation has Samsung weighing price hikes of up to 19% on its budget Galaxy A phones.

The Hidden Risks Behind the Boom

On paper, South Korea’s memory duopoly looks untouchable, yet its massive spending spree carries real execution risks. SK Hynix’s four newly formed U.S. investment entities and more than 147 trillion won (about $105 billion) in U.S.-bound sales underscore a heavy reliance on a single buyer: Nvidia alone accounts for 35 trillion won (about $25 billion) of revenue. Meanwhile, Samsung’s Taylor, Texas fabrication plant posted losses through the first three quarters, a reminder that cutting-edge expansion punishes margins if production yields lag.

Alternative architectures could also chip away at HBM’s dominance. As heat and data bottlenecks challenge next-generation HBM4 memory, researchers at Nvidia and Georgia Tech—alongside chip design firm Semifive—are exploring 3D vertical chip stacking and stacked NAND flash (HBF) to reduce reliance on traditional HBM.

Why Market Volatility Misses the Bigger Picture

Still, short-term stock swings driven by pre-earnings caution around Nvidia obscure a structural shift favoring Seoul. Even if soaring chip costs eventually cool smartphone demand and force price hikes onto budget buyers, Samsung and SK Hynix have insulated their core capacity through multi-year contracts and South Korean chip-equipment suppliers like Hanmi Semiconductor, Justem, and YEST. For global tech giants, memory is no longer a commodity to squeeze—it is a strategic bottleneck they must pay upfront to survive.
