The Great Prosecution Rebrand: Cosmetic Reform or Strategic Deception?

September 07, 2026

South Korea’s plan to dissolve the Prosecution Service and replace it with a new "Prosecution Agency" is being criticized by experts and political observers as a high-stakes shell game that preserves the status quo under the guise of reform.

  • What happened: The government announced plans to replace the current Prosecution Service with a new, slimmed-down agency, but the organizational structure, staffing levels, and investigative mandates remain largely unchanged.
  • The contradiction: While the new agency claims to move away from direct investigations, it retains thousands of investigators and establishes new departments to oversee and command other law enforcement bodies.
  • Who is affected: The move affects the massive bureaucratic structure of the South Korean legal system, effectively shielding the existing establishment from the loss of power.

A Rebrand, Not a Reform

The proposed transition—shifting from the Prosecution Service to a Prosecution Agency—is being derided as a "cover-swap" renovation. While the names of internal bureaus have been changed—such as rebranding anti-corruption and drug units into a "Major Crimes Department"—the actual headcount and influence of these units remain intact. Critics note that if the goal were truly to limit the prosecution's power to initiate investigations, these massive departments would be abolished, not renamed.

Furthermore, the organizational bloat is staggering. The new plan includes 25 "chiefs" and 42 "branch chiefs" across the country, maintaining a hierarchy that mirrors the current system's expensive and top-heavy structure. By keeping roughly 7,000 investigators—roughly 10% of the entire national police force—on the payroll, the agency ensures it retains the capacity to intervene in investigations whenever it chooses.

Politics Over Policy

The failure of this reform to deliver meaningful change is rooted in deep-seated political maneuvering. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal party) has been accused of manipulating internal procedures and electoral rules to suit the interests of its core factions rather than addressing public demands for institutional transparency. Observers point to "rule-by-decree" tactics and arbitrary adjustments to internal voting systems as evidence that the leadership is more interested in maintaining control than in dismantling an unaccountable institution.

The broader concern is that the executive branch, both past and present, has used "reform" as a tool to tame specific agencies rather than to build a balanced, checks-and-balances system. When the highest levels of government openly signal distrust toward entities like the police, the entire policy apparatus bends to satisfy the political whims of the leadership. The result is a cycle where institutional reform becomes a political weapon, leaving the public with a judiciary that looks different on the surface but functions with the same concentration of power as before.
