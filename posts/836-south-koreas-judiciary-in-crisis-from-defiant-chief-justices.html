South Korea’s Judiciary in Crisis: From Defiant Chief Justices to Soft Justice for the Elite

October 08, 2026

South Korea’s judicial system is facing an unprecedented crisis of public trust, with top judges accused of dodging accountability, sheltering colleagues from serious discipline, and leaning heavily toward the political right in high-profile trials.

  • The Chief Justice's Defiance: Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae refused to take the witness oath during a parliamentary audit, citing judicial independence—a move critics call a convenient shield against scrutiny over judicial appointments and spending.
  • Two-Tiered Discipline: While lower-level court employees face harsh terminations for minor infractions, judges caught taking expensive drinks, accepting luxury goods, or destroying evidence routinely receive slap-on-the-wrist suspensions.
  • Political Trials Under Fire: Recent appellate rulings have sparked public outrage for acquitting figures tied to the scandal-plagued administration of jailed former leader Yoon Suk Yeol, while prosecutors and courts selectively edit transcripts to protect political allies.
  • Cultural Censorship: Conservative politicians and compliant media outlets are attempting to suppress public discourse, targeting hit films and spreading unverified claims while dodging basic journalistic fact-checking.

Judicial Impunity and the Myth of Independence

The standoff at the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee revealed an alarming sense of entitlement among South Korea’s highest legal minds. When Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae took his seat, he not only refused to stand for the mandatory witness oath but walked out by lunchtime, pleading a bad cold. Using "judicial independence" as a blanket excuse to avoid answering questions about transparency and appointment wrangling with the presidential office sets a dangerous precedent.

Worse still is the staggering hypocrisy regarding personal conduct. While ordinary workers and junior civil servants face immediate firing for minor ethical breaches—such as misusing corporate cards or pocketing small transit fares—top judges escape accountability entirely. When questioned about lavish dining and entertainment expenses, Cho dismissed them by claiming he didn't even know how much the meals cost, adding the insulting excuse that staff simply wanted to try food they couldn't normally afford. Meanwhile, judges caught receiving millions of won in entertainment or attempting to destroy evidence by swapping phones face only months of suspension under the lenient Judges Disciplinary Act, proving that the legal establishment operates as an untouchable sanctuary.

Politicized Courts and the Suppression of Truth

This institutional rot extends directly into the courtroom, where political loyalty often outweighs the rule of law. Recent appellate decisions have cleared figures linked to illegal political polling scandals, leaning on bizarre legal definitions to excuse transactions as mere "political assistance." Observers note that courts handling cases involving figures like Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon appear increasingly detached from standard fairness, accepting selectively edited transcripts while ignoring mountains of digital evidence.

Outside the courtroom, this defensive posture has spilled over into a broader culture war. Conservative politicians and right-leaning media outlets have launched aggressive campaigns to censor popular historical films, deploying baseless attacks and manufactured rumors to discredit art that challenges their narrative. By abandoning basic fact-checking in favor of red-scare propaganda, much of the mainstream press has reduced itself to a mouthpiece for entrenched power.

When the judiciary acts as a shield for the powerful rather than an arbiter of justice, the foundation of democracy begins to crack. Restoring public faith will require dismantling these self-serving legal cartels and demanding true accountability from those who claim to uphold the law.
