Loyalty Tests Over Livelihoods: The Fracturing of South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 09, 2026

The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is risking its mandate by prioritizing internal purges and "loyalty tests" over a deepening economic crisis that has left small business owners and the youth in their worst state in over a decade.

  • Internal factionalism is alienating key allies: Critics of President Lee Jae-myung are being labeled "Anti-Lee" and ostracized, contributing to a 28.4% drop in support among the administration's original base.
  • Economic indicators are masked by a semiconductor boom: While chip exports hit record highs, the monthly wage gap between permanent and temporary workers has widened to a record $1,400 (1.94 million KRW).
  • Small businesses are at a breaking point: Loan delinquency rates for the self-employed have hit a 13-year high, contradicting the government’s rhetoric of a "Golden Age of Democracy."
  • Legislative distractions: Efforts by DPK lawmakers to prioritize the President’s legal issues—such as the "Gongchimo" group’s push to drop his pending charges—are being criticized as "political loyalty tests" that ignore public suffering.

The Peril of the "Infallibility Myth"

The core of the current tension within the "liberal camp"—the coalition that supported the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and the election of President Lee Jae-myung—is a culture of blind loyalty. Party hardliners have begun labeling any internal criticism as "Anti-Lee" sentiment. This has manifested in derogatory online tags targeting anyone from former President Moon Jae-in to Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, if they offer even mild policy corrections. By demanding total conformity, the ruling party is shrinking its own political territory and ignoring the "Red Team" warnings necessary for healthy governance.

This factionalism is not just a social media phenomenon; it has paralyzed the legislature. A group of 100 DPK lawmakers recently formed "Gongchimo," a committee focused on dropping the President’s remaining legal charges from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration. Critics argue that while the previous administration’s "prosecutorial overreach" was a real issue, focusing on the President’s personal legal risk while the economy craters sends a message of profound elitism to a struggling public.

A "Golden Age" Only for the Top Tier

The disconnect between the government’s optimism and the street-level reality is stark. DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok has touted a "Golden Age," citing annual semiconductor exports of $120 billion. However, this wealth is concentrated in giants like Samsung and SK Hynix. Outside this bubble, one in four listed Korean companies is currently operating at a loss. The gap in operating profit margins between large conglomerates and small-to-medium enterprises has hit its widest point since 2015.

For the youth, the situation is increasingly grim. During the 1997 IMF crisis, about 17% of graduates started their careers in "non-regular" (temporary or contract) positions. Today, that number has nearly doubled to 33.5%. With the self-employed facing record debt and young workers locked out of stable employment, the administration’s focus on internal "loyalty tests" appears increasingly out of touch. If the government does not pivot to aggressive welfare and labor reforms within the next year, it faces a harsh judgment in the April 2028 general elections.

"True collective intelligence does not come from a choir of praise, but from the ability to listen to the most painful criticisms."
