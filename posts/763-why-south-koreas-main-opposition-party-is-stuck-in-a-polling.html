Why South Korea’s Main Opposition Party is Stuck in a Polling Rut

September 15, 2026

  • What happened: The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal opposition, is struggling with stagnant approval ratings, hovering in the mid-30% range.
  • The core conflict: Public support for the party is inextricably linked to the president's performance; as long as the president's approval rating remains mired around 33%, the DPK finds itself unable to gain momentum regardless of its internal efforts.
  • The shift: Moderates are abandoning the DPK, signaling that voters are less interested in the party’s ideological rhetoric and more concerned with its perceived incompetence and lack of accountability.
  • The takeaway: The party's tendency to protect its own members regardless of controversy—coupled with questionable appointments to key posts—is alienating the public and undermining its platform for reform.

The Symbiosis of Failure

In South Korean politics, a party’s fate is often tied to the president’s performance, acting like Siamese twins. Recent polling data showing the DPK oscillating between 36% and 41% isn't just a sign of policy failure; it is a symptom of a broader structural decline. When the president’s approval rating sits at 33%, the political gravity pulls the entire establishment down with it. While the DPK leadership focuses on aggressive political framing—even discussing the dissolution of rival parties—the moderate middle is focused entirely on the "report card" of their daily economic lives. As long as the administration remains in the 30% basement, the DPK’s calls for reform simply ring hollow.

Accountability Over Ideology

The DPK is currently suffering from a "fortress mentality" that keeps it from addressing its own systemic issues. Voters are increasingly disillusioned by the party’s selective morality—often described in Korea as naeronambul (roughly: "if I do it, it's romance; if you do it, it's an affair"). When the party shields its own lawmakers from allegations of misconduct while simultaneously demanding high ethical standards from others, it loses its moral authority.

This is further exacerbated by questionable personnel choices, such as appointing critics of the party's own flagship "prosecution reform" policy to head the new investigative agency, or placing controversial figures in leadership roles at its think tank, the Institute for Democracy. These moves suggest that the leadership is prioritizing loyalty over expertise and reform.

Ultimately, the DPK is facing a crisis of identity. By alienating the moderate voters who demand pragmatic governance, the party is no longer positioned as a viable alternative, but rather as an insular group protecting its own power. For a party that prides itself on the democratic values established by previous liberal administrations, this era of "coddling" its own members is a betrayal of the very spirit they claim to represent. Unless the DPK is willing to embrace internal discipline and demonstrate genuine reform, it risks remaining a party that lives in the past, unable to secure the future of the nation.
