Why Panic-Selling South Korean Chip Stocks Could Be a Costly Mistake

October 26, 2026

Despite sharp recent pullbacks in semiconductor heavyweights, underlying demand for artificial intelligence hardware and historically depressed valuations suggest South Korea's chipmakers are sitting in deeply oversold territory rather than at the end of a cycle.

  • Heavyweights under pressure: Leading semiconductor stocks such as Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix have tumbled to price-to-earnings (P/E) multiples between two and three times, well below global peers like Micron.
  • Data center payback accelerates: Physical AI infrastructure demand remains critical, with legacy chips like Nvidia’s H100 leasing above original prices and major cloud providers recouping data center capital expenditures in as little as six months to three years.
  • Solidigm turnaround: SK Hynix’s once-criticized $9 billion acquisition of Intel’s NAND unit, Solidigm, has turned into a strategic asset, topping 8 trillion won (about $6 billion) in 2024 revenue on surging enterprise SSD (eSSD) demand.
  • Asymmetric risk-reward: Analysts argue the current market floor offers an upside-to-downside ratio of roughly 4-to-1, with Samsung's projected shareholder returns for next year approaching 15% of its market capitalization.

Underlying Numbers Clash With Market Pessimism

Retail sentiment across Seoul's trading floors has soured rapidly following steep declines in market indexes, but operational data from AI supply chains tells a different story. In the physical computing market, shortages remain pronounced enough that secondary market leases for Nvidia's older H100 chips command higher rates than newer hardware. Furthermore, Amazon Web Services (AWS) data center investments reportedly yield returns near 33% annually against corporate borrowing costs around 5% to 6%, providing strong commercial incentives for hyperscalers to keep buying enterprise memory and processing hardware.

Fears that infrastructure insolvencies among specialized cloud providers would erase chip demand misread liquidation realities. Physical compute clusters retain high collateral value, quickly absorbed by competing tech giants during insolvencies. Meanwhile, SK Hynix's corporate restructuring is paying dividends: its high-capacity eSSDs have become indispensable for generative AI workloads, transforming Solidigm from a drag on the parent balance sheet into an 8-trillion-won revenue engine, all while the company shelves short-term pre-IPO dilution in favor of organic balance-sheet management.

Valuation Disconnect and Shareholder Returns

From a trading perspective, index levels hovering near forward price-to-book (P/B) ratios of 1.0x present an asymmetrical risk-reward setup. Assuming a 10% downside against a prospective 30% to 40% rebound toward historical highs, dumping chip exposure at current levels means selling at the bottom of standard valuation bands.

The gap between South Korean producers and overseas competitors has also widened beyond fundamental justification. Micron trades at double the multiple of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, despite Samsung projecting between 550 trillion and 600 trillion won (roughly $415 billion to $450 billion) in revenue next year. Backed by massive free cash flow, Samsung’s total shareholder return package—combining dividends and share buybacks—is modeled to approach nearly 15% of its market capitalization, compared to sub-2% yields at international peers. For patient capital, the current sell-off reflects market psychology rather than a breakdown in the semiconductor supercycle.
