South Korea’s Main Opposition Party Launches Filibuster and Street Protests Against Special Counsel Bill

November 06, 2026

The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has declared "total war" against the ruling party’s push for a special counsel investigation into alleged prosecutorial misconduct, paralyzing the National Assembly with a filibuster while mobilizing mass street protests.

  • Legislative Paralysis: PPP lawmakers began a filibuster in the National Assembly to block the "Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel Act," which they label an "assembly coup."
  • Street Mobilization: The opposition announced a major rally at Gwanghwamun—Seoul’s central protest square—for December 7, signaling a move from parliamentary debate to populist resistance.
  • Targeting the Past: The bill seeks to investigate alleged evidence tampering and "fabricated" cases handled by the prosecution during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment.
  • Budget Gridlock: The standoff has frozen the National Assembly’s Budget and Accounts Committee, delaying the approval of next year’s national budget as the year-end deadline looms.

A Fight for Political Survival

The intensity of the PPP’s resistance stems from the specific nature of the proposed special counsel. The bill targets what the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) calls "fabricated indictments" and investigative abuses committed during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. For the PPP, this is not just a legal battle but a fight for their political legacy. By labeling the bill a "defense firm for President Lee Jae-myung," the opposition is attempting to frame the investigation as a partisan vendetta rather than a pursuit of judicial reform.

However, the PPP’s decision to abandon the assembly floor for street protests carries significant risks. While Jeong Jeom-sig, the PPP floor leader, claims they are "defending democracy," the optics of a major political party skipping budget deliberations to hold rallies with far-right activists may alienate moderate voters. The gridlock is already affecting critical legislation, including amendments to the National Agricultural Cooperative Federation Act, leaving essential sectors of the economy in limbo while the two sides trade insults.

The Shadow of the Impeached Administration

Internal tensions are also simmering within the conservative camp. The PPP is currently haunted by the financial and legal "invoices" of the Yoon era. This includes a mounting controversy over the party's failure to return approximately $28 million (39.7 billion won) in state-reimbursed election funds following Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's removal from office. The ruling DPK has seized on this, accusing the opposition of "hit-and-run" politics—demanding legal purity from the government while failing to settle their own massive debts to the taxpayer.

As the PPP moves its base of operations to the streets, the political climate in Seoul is reaching a boiling point. The government under President Lee Jae-myung views the special counsel as a necessary tool to "cleanse" the judiciary of the previous administration's influence. Meanwhile, the opposition's rhetoric has escalated to calling for the "cancellation of the current administration." This total refusal to recognize the legitimacy of the legislative process suggests that South Korea’s political polarization has moved past the point of simple policy disagreement and into a fundamental crisis of governance.

"The opposition is choosing to hold the national budget hostage to protect the remnants of a disgraced administration. Leaving the assembly for the streets is not leadership; it is an admission of logical defeat."

Whether the filibuster and the Gwanghwamun rallies will succeed in swaying public opinion remains to be seen. For now, the only certainty is that the "Special Counsel 정국" (political situation) will keep the National Assembly dark and the streets of Seoul loud for the foreseeable future.
