Leaked Telegrams Expose Culture of Arrogance in South Korea’s Main Opposition Party

September 30, 2026

Leaked mobile communications from senior members of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country’s primary liberal opposition party—have revealed a pattern of internal disdain, the silencing of dissent, and a disconnect from the party's own grassroots supporters.

  • The "Telegram Gate": Senior DPK lawmaker Kim Min-seok ignored repeated warnings from a staffer regarding the flawed vetting of Kim Seung-won, a candidate for Minister of Justice, ultimately leading to the candidate's failed nomination.
  • Silencing Dissent: The party leadership has been caught using threats of disciplinary action and "real-name" posting requirements to suppress criticism from party members, particularly regarding controversial local nominations.
  • Internal Fractures: Leaked messages show leadership actively mocking allies, including the Rebuilding Korea Party (a smaller liberal party) and prominent progressive intellectual Rhyu Si-min, revealing deep hostility toward anyone outside their immediate circle.

A Pattern of Dismissive Leadership

The controversy centers on Kim Min-seok, a DPK supreme council member, whose private messages were leaked to the media. When a former aide attempted to warn him about serious irregularities surrounding Kim Seung-won and the DPK’s Gyeonggi provincial chapter, Kim simply replied, "Busy. Tight schedule. Until Chuseok (the Korean harvest holiday)." Despite being sent detailed evidence of systemic failures, Kim remained silent, choosing to publicly double down on his support for a candidate who had almost no public trust—only 22% of the public deemed the nomination appropriate at the time.

This incident is not an outlier but a symptom of a leadership style that treats internal communication as a one-way street. By labeling dissent as "harmful to the party" and threatening to expel those who raise valid questions, the DPK leadership has effectively dismantled the mechanisms of internal accountability. The result is a fragile "bubble" where the party ignores data and warnings until a crisis, such as a candidate's sudden resignation, forces a reckoning.

Beyond Policy: The Politics of Derision

The leaked Telegram messages from the National Assembly floor have laid bare a culture of petty infighting. In one instance, Kim Min-seok and his colleagues were seen mocking the Rebuilding Korea Party for missing a vote due to pre-scheduled regional events, using disparaging language to label them "provincial politicians." In another, they derided Rhyu Si-min, a highly respected progressive figure, as a "Gangnam intellectual who enjoys TV appearances."

These are not merely policy disagreements; they are signs of an arrogant political culture that views anyone not perfectly aligned with the leadership's current whims as an enemy. When the party faces criticism, it frequently retreats into "both-sidesism"—the lazy argument that everyone is equally at fault—to avoid addressing the root cause: a leadership that has lost touch with its own base. By silencing the grassroots and alienating potential allies, the DPK is not just struggling to govern; it is actively eroding the democratic foundations it claims to represent.
