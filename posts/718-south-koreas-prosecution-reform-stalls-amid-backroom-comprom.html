South Korea's Prosecution Reform Stalls Amid Backroom Compromises

September 11, 2026

  • South Korea's Ministry of Justice has proposed a restructuring plan that would add 281 new prosecutor positions while creating a new investigative agency—moves critics say water down the long-sought separation of investigation and indictment.
  • Shin Chang-sik, a lawmaker for the Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive minor opposition party), lashed out in parliament, accusing the establishment of staging a superficial "name change" rather than true reform.
  • The main opposition Democratic Party faces mounting pressure from its base for failing to push back against these compromises and for drifting rightward on key economic issues.

South Korea’s long-running crusade to strip the prosecution service of its unchecked powers is hitting a wall, and progressives are furious. What was supposed to be a fundamental restructuring of the country's investigative apparatus now looks increasingly like a bureaucratic shell game. Under the guise of handling heavier court caseloads, the government is pushing to expand prosecutor staffing while placing former prosecutors at the helm of newly planned investigative bodies. Critics argue this preserves the old guard's privileges under a fresh coat of paint.

The political fallout extends well beyond judicial reform. The Democratic Party, South Korea's dominant liberal opposition, is taking heavy friendly fire for standing by quietly. Lawmakers like Shin Chang-sik have publicly upbraided their colleagues for letting reform momentum die. Compounding these frustrations is the party's wobbly stance on real estate taxation—specifically, moves to ease or scrap the comprehensive real estate holding tax (known as the Jongbu-se). For a party that claims to represent working people and the young, trimming taxes on high-value property to chase swing voters looks like a betrayal of core principles.

Reform in Name Only

The core dispute centers on the government's new organizational decree for the prosecution service. Proponents frame the push for 281 new prosecutors as a practical fix for court backlogs. But legal experts and civic groups, including People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy, warn that it preserves the structural overlap between investigating crimes and prosecuting them. Worse, floating former prosecutors to lead upcoming anti-corruption and major crime investigation agencies risks turning new bodies into extensions of the old regime.

For the voters who mobilized behind progressive causes, these compromises are hard to swallow. The frustration is not merely about headcounts or tax brackets; it is about trust. When the political class waters down structural reforms to court conservative voters or soothe institutional heavyweights, it abandons the mandate given to it by the public. If progressives want to maintain credibility heading into future elections, they will need to decide whether they are instruments of genuine change or just another group of managers protecting the status quo.
