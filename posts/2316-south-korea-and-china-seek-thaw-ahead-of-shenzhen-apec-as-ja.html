South Korea and China Seek Thaw Ahead of Shenzhen APEC as Japan Falters

November 14, 2026

South Korea is aggressively pursuing a diplomatic reset with Beijing ahead of next week’s APEC summit, positioning itself as a pragmatic mediator while Japan’s new leadership struggles with isolation and economic volatility.

  • Foreign Minister Cho Hyun met with China’s Wang Yi in Beijing to finalize a bilateral summit between President Lee Jae-myung and President Xi Jinping.
  • Seoul is prioritizing "practical diplomacy" to stabilize industrial supply chains and ease non-tariff barriers that have hindered trade since the THAAD missile defense dispute.
  • Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi faces a diplomatic "cold shoulder" from China and a crippling currency crisis as the Yen hit 160 per dollar.
  • South Korean officials have dismissed rumors of North Korean leader Kim Jong-un attending the summit, signaling a preference for stable, predictable outcomes over diplomatic "surprises."

Seoul’s Pragmatic Pivot

The meeting between Foreign Minister Cho Hyun and his Chinese counterpart Wang Yi marks a significant shift for the Lee Jae-myung administration, led by the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's liberal party). Unlike the ideological friction of the previous era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the current government is leaning into a "national interest first" strategy. By defining the peace of the Korean Peninsula as a "vital shared interest" with China, Seoul is successfully reopening high-level communication channels that had been frozen for years. This isn't just about security; the Ministry of Trade, Industry, and Energy is simultaneously coordinating with Beijing to ensure that industrial supply chains remain insulated from broader geopolitical shocks.

For President Lee, the upcoming summit in Shenzhen is a high-stakes debut of his pragmatic doctrine. The goal is to secure tangible economic wins—such as the normalization of cultural content exports and the removal of hidden trade barriers—rather than getting bogged down in the "new Cold War" rhetoric. While China remains aggressive regarding Taiwan, its willingness to coordinate military hotlines with Washington suggests a temporary window of stability that Seoul is keen to exploit for economic gain.

Japan’s Diplomatic and Economic Quagmire

In stark contrast to Seoul’s momentum, Tokyo is entering the APEC cycle in a state of defensive crisis. Prime Minister Takaichi, known for her hawkish stance, is finding that ideology has a high price. Chinese Premier Li Qiang has conspicuously avoided individual contact with her at recent multilateral gatherings, a snub that Japanese media is labeling "Japan Passing." This isolation is being compounded by a "double squeeze" from the United States: the incoming Trump administration has reportedly demanded that Japan quadruple its payments for hosting U.S. troops, a staggering bill that the Takaichi cabinet is ill-prepared to pay.

Domestically, the situation is equally dire. With the Yen breaching the 160 mark against the dollar, Takaichi has been forced to pivot toward populist measures, announcing massive cash handouts for over half of Japan's workforce to stem plummeting approval ratings. As Japan attempts a desperate diplomatic "hail mary" by reaching out to Russia for a potential summit, it highlights a fractured foreign policy. While South Korea builds a bridge to Beijing, Japan appears to be drifting, caught between a cooling relationship with its largest neighbor and an increasingly transactional ally in Washington.
