The Prosecution's Survival Instinct: Bureaucratic Reform or Power Grab?

September 10, 2026

The South Korean Ministry of Justice’s new proposal to reorganize the prosecution into a "Public Prosecution Office" (Gongso-cheong) is facing fierce criticism for being a superficial reform that aims to preserve the prosecution's vast influence under a new name.

  • What happened: The Ministry of Justice proposed a restructuring plan that keeps roughly 7,000 existing investigators, resulting in a negligible reduction in personnel despite claims of downsizing.
  • The "Judicial Control" Unit: A new "Judicial Control Department" is being created to review cases that police decide not to forward to prosecutors, effectively granting the prosecution a new layer of oversight over other investigative agencies.
  • The core conflict: Critics argue the plan violates the public mandate to separate investigative and prosecutorial powers, suggesting the prosecution is merely "laundering" its existing authority to remain a political powerhouse.

A Trojan Horse for Prosecutorial Power

The proposal is being dismissed as "reform in name only." Out of an existing staff of 8,412, the plan claims a 21.4% reduction, but when accounting for personnel moving to the proposed Major Crimes Investigation Agency, the actual reduction is a mere 238 people. By retaining the vast majority of its investigative staff, the prosecution is positioning itself to continue "investigative command" from the shadows, constantly monitoring case files from other agencies despite being officially barred from leading investigations.

The creation of the "Judicial Control Department" is particularly galling to critics. While the prosecution frames this as a necessary check on the system, it is viewed by opponents as an arrogant play to maintain dominance over the police and other agencies. By positioning themselves as the ultimate arbiters of justice, prosecutors are accused of ignoring their own need for reform while clinging to their "iron rice bowl" status.

Beyond the Prosecution: A Nation Under Pressure

The internal fight over prosecutorial reform is unfolding against a backdrop of external challenges that threaten South Korea’s economic and diplomatic sovereignty.

  • Economic Coercion: Reports indicate that the U.S. is pressuring South Korean firms to invest over $350 billion (about 480 trillion won), including projects that global energy giants have already deemed unprofitable.
  • Geopolitical Risks: There is growing pressure for South Korea to deploy troops to the Strait of Hormuz, a move that critics describe as serving U.S. security interests at the cost of South Korean lives.
  • Technological Shift: The rapid advancement of AGI (Artificial General Intelligence), such as the "GPT-6 Astra" model mentioned by NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang, is fundamentally changing the labor landscape, yet the government remains distracted by internal political squabbles rather than preparing for the inevitable social disruption.

The common thread between these crises is a lack of independent strategy. Whether it is the prosecution's stubborn fight to maintain its traditional political role or the government's perceived subservience to U.S. investment demands, the current leadership is being criticized for prioritizing short-term survival over necessary structural change. Without a clear plan to cut away the "dead wood" of the old guard and assert sovereign interests on the global stage, South Korea risks being sidelined by the very forces it fails to confront.
