Global Crises and Seoul's Strategic Blind Spots

September 14, 2026

Global turbulence—from stalled South Korean defense projects and Middle Eastern shipping chokepoints to shifting superpower alignments—exposes deep vulnerabilities in Seoul's foreign policy and economic readiness.

    South Korea's planned nuclear-powered submarine program has stalled, with ten months passing before real talks began after a presidential summit, hampered by bureaucratic inaction and Washington's coolness. Yemeni Houthi rebels have choked the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, threatening a crucial artery for South Korean oil imports and creating a dangerous deployment dilemma for Seoul. The BRICS summit showcased a shifting global order, as nations like India and traditional US partners in the Gulf hedge their bets between Washington and Beijing. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu continues to leverage the Gaza conflict to delay his political reckoning ahead of the 2026 general election.

Stalled Submarines and Middle Eastern Chokepoints

South Korea's push to acquire nuclear-powered submarines—intended to counter North Korean underwater threats—has run aground due to bureaucratic foot-dragging and American non-proliferation resistance. Despite a presidential agreement, an effective multi-agency command structure is absent, leaving Seoul unable to present a coherent diplomatic package to Washington. Bureaucratic turf wars are actively compromising long-term national security.

Simultaneously, the blockade of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait by Yemen's Houthi rebels threatens global logistics and South Korea's energy lifeline. While the US navigates its own diplomatic calculus with Iran, Seoul faces mounting pressure to send naval assets. Joining a high-risk military deployment without guarantees of maritime safety or clear national interests would be a reckless gamble rather than a diplomatic duty.

The End of Single-Superpower Diplomacy

The recent BRICS summit made clear that the unipolar era is giving way to a fragmented, multipolar world. Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi's pivot to re-engage China while maintaining ties with the US demonstrates a pragmatic "hedging" strategy. Traditional Middle Eastern allies of Washington are playing a similar game, leaving rigid, single-track alliance diplomacy increasingly obsolete.

For an export-driven economy like South Korea, clinging blindly to a traditional camp-based foreign policy is a dangerous trap. Seoul must adopt a flexible, interest-driven approach rather than relying on outdated assumptions that unbending loyalty to Washington automatically safeguards its economic and security future.
