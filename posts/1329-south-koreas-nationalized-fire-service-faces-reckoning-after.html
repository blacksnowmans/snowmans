South Korea's "Nationalized" Fire Service Faces Reckoning After Bucheon Tragedy

October 19, 2026

Despite a high-profile reform four years ago to nationalize South Korea’s firefighting ranks, the central government covers barely 10% of the budget, leaving frontline responders equipped with expired safety gear while politicians trade blame over funding shortfalls.

  • Fatal rescue exposes gear crisis: The death of 44-year-old veteran firefighter Kim Jin-seok during an October 16 factory fire in Bucheon has ignited public fury over hazardous working conditions and structural neglect.
  • A "national" service in name only: According to parliamentary research, the central government provides only 10.7% of the total fire budget, leaving nearly 90% to cash-strapped local municipalities and entrenching stark regional disparities.
  • Critical equipment past expiration: Nationwide, 38.9% of six essential personal protective items—including oxygen respirators and fire-retardant suits—have exceeded their official service life, a figure that surges to 74.4% for respirators in Gyeonggi Province.
  • Budget slashed over tobacco sales: The 2027 draft budget cuts the Fire Safety Grant Tax by 27.6% (289.7 billion won, or about $210 million) to 760.5 billion won (about $550 million) simply because national tobacco tax revenues declined.
  • Political fallout: President Lee Jae-myung signaled support for nationwide standardized benefits, while Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae faced harsh parliamentary criticism from the conservative People Power Party after likening fire expenditures to a "straw" draining regional coffers.

The Dangerous Illusion of Central Reform

When Seoul officially reclassified municipal firefighters as national civil servants in April 2020, the public expected uniform protection across the country. In practice, the reform changed job titles without transferring the financial burden. Because municipal authorities still shoulder nearly 90% of operating expenses, a firefighter's physical safety depends on the fiscal health of their local jurisdiction. Tying frontline safety grants to fluctuating tobacco excise taxes rather than the central government's general budget is an indefensible bureaucratic trap that forces first responders to rush into flames wearing degraded gear.

Political Rhetoric Versus Budgetary Reality

The tragedy immediately spilled into the annual parliamentary audits. Governor Choo Mi-ae’s clumsy choice of words—calling the lopsided local burden a "straw" on provincial finances—drew bipartisan outrage and forced an apology, even though her underlying complaint about missing national subsidies was accurate. Careless political rhetoric has once again obscured the core systemic failure: the state's refusal to adequately fund the personnel it claims to protect.

President Lee Jae-myung’s commitment at the presidential press hall to establish uniform national support outlines the right policy direction. However, with the latest Realmeter survey putting Lee’s approval rating at 37.3% against 60.4% disapproval, and the ruling Democratic Party locked in fierce legislative combat with the People Power Party, rhetoric alone will not replace expired oxygen tanks. The administration must legally mandate central general-fund contributions, proving that protecting frontline lives takes precedence over fiscal inertia.
