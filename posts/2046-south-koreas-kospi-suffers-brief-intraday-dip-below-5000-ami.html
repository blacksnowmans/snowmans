South Korea's KOSPI Suffers Brief Intraday Dip Below 5,000 Amid U.S. Tech Panic

November 06, 2026

South Korea’s benchmark stock market suffered a historic intraday panic as fears over U.S. tech spending cuts and legislative regulation triggered a massive foreign sell-off.

  • What happened: On November 6, 2026, the KOSPI—South Korea's main stock index—briefly plummeted over 8% intraday, breaking below the 5,000-point mark before recovering to close down 3.6% at 5,190 points. Foreign investors dumped over 1.1 trillion won (about $800 million) in a single session.
  • Key market fallout: Shares of semiconductor giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix swung violently, with Samsung touching an intraday low of 175,000 won before rebounding. The KOSDAQ, South Korea's tech-heavy secondary board, closed down 3% at 656.9 points.
  • The catalyst: Panic spread across Asian markets following a Democratic sweep in the U.S. midterms, which ignited fears of tighter power grid regulations on artificial intelligence data centers and reduced capital expenditures by U.S. tech giants.
  • Who is affected: Foreign institutional investors drove the sell-off, while domestic retail investors—backed by a massive 89 trillion won ($65 billion) in deposit reserves—and local institutions absorbed the selling pressure to cushion the fall.

Panic Pricing Ignores Physical Realities

The market's knee-jerk sell-off reflects short-term political noise and liquidity bottlenecks rather than a fundamental collapse in artificial intelligence demand. While headlines focused on U.S. legislative proposals and Microsoft restructuring its infrastructure leases, physical data center expansion remains largely locked in. Over 30 gigawatts of power capacity are already under construction in key U.S. hubs like Texas and cannot be undone overnight. Furthermore, major firms are actively reallocating capital to regions with flexible regulations, as demonstrated by Google's recent $17 billion data center investment in Wyoming.

Forced Liquidations Mask Strong Corporate Fundamentals

Much of Friday's market collapse was driven by structural liquidity squeezes rather than deteriorating corporate earnings. Margin calls on over-leveraged U.S. hedge funds—alongside forced portfolio adjustments by British institutional funds responding to domestic tax law changes—created severe localized selling pressure across Asian equities. At its intraday low, Samsung Electronics traded at a price yielding an extraordinary 8% in dividends, exposing a glaring disconnect between irrational market sentiment and core corporate valuation. As forced liquidations subside, resilient balance sheets and robust retail reserves should allow the market to recover its footing.
