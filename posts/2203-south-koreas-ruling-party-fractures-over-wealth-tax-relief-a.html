South Korea’s Ruling Party Fractures Over Wealth Tax Relief and Stock Market Fixes

November 11, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party has plunged into fierce internal turmoil after metropolitan lawmakers pushed to slash property taxes ahead of upcoming elections, triggering immediate accusations of abandoning core economic principles for short-term votes.

  • Tax cut demands: Democratic Party lawmakers representing the Seoul metropolitan area demanded lifting the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax deduction threshold for non-resident single-home owners to 1.4 billion won (around $1 million), with some calling for tax relief on apartments valued up to 2 billion won (around $1.5 million).
  • Progressive backlash: Progressive lawmakers within the party—including Representative Choi Min-hee—alongside allied minor parties and civic groups, condemned the initiative as a backward "tax cut for the top 0.7%" that dismantles tax justice.
  • Executive resistance: The presidential administration drew a hard line against the lawmakers' demands, insisting on maintaining its policy of favoring primary residents over non-resident property holders.
  • Stalled capital market bills: Concurrently, leadership efforts to soothe retail investors through fast-tracked legislation—such as an "anti-stock price suppression" bill and revisions to the mandatory tender offer system—hit a wall due to internal disagreements and opposition pushback.

Election Panic Collides with Tax Justice

The bitter clash inside the Democratic Party exposes a widening fault line between election pragmatism and core progressive dogma. Facing mounting voter discontent across the capital region, metropolitan lawmakers moved to soften the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax, long considered a cornerstone of the left's policy to curb housing speculation. Yet by scrambling to court middle- and upper-class property owners, the party has triggered an immediate crisis of legitimacy, alienating traditional progressive allies who view the reversal as an unprincipled surrender of tax justice.

The open discord between parliamentarians and the executive branch further compounds the political fallout. With government officials reaffirming that tax benefits should belong strictly to actual residents, the ruling bloc has projected mixed messages on one of the country's most volatile economic issues. Any compromise hammered out before late November will struggle to repair the credibility already lost from such public vacillation.

Rushed Market Interventions and Legislative Paralysis

The party's concurrent push to stabilize sliding domestic equities reflects a similar vulnerability: racing to appease angry retail investors with half-baked legal remedies. Facing heavy criticism over the Korea Discount and low price-to-book ratios, party leaders championed emergency bills aimed at protecting individual traders. Yet even within the party caucus, key measures like the mandatory tender offer overhaul stalled because lawmakers could not agree on technical details or navigate parliamentary review.

This policy whiplash handed an easy target to the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, which dismissed the initiatives as cynical fear-mongering and political theater. Rather than projecting steady economic governance, the ruling party's dual retreat on property taxes and capital market reforms leaves it caught in a political bind: rushing reactive legislation that satisfies neither its progressive base nor the broader electorate.
