The Democratic Party’s Approval Crisis Under Kim Min-seok

October 13, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation’s ruling party currently led by President Lee Jae-myung, is facing a severe political tremor as its approval ratings have plummeted 7 percentage points in just one week.

  • Sharp Decline: DPK approval dropped to 35.5% in the latest Realmeter survey, while the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) rose to 38.4%, effectively erasing the ruling party’s lead.
  • Leadership Under Fire: DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok, who promised a 10-percentage-point boost within his first 100 days, is facing public criticism and internal pressure from senior party members.
  • Internal Fragmentation: The party is increasingly divided, with "pro-Lee" factions and party leadership struggling to maintain cohesion amidst external scrutiny and internal finger-pointing.

A Leadership in Crisis

The current freefall in support is not merely a temporary fluctuation; it is a clear signal of public exhaustion with the current administration's direction. Even core supporters are showing signs of distancing themselves, and the "pro-Lee" outer circles—who are often the most vocal during prosperous times—are reportedly the first to turn against the leadership when things go south.

The urgency within the party is palpable. During recent morning strategy meetings, senior members like Choi Min-hee and Park Ju-min explicitly warned that the current "scattergun" approach to governance is failing. They highlighted that Chairman Kim Min-seok is juggling too many tasks without achieving tangible results, contrasting this with the focused, issue-driven leadership styles of the past. The clock is ticking toward a self-imposed 100-day deadline, and without a significant pivot, the party risks losing the very base that brought it to power.

Internal Infighting and Strategic Missteps

Perhaps more damaging than the opposition’s attacks is the DPK’s tendency to fire upon itself. Recent parliamentary audit sessions saw DPK members criticizing their own party figures, such as Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae, rather than focusing on the opposition. This "friendly fire" is viewed by many as a catastrophic lack of political judgment that provides the media and the PPP with easy ammunition to push a "hypocritical" narrative.

Compounding this is the lingering suspicion regarding the alleged infiltration of the party by members of the Shincheonji Church, a religious group often embroiled in political controversy. The leadership’s inability to provide a clear, transparent response to these claims has created a vacuum of trust. If the DPK fails to resolve its internal squabbles and fails to address the bread-and-butter economic issues—such as inflation and housing—that weigh most heavily on the public, the current dip in approval may harden into a permanent loss of mandate. In politics, arrogance is the fastest path to obsolescence, and the DPK is currently learning that lesson the hard way.
