OpenAI’s Sam Altman Lands in Seoul to Secure Korea’s High-Tech Memory Chips

November 02, 2026

OpenAI Chief Executive Sam Altman made a whirlwind two-day visit to South Korea to lock down vital High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) chips and manufacturing partnerships with tech giants Samsung Electronics and SK Group.

  • What happened: Altman held an unprecedented strategy session with over 50 top Samsung executives and met SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won to discuss HBM supply chains and next-generation AI data center infrastructure.
  • What it changes: OpenAI is laying the groundwork to diversify its infrastructure away from Nvidia’s dominance by securing direct memory supply and foundry capacity for custom AI hardware.
  • Who is affected: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix solidify their status as irreplaceable hardware anchors in the global AI boom, while domestic manufacturers face growing pressure to integrate AI into their own operations.

The Physical Bottleneck Behind Silicon Valley's Software

Altman’s unexpected trip underscores a cold reality in the tech industry: the most sophisticated artificial intelligence models are useless without physical hardware to compute them. As processing demands surge, the global AI bottleneck has expanded beyond graphics processors to High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—a premium semiconductor segment dominated by South Korea’s Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. To expand its computational infrastructure and build alternative hardware ecosystems, OpenAI has no choice but to negotiate directly with Seoul's manufacturing powerhouses.

During his briefing with Samsung leadership, Altman presented a symbolic souvenir representing OpenAI's coding tools alongside a blunt warning: holding AI technology alone offers no competitive edge unless companies integrate it into their operational workflows with extreme speed. Driven by this urgency, Samsung is pushing a company-wide AI transformation across its manufacturing lines and corporate processes to avoid being left behind as a legacy manufacturer.

Leverage, Sovereignty, and the Subcontractor Risk

While Korean chipmakers currently hold tremendous market power, Altman's visit highlights a strategic dilemma for South Korea. Supplying the hardware for Silicon Valley platforms carries the risk of remaining a high-end manufacturing subcontractor while American software firms capture the vast majority of platform profits. Maintaining an edge in memory production must be paired with developing homegrown software applications and retaining technological sovereignty.

This dynamic reinforces the need for consistent policy support for core tech industries. In contrast to the abrupt R&D budget cuts seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency—which undermined foundational research—the current stance under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration emphasizes public infrastructure support and safeguarding critical technology assets. For South Korea, preserving its semiconductor leadership is not merely a corporate objective, but a crucial element of national economic security.
