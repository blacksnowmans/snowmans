YouTube Shakes South Korea's Media: News Factory Tops 2026 Trust Rankings

September 24, 2026

  • The shift: The YouTube current affairs show Kim Ou-joon's Humble News Factory ranked as South Korea's most trusted broadcast program (6.3%), beating traditional terrestrial heavyweights like MBC's News Desk (5.9%) and KBS's News 9 (4.9%).
  • The context: A joint media trust survey by the weekly magazine *SisaIN* and pollster Gallup Korea shows digital-native political commentary overtaking legacy broadcasting in public influence.
  • The fallout: While veteran anchor Sohn Suk-hee still holds the top individual journalist spot, overall trust in traditional news media and reporters continues to plummet, leaving a massive uncommitted public looking for clear editorial frameworks rather than neutral reporting.

Legacy Media's Monopoly Collapses

South Korea's media landscape has crossed a definitive threshold. For decades, major public and commercial television networks held a tight monopoly on public credibility. The rise of a creator-led, subscription-and-donation-funded YouTube broadcast to the number-one trust spot just three years and nine months after its launch signals a structural break. Viewers are no longer waiting for the traditional evening news to learn what is happening; they are opting for participatory journalism that wears its political leanings openly.

This does not mean the public trusts journalists blindly—far from it. Over 64% of respondents in the survey registered no trust, indifference, or refusal to answer regarding media figures. Instead, audiences are hitching their trust to platforms and distinct editorial voices like Kim Ou-joon and former politician Rhyu Si-min. When mainstream broadcasters face internal contradictions—such as MBC landing simultaneously at the top of both trust and distrust charts—digital platforms step into the vacuum with dedicated, highly loyal audiences.

Political Turmoil Meets Judicial Scandals

The transformation in how South Koreans consume news comes against a backdrop of severe institutional strain. Courts are handing down lengthy prison sentences—up to 30 years—for military commanders involved in recent insurrection attempts, though figures like former Special Warfare Commander Kwak Jong-geun have won rare sympathy from victims' families by acknowledging their guilt and apologizing. Meanwhile, the judiciary itself is battling a severe credibility crisis following revelations that Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae's office misreported official expense accounts, spending hundreds of thousands of dollars on high-end meals while splintering bills to skirt oversight.

Simultaneously, President Lee Jae-myung faces the double-edged sword of public accountability. While a recent presidential press conference nudged his approval ratings upward to 34.8% by emphasizing communication and self-reflection, persistent missteps—such as a brief, ill-advised social media repost and the swift resignation of Justice Minister nominee Kim Seung-won over sexual misconduct allegations—highlight ongoing blind spots in administrative vetting. As South Korea navigates high-stakes diplomacy and deep ideological divisions, both the media and the state are being forced to prove their worth to a public that is increasingly tired of institutional excuses.
