Why OpenAI's Massive New AI Model Signals a Supercycle for South Korean Memory Chips

September 21, 2026

  • OpenAI's new "Astra" model required 100,000 GPUs to train, shifting AI from basic chatbots to autonomous agents and shattering previous estimates of memory demand.
  • South Korea's top memory giants—Samsung Electronics (monthly capacity around 650,000 wafers) and SK Hynix (around 500,000 wafers)—face a structural shortage as tech giants demand unprecedented volumes of DRAM.
  • While foreign investors have heavily favored SK Hynix for its aggressive share buybacks, institutional buyers are quietly betting billions on Samsung amid expectations of a foundry turnaround and 1C DRAM advances.
  • Despite macro fears over interest rates and rising energy costs for data centers, big tech's multi-trillion-dollar AI infrastructure spending is backed by real corporate cash flow, keeping the semiconductor supercycle firmly on track.

The global semiconductor market is caught in a high-stakes scramble as artificial intelligence development scales up at a pace that leaves traditional market forecasts in the dust. While retail investors panic over interest rate jitters and peak-cycle warnings, the underlying data points to an entirely different reality: big tech companies are locked in a zero-sum race where failing to out-spend rivals means total obsolescence.

In South Korea, this friction is playing out directly in the contrasting strategies of the nation's twin memory titans, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. SK Hynix has captured foreign capital by prioritizing steady 1B-process production and heavy share buybacks, locking down early leadership in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). Meanwhile, Samsung is playing a high-risk, high-reward game by forcing advanced 1C DRAM integration for HBM4 while pushing its troubled foundry business toward a long-awaited profitability turnaround. If Samsung stabilizes its yields, the current valuation gap between the two giants could snap shut overnight.

The Real Driver: 100,000 GPUs and a Structural Chip Shortage

The recent market jolt came straight from OpenAI, whose new ASTRA model required an astounding 100,000 GPUs for training. Developing the model reportedly cost between 700 billion and 1.3 trillion won (about $500 million to $950 million), creating an efficiency leap that allows the AI to handle 3.5 days' worth of human labor tasks in a single sitting. When OpenAI CEO Sam Altman previously warned South Korean executives that his company would soon need 90,000 wafers of DRAM per month, skeptics dismissed it as hyperbole. The astronomical compute requirements of ASTRA prove it was a sober warning.

Against this backdrop, the combined monthly production capacity of Samsung (65万 / ~650,000 wafers) and SK Hynix (50万 / ~500,000 wafers) looks dangerously tight. Even with Chinese competitors like ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) scaling up generic memory production, their low-end output poses little threat to the high-value HBM and server-grade chips dominated by Korean firms. The massive capital being poured into global data centers—projected by Goldman Sachs to hit $7.6 trillion by 2031—will inevitably strain power grids and drive up short-term inflation, but it guarantees that demand for advanced silicon remains insatiable.
