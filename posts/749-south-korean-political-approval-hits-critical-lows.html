South Korean Political Approval Hits Critical Lows

September 14, 2026

South Korean political sentiment is in a state of deep fragmentation, as recent polling data reveals a deadlocked rivalry between the country's two main parties and a sustained, record-breaking collapse in public support for the presidency.

  • Party Deadlock: The liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the conservative People Power Party (PPP) are tied at 42% and 41% respectively, signaling a volatile and aggressive political climate.
  • Presidential Approval Nosedive: The president’s approval rating has hit a fresh low at 37.3%, with disapproval soaring to 62.2% in recent ARS (automated response system) polling.
  • Third-Party Growth: The Rebuilding Korea Party, a newer political movement, has surged to 6.2%, reflecting growing voter fatigue with the traditional two-party duopoly.
  • Sustained Decline: Polling firm Realmeter reports that presidential approval has fallen for nine consecutive weeks without a single rebound.

A Stagnant Political Landscape

The current polling numbers suggest more than just a fluctuation in public opinion; they indicate a structural crisis of confidence. With the two major parties locked in a statistical tie, the political scene has devolved into a cycle of mutual character assassination rather than constructive policy competition. Voters are increasingly exhausted by this "mud-slinging" dynamic, which has failed to address the core issues affecting their daily lives.

The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party to 6.2% serves as a warning sign. It is not necessarily a reflection of total alignment with the party's platform, but rather a reactionary vote from citizens who feel that neither the DPK nor the PPP offers a viable path forward. The traditional political establishment is currently failing to read this shift as a demand for reform, instead choosing to ignore the signal or leverage it for further partisan division.

The Erosion of Presidential Authority

The most alarming trend is the president’s nine-week slide in approval ratings. In South Korean politics, falling into the 30% range often signals a loss of control over the national agenda. Analysts attribute this ongoing decline to a perceived failure in communication and an "arrogant" political style. Specifically, the administration's tendency to treat internal critics and its own base with hostility—often referred to in the source as the "anti-clone" (반분신) phenomenon—has alienated supporters who might otherwise have stood by the government.

The prevailing view is that the "golden time" for the administration to recover has already passed. While the president’s office often responds to these numbers with claims of distorted media coverage or external blame, the consistency of the nine-week drop suggests a fundamental hardening of public sentiment. At this stage, superficial apologies or late-term reshuffling of personnel are unlikely to restore credibility. The public appears to have moved beyond waiting for an explanation, signaling that the current administration is rapidly losing its mandate to govern effectively.
