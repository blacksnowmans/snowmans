Physical AI Moves Beyond the Screen: How South Korea and Global Tech Are Building the Hardware Frontier

November 09, 2026

The global artificial intelligence race is shifting rapidly from screen-based chatbots to "Physical AI," integrating intelligent algorithms directly into industrial robots, smart manufacturing, and autonomous logistics systems.

  • What happened: Global tech leaders and South Korean municipalities are pivoting heavily toward physical AI, highlighted by Namwon City’s planned 150 billion won ($108 million) logistics testbed and an unusual partnership between defense contractor Hanwha Aerospace and game developer Krafton.
  • What it changes: Competition is moving from cloud software to physical infrastructure, edge computing, and hardware supply chains, prompting local governments to scramble for land, power, and regulatory relief.
  • Who is affected: Industrial manufacturers, regional Korean economies building tech hubs, deep-tech startups attracting venture capital, and workforces adapting to real-world automation.

From Software to Steel: Regional Budgets and Unlikely Alliances

Unlike generative text models operating in the cloud, Physical AI requires real-world land, high-capacity power grids, and specialized local infrastructure. In South Korea, regional governments are competing to build these physical foundations. Namwon City in North Jeolla Province is pushing for a 150 billion won ($108 million) national demonstration center for autonomous logistics, while nearby Iksan and the southeastern industrial belt of Busan, Ulsan, and South Gyeongsang Province are racing to secure power and industrial plots. Local leaders are now lobbying the National Assembly to pass a dedicated Physical AI Special Act to remove regulatory roadblocks surrounding autonomous robots and real-world data collection.

Corporate strategies are evolving to match this hardware pivot. In one of the sector's most unexpected pairings, defense giant Hanwha Aerospace has joined forces with global video game publisher Krafton. Hanwha plans to use Krafton's virtual physics engines to run billions of simulated training runs for autonomous military vehicles before deploying them in the physical world. Meanwhile, edge-computing firms like Advantech and state institutions like the Korea Development Bank (KDB) are pouring capital into on-device AI solutions aimed at automating small- and medium-sized manufacturing plants.

Geopolitics and Local Execution

As AI enters factories and warehouses, hardware supply chains have become a primary geopolitical bottleneck. China's recent export controls on core robotics technologies have already disrupted cross-border tech alliances, including investment plans led by SoftBank Group. These friction points demonstrate that physical AI cannot thrive on software alone—it is bound by national borders, physical parts, and material supply chains.

Following the deep R&D budget disruptions during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, the current Lee Jae-myung administration’s focus on regional industrial clusters offers a pragmatic path forward. To remain competitive against US and Chinese tech giants, South Korea must rapidly establish regional physical AI hubs and enact clear legislative frameworks that allow real-world testing to move forward safely.
