South Korea's Political Tide: Why Presidential Approval Ratings are Signaling a Crisis

October 02, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing a sharp decline in public support, with negative job performance ratings consistently exceeding 60% and over half of the electorate now favoring a "judgment" against the current administration—a term used in Korean politics to describe a voter mandate to punish the ruling party.

  • Falling Approval: Presidential support has dipped into a danger zone, historically correlating with poor electoral outcomes for the ruling party.
  • The "Rule of Three": Political analysts note a grim historical trend: a party’s legislative seat count often mirrors the president’s approval rating multiplied by three.
  • Internal Friction: Strategic losses in local elections and a perceived disconnect between Lee’s past persona and his current presidency are fueling voter fatigue.
  • Statistical Warning: Experts warn against dismissing poor polling as "statistical noise," viewing it instead as a structural crack in the administration’s foundation.

The Math of Political Survival

A long-standing, chilling rule of thumb in Korean politics suggests that the number of seats a ruling party wins in a general election is roughly three times the president’s approval rating. While not a mathematical absolute, it has been eerily accurate in recent cycles. When former President Moon Jae-in held a 53.7% approval rating, his Democratic Party—the current liberal opposition-turned-ruling-power—secured a landslide 180 seats. Conversely, when his successor struggled with low ratings, the ruling party saw its parliamentary numbers plummet.

With Lee’s disapproval ratings topping 60%, the prospect of the ruling party maintaining a majority is becoming statistically improbable. The "judgment" sentiment, which currently stands at 52.8%, suggests that voters are viewing the next election as a referendum on the administration rather than a choice between competing policy platforms. When the president’s approval falls below 40%, the party’s ability to govern effectively is fundamentally compromised.

The Gap Between Perception and Reality

There is a growing sense of cognitive dissonance among the public regarding President Lee. Many voters who supported his rise are now questioning whether the man leading the country is the same individual who campaigned as a sharp, reform-minded politician. This is exacerbated by a tendency within the administration to blame the media or "external forces" for declining numbers, rather than addressing the underlying issues like judicial risks and party infighting.

The administration’s reliance on "blame-shifting" is failing to resonate. As commentators have noted, the public is not merely swayed by negative headlines; they are reacting to a perceived lack of transparency and a failure to deliver on promised reforms. Personnel reshuffles—such as replacing key staff—are being viewed as cosmetic fixes rather than substantive changes. Unless the administration shifts from a defensive stance to one of genuine accountability, it risks heading toward a full-blown "lame duck" phase well before its term concludes. The electorate is no longer interested in hearing excuses; they are waiting to see if the government has the courage to admit its own mistakes.
