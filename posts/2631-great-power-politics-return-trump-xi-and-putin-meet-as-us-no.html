Great Power Politics Return: Trump, Xi, and Putin Meet as US-North Korea Talks Collapse

November 23, 2026

A high-stakes trilateral summit between Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin alongside the collapse of last-minute US-North Korea meeting attempts signal a dangerous return to sphere-of-influence diplomacy that risks leaving South Korea sidelined.

  • Trilateral Summit: US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin held a 90-minute meeting on November 22, after which Trump publicly showcased photos comparing the gathering to the 1945 Yalta Conference while explicit references to North Korean denuclearization disappeared from discussions.
  • Failed North Korea Outreach: Trump attempted to broker a last-minute summit with Kim Jong Un, reportedly considering an end-of-war declaration and dropping immediate nuclear dismantlement demands, but Pyongyang rejected the effort by throwing back Trump’s personal letter unopened and demanding major sanction relief and a halt to US-South Korea joint drills.
  • Ukraine Peace Push: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky accepted a US proposal for three-way peace talks involving the US, Russia, and Ukraine before Christmas, with venues like Miami or the upcoming G20 summit under consideration.
  • South Korea Sidelined: The resurgence of great-power bargaining threatens to bypass Seoul, pressuring the administration of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung to bolster independent defense capabilities and expedite the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON).

The Eclipse of Denuclearization and "Yalta 2.0"

The 90-minute sit-down between Trump, Xi, and Putin—framed by Trump's social media post invoking the 1945 Yalta Agreement—marks an overt return to big-power cartel diplomacy. What is most alarming for Seoul is what vanished from the agenda: any explicit mention of the denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula. With Russia backing Pyongyang and China seeking strategic leverage, North Korea's nuclear capability is increasingly treated by global powers as an established reality to be managed rather than a threat to be eliminated.

Washington's aggressive push to secure a meeting with Kim Jong Un underscores this shift. Internal US deliberations reportedly weighed extreme concessions, including declaring a formal end to the Korean War and omitting demands for immediate nuclear dismantling. Yet Pyongyang's hostile posture—refusing even to unseal Trump's letter while raising non-negotiable demands—shows that Kim Jong Un feels sufficiently backed by Moscow and Beijing to raise his price and play hardball.

Implications for President Lee Jae-myung's Security Strategy

For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea's liberal leader, these developments raise immediate alarms over "Korea Passing"—the risk of Seoul being excluded while major powers bargain over the peninsula's fate. As Ukraine navigates US-pressured negotiations with Russia, South Korea faces its own strategic reckoning.

To prevent South Korean security from becoming a bargaining chip in transactional summitry, Seoul must reinforce its strategic autonomy. The current environment adds urgency to completing the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON) from Washington to Seoul and acquiring advanced deterrence assets, such as nuclear-powered submarines, ensuring South Korea maintains a decisive voice in its own regional defense.
