Internal Feuds and Merger Wars Shake South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 04, 2026

Conflict is erupting within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) as top leadership figures clash over past political smears and a potential merger with its smaller ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party.

  • Rep. Kim Min-seok issued a public apology for baseless claims linking party rivals to the Shincheonji cult, a move dismissed by rival Rep. Jung Chung-rae as a "successful political operation."
  • Speculation regarding a January merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the DPK’s staunchest liberal ally led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, has triggered a backlash from the smaller party over "media manipulation."
  • The DPK leadership’s decision to delay a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae has led to internal accusations that the party is losing its reformist edge.
  • These internal fractures threaten to dilute the legislative momentum of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration as it seeks to stabilize its governing mandate.

Leadership Feuds and the "Shincheonji" Smear

The friction centers on a public apology by Rep. Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the DPK, who expressed regret for past allegations that certain party factions were linked to Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious group often used in South Korean politics to smear opponents with "cultist" associations. While Kim attempted to frame the apology as a step toward party unity, it backfired spectacularly. Rep. Jung Chung-rae, a high-ranking DPK official and influential voice within the party, characterized the original smear as a calculated move to secure leadership positions, calling the apology a hollow gesture after the "political operation" had already succeeded.

This public spat highlights a deepening power struggle within the DPK. During the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently imprisoned), the party remained largely unified as an opposition force. However, since becoming the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, the DPK is struggling to maintain internal discipline. The exchange of labels like "anti-party activities" between top leaders suggests that the battle for influence within the party's hierarchy is reaching a boiling point.

A Fractured Liberal Alliance

The DPK’s relationship with the Rebuilding Korea Party is also fraying. While the DPK seeks to absorb the smaller party to solidify a dominant majority in the National Assembly, the Rebuilding Korea Party is resisting what it calls "unilateral media play." Members of the smaller party, including Rep. Shin Jang-shik, have criticized the DPK for leaking merger dates to the press without proper consultation. Instead of a simple absorption, they are demanding competitive primaries to maintain their political identity and "reformist" credentials.

This "identity crisis" extends to the DPK’s legislative strategy. By softening its stance on judicial probes—such as the investigation into Chief Justice Jo—the DPK risks alienating its most ardent supporters who demand aggressive reform of the prosecution and judiciary. The Rebuilding Korea Party has been quick to capitalize on this, mocking the DPK for dropping the "T" for impeachment (tan-haek) and the "G" for parliamentary probe (guk-jo). For President Lee Jae-myung, these internal and external liberal divisions represent a significant hurdle; if the ruling bloc cannot speak with one voice, its ability to pass major policy initiatives will be severely compromised.
