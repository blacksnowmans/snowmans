South Korea’s Semiconductor Boom: A Prosperity That Isn't Trickling Down

October 04, 2026

While South Korea’s export-heavy economy is being propped up by a record-breaking semiconductor boom, the reality for the average household is increasingly dire, characterized by stagnant wages, rising costs of living, and a crumbling domestic market.

  • The Wage Gap: While semiconductor industry employees have seen bonuses, the average real wage for most workers remains lower than it was in 2022.
  • The Vulnerable Left Behind: Real income for temporary and day laborers has plummeted, leaving the most vulnerable without a share of the industry's success.
  • Economic Distortion: A massive influx of liquidity to fuel the boom has triggered runaway inflation and a real estate crisis, pushing homeownership further out of reach for the middle class.
  • Export Reliance: With domestic consumption lagging, the economy is dangerously dependent on volatile global trade, making it highly susceptible to external shocks.

A Mirage of Growth

The "trickle-down" effect, once promised as a byproduct of industrial success, has failed to materialize. While headlines highlight record-breaking corporate profits and export milestones, these numbers are detached from the daily struggles of workers. Real average monthly wages have failed to recover to their 2022 levels, hovering around 3.84 million won (about $2,800). For the vast majority of the workforce, the "semiconductor heat" has provided no warmth.

Furthermore, the employment landscape has shifted in a troubling direction. The growth in jobs is concentrated among citizens aged 65 and older, while employment for the core 25–54 age group has stagnated. As small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) struggle to keep up with domestic consumption demands, quality jobs for young professionals are vanishing, replaced by temporary gigs. This structural mismatch suggests that the current economic prosperity is essentially hollow, benefiting only a few while isolating the rest.

Asset Bubbles and the Cost of Living

The government's focus on keeping the export engine running has resulted in a massive increase in market liquidity—a policy that has acted as a double-edged sword. With interest rates and money supply fluctuating to support industrial growth, the excess cash has flooded into the real estate sector rather than productive investment. This has sent housing and rental prices in Seoul soaring to levels that are unsustainable for the average family.

It is my view that the current economic management is dangerously unbalanced. By prioritizing export metrics over the health of the domestic economy, the country has become an "export-only" ship without a ballast. If the global semiconductor cycle turns or geopolitical tensions disrupt supply chains, the lack of a strong, healthy domestic consumer base will leave South Korea uniquely vulnerable to a severe systemic collapse. Unless policymakers pivot to prioritize wage growth and local consumption, this "record boom" will be remembered as a missed opportunity that widened the nation’s social divide.
