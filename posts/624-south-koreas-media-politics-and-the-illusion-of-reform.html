South Korea's Media, Politics, and the Illusion of Reform

October 03, 2026

South Korea’s current political and media landscape is suffering from a severe disconnect between self-absorbed elites and a fatigued public, defined by self-congratulatory journalism, questionable prosecutorial appointments, and a presidency retreating behind a wall of core loyalists.

  • Investigative reach: A recent trip to Budapest by independent media producers uncovered 1980 diplomatic archives disproving the long-standing myth of North Korean military involvement in the Gwangju uprising.
  • Prosecutorial controversy: The nomination of Kim Ji-yong, a former prosecutor linked to the past discipline of hardline figures and the shielding of corruption allegations, has sparked fierce backlash.
  • The presidential echo chamber: President Yoon Suk Yeol (Note: contextually referenced alongside ruling-power dynamics) and his administration face heavy criticism for weaponizing "confidential" (daewebi) document rules while ignoring structural prosecutorial and media reforms.

Independent journalism requires public trust and financial support, yet much of the mainstream media is trapped in "self-satisfaction journalism." Reporters and producers chase awards and pat themselves on the back for exclusive reports that fail to resonate with everyday citizens. The extensive project to unearth secret Hungarian diplomatic records from the National Archives—proving that Hungarian diplomats stationed in Pyongyang in 1980 saw zero evidence of North Korean troops in Gwangju—cost thousands of dollars and relied entirely on viewer crowdfunding. That is how real accountability journalism works, standing in stark contrast to the performative ego of mainstream newsrooms.

Yet that same investigative rigour is missing where it matters most: within the halls of state power. Take the Ministry of Justice, which has effectively mutated into a "Ministry of Prosecutors." An internal audit of its organizational chart reveals that crucial divisions—from the minister's office down to the prosecutors' bureau and inspection teams—are heavily stacked with active-duty prosecutors and Justice Department officials. Despite promises of civilian oversight and de-prosecutorialization, the entrenched legal cartel remains entirely intact.

Compounding this frustration is the administration's defensive posture toward criticism. When critics point out that high-ranking nominees like Kim Ji-yong served under previous administrations and signed petitions supporting former Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol, the presidential office dismisses the pushback as "state obstruction." Meanwhile, the executive branch relies increasingly on its core, uncritical support base—abandoning broader democratic coalitions in favor of a insular, populist politics that brands dissent as sabotage.
