The Ethics Crisis at the Heart of South Korea’s Legal Institutions

October 06, 2026

South Korea’s legal and political landscape is facing a dual crisis of credibility, as top officials in both the prosecution and the judiciary are embroiled in scandals involving hypocrisy, potential corruption, and the manipulation of public funds.

  • The Prosecution: Kim Ji-yong, a nominee for the head of the newly formed Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA), is facing backlash for his past efforts to block prosecution reform while serving as a senior prosecutor.
  • The Judiciary: Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is under fire for allegedly manipulating government expense receipts to cover personal dining costs, leading him to skip a scheduled parliamentary audit.
  • Accountability Gap: Both cases highlight a systemic issue where officials protect their institutional "cartels" while avoiding the very transparency they are expected to uphold.

The "Reformed" Prosecutor with a Controversial Past

Kim Ji-yong has been nominated to lead the MCIA—an agency born out of the push to reform the historically powerful South Korean prosecution service. However, critics point out a glaring irony: in 2022, Kim was a vocal opponent of the exact reforms he is now expected to champion. As a senior official at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, he fought tooth and nail against efforts to separate investigative and prosecutorial powers, arguing they would harm crime victims.

Kim’s current pivot to "reformist" has been met with skepticism. He claims he had internal disagreements with his superiors in the past, yet he offers no documentary evidence—such as formal objections (a mechanism available to prosecutors)—to support these claims. Critics argue that his attempt to rebrand himself without offering any apology or explanation for his past conduct is a profound act of gaslighting.

Furthermore, Kim is linked to allegations that he obstructed the forensic analysis of a smartphone belonging to former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon during high-profile investigations. While Kim denies involvement due to his position at the time, opponents argue that his silence and failure to secure resources for the investigative team constitute professional negligence. The upcoming confirmation hearing is expected to be a test of whether the National Assembly will hold him to account or allow his past allegiances to be swept under the rug.

The Judiciary’s Expense Scandal

Meanwhile, the credibility of the judiciary has taken a hit with revelations regarding Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Reports indicate that Cho’s office allegedly falsified expense receipts, splitting large meal bills into smaller amounts to bypass spending caps. When the reports surfaced, Cho—who had previously positioned himself as a champion of judicial independence—abruptly canceled his appearance at a National Assembly audit.

The incident has deepened public distrust in the judiciary. Critics argue that this is not merely a clerical error but a symptom of a culture where high-ranking officials treat public funds as their personal coffers. With the Supreme Court also currently deliberating high-stakes cases involving political figures, including First Lady Kim Keon-hee, there is growing concern that the court is stalling to avoid political fallout.

The situation has reignited calls to abolish the National Court Administration (NCA), the body responsible for judicial logistics. Detractors argue that the NCA acts as a hub for political maneuvering and "cartel-building" among judges. For many, the refusal of the main opposition Democratic Party to aggressively push for the NCA's abolition is seen as a sign of their own timidity, as they fear antagonizing the judges who hold power over their own potential future legal battles.

Ultimately, these scandals underscore a sobering truth: when the institutions tasked with upholding the law appear to treat ethics as optional, the public trust that forms the bedrock of justice begins to crumble.
