Police Accused of Botching Probe Into Conservative Politician's Amazon E-Book

October 13, 2026

South Korean police are facing heavy scrutiny for allegedly burying a copyright investigation involving Han Dong-hoon, a high-profile lawmaker from the conservative People Power Party, after a parliamentary audit revealed glaring oversights in how the case was handled.

  • What happened: During a parliamentary audit session, opposition lawmaker Park Joo-min of the liberal Democratic Party exposed that police dropped a probe into an Amazon e-book linked to Han by falsely claiming it generated zero profit.
  • The evidence: Police argued the book cost $0.99 and incurred equal platform fees, leaving no commercial intent—yet basic checks show Amazon's Kindle self-publishing tier grants authors a 35% royalty.
  • Who is affected: The scandal places the national police force under intense pressure for perceived political bias, while demanding fresh scrutiny on whether law enforcement shields prominent figures from legal accountability.

How Police Looked the Other Way on a Basic E-Book Structure

The core controversy centers on whether the material in question violated copyright laws for commercial gain. Copyright infringement in South Korea generally requires a formal complaint from the victim to trigger a police investigation—unless the act was done for commercial purposes, which allows authorities to initiate a probe independently. Police previously used the argument of "no profit" to bypass this exception and drop the matter entirely.

Yet critics point out that this defense falls apart with minimal effort. Anyone spending thirty minutes browsing Amazon's author guidelines would instantly recognize the standard 35% royalty payout on Kindle digital publications. Dismissing the case without checking basic publishing metrics raises serious questions about whether investigators deliberately looked the other way to avoid crossing a powerful politician.

Why This Forces a Reckoning for South Korea's Investigative Agencies

In our view, this episode is a classic symptom of a broader institutional malaise where public agencies bend over backward to accommodate political power. Investigators tried to hide behind procedural excuses like international cooperation hurdles, but the exposure of actual revenue structures during a high-profile audit leaves them with zero cover.

Law enforcement must now drop its protective instincts and open a proper re-investigation. If agencies continue to drag their feet, it only confirms public suspicions that the scales of justice remain rigged depending on whose name is attached to the file.
