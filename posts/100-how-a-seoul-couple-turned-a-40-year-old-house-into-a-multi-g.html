How a Seoul Couple Turned a 40-Year-Old House into a Multi-Generational Masterpiece

September 27, 2026

  • The Project: A university professor couple in Gwangjin-gu, Seoul, sold their apartment to completely gut and remodel a 40-year-old multi-family home into a multi-generational living space named "Yeohunjae."
  • The Cost: The total renovation came to about $300,000 (400 million won), complicated by strict mortgage rules for standalone houses that left them with zero bank financing.
  • The Design: The home features distinct generational separation—separate floors for parents and daughter, an external staircase, and a newly permitted third-floor rooftop extension functioning as an art studio.
  • The Takeaway: While apartment living offers uniform convenience, deep-diving into old-house remodeling demands heavy cash reserves, structural reinforcements like H-beams, and an acceptance that spatial separation can actually strengthen family ties.

Urban neighborhoods in Seoul often juxtapose decades of architectural history within a single alleyway, where 1980s red-brick houses sit shoulder-to-shoulder with modern builds. Turning these aging structures into modern homes is increasingly popular, but as one recent project in Gwangjin-gu shows, the glossy finished product hides a grueling gauntlet of structural hurdles and ballooning budgets.

The core struggle of overhauling a 40-year-old property lies underneath the surface. Once contractors strip away the interior finishes, hidden issues like sagging frames and compromised foundations routinely emerge. For this project, extensive structural reinforcement using H-beams was mandatory just to stabilize the base before any aesthetic upgrades could begin. Compounding the physical labor, strict South Korean zoning and building regulations—such as permitting a third-floor vertical extension—required professional architecture and engineering oversight, pushing project expenses far past initial estimates.

Space to Breathe: Why Separation Saves Family Relationships

Perhaps the most striking element of the home is its uncompromising generational layout. The first floor serves as the couple's domain, the second floor belongs to their daughter, and the third-floor acts as a shared research studio. Notably, the staircase leading to the second floor is entirely outdoors—meaning family members must brave the elements to visit one another.

It sounds harsh, but the owners deliberately chose this friction after years of living in a spacious apartment where overlapping schedules and cramped routines created quiet tension. By intentionally carving out physical distance, the family achieved a rare balance: the closeness of living near one another combined with the independence of studio living. In a post-pandemic era where home doubles as a workplace, having distinct, impenetrable boundaries under one roof has proven to be the ultimate luxury.
