South Korea's Ruling Party Pushes to Impeach Chief Justice, Citing "Judicial Coup"

October 16, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party is moving toward a historic impeachment vote against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, accusing the nation's top judge of plotting to strip President Lee Jae-myung of his political rights and derail future elections.

  • The Move: Senior ruling party figures, led by Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok, say an impeachment motion is nearly ready for party adoption, backed by 112 lawmakers from the pan-ruling bloc.
  • The Allegations: The Democratic Party claims the judiciary, under Cho, is intentionally delaying case reviews and manipulating bench assignments to issue politically motivated rulings before Cho's retirement in June 2027.
  • The Impact: If the National Assembly passes the impeachment motion, the Chief Justice's powers would be immediately suspended while the Constitutional Court reviews the case, halting what the party terms a "judicial coup."

A Preemptive Strike Against the Courts

The Democratic Party argues that impeachment is not a political stunt, but a necessary constitutional defense. Tensions have boiled over regarding the Supreme Court's handling of judicial assignments and delayed rulings—most notably a five-month stall on a judge recusal motion involving a lower court panel led by Judge Lee Jin-kwan. Critics within the ruling camp claim these delays are deliberate maneuvers designed to isolate independent judges and clear the path for a guilty ruling against President Lee under the Public Official Election Act.

With Cho's mandatory retirement age of 70 set to force him out in June 2027—a timeline overlapping with the next presidential election cycle—the ruling party fears the judiciary is preparing a final, disruptive intervention. Because the Democratic Party holds enough seats in the National Assembly to initiate and pass an impeachment motion independently, the warning shot from party leadership signals that the legislature is prepared to use its constitutional check on judicial power.

The Stakes for South Korean Democracy

Under South Korea's constitution, passing an impeachment motion in parliament immediately suspends the target official's duties pending a Constitutional Court verdict. For the ruling bloc, freezing Chief Justice Cho's powers is the primary objective, as it would instantly strip him of administrative control over judge appointments and case distribution.

Critics of the push warn that targeting the head of the judiciary risks severe institutional friction. However, supporters of the impeachment argue that letting the judiciary weaponize legal procedures to overturn democratic election results poses an existential threat to the republic. As the standoff moves from political rhetoric toward formal parliamentary action, the core battle lines are drawn over whether the courts can remain an independent arbiter or whether legislative intervention is required to stop alleged political overreach.
