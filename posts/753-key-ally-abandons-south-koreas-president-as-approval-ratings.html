Key Ally Abandons South Korea's President as Approval Ratings Slide

October 06, 2026

A prominent South Korean economist and former key supporter has publicly withdrawn his backing of President Lee Jae-myung, accusing the administration of abandoning its reform agenda as national approval ratings fall to 43%.

    What happened: Professor Choi Bae-keun renounced his support for President Lee, stating that the administration had fundamentally changed and warning that his promised vision of a prosperous society has failed to materialize. The context: Recent polling shows President Lee's approval rating dropping to 43%, with a 54.1% disapproval rating, as the ruling Democratic Party faces growing criticism over policy direction and political appointments. The fallout: Rather than addressing the policy critiques, the administration and its leadership—including Democratic Party floor figures—have labeled internal critics with dismissive terms like "irresponsible extremists," sparking accusations of stifling debate. Who is affected: The progressive coalition and core supporters are increasingly fractured, raising questions about the ruling party's readiness for upcoming political battles against a resurgent conservative opposition.

A Progressive Betrayal and the Echo Chamber of Power

Professor Choi's defection is more than just a routine political falling-out. As one of the most vocal advocates who urged voters to trust President Lee during the election, Choi's public confession that "I was deceived" cuts deep into the administration's moral authority. When the architects of a reform movement turn around and declare that the government has warped beyond recognition, it signals a profound crisis of trust. Instead of engaging with these substantive criticisms, the presidential office and party loyalists have resorted to creating labels like the "no-extremism faction" to dismiss dissenting voices as selfish or disruptive.

This defensive reflex points to a deeper institutional rot within the ruling Democratic Party, often criticized under the leadership of figures like Kim Min-seok for prioritizing internal factional positioning over legislative competence. By systematically pushing critics out of their political inner circle and treating any drop in dissent as proof of a pure, unwavering core—even as total public support shrinks—the administration risks creating a dangerous echo chamber. Democracy relies on the uncomfortable friction of debate; when a ruling camp replaces policy verification with loyalty tests, it blinds itself to the rising tides of public discontent.
