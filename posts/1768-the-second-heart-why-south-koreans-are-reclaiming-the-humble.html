The "Second Heart": Why South Koreans are Reclaiming the Humble Foot Bath for Modern Wellness

October 31, 2026

In a society increasingly burdened by high-cost health supplements and chronic burnout, South Koreans are returning to a simple, scientifically backed ritual: the 15-minute foot bath as a primary tool for managing immunity and cardiovascular health.

  • Medical validation: Specialists at major institutions, including the Ilsan Cha Hospital Integrated Cancer Center, are prescribing foot baths to help chemotherapy patients manage stress and bolster natural defense mechanisms.
  • Circulatory efficiency: Known as the "second heart," the feet are the furthest point from the cardiac center; warming them is proven to lower sympathetic nervous system activity and improve oxygen delivery to the brain.
  • Cardiovascular safety: Unlike full-body baths, which can dangerously spike blood pressure, foot baths (partial baths) are being promoted as a safer thermal therapy for the elderly and those with heart conditions.
  • Standardized protocol: Health experts are popularizing a specific routine involving pre-hydration, a 37-40°C temperature range, and post-bath "thermal locking" with socks to maximize metabolic benefits.

The Science of "Cool Head, Warm Feet"

At the heart of this trend is the traditional Korean medical concept of Duhan-jokyeol—literally "cool head, warm feet." While it sounds like ancient folk wisdom, modern South Korean medical research is providing the data to back it up. A 2010 study in the Journal of the Korean Academy of Rehabilitation Medicine found that foot baths temporarily suppress the sympathetic nervous system, which is responsible for the "fight or flight" response. For the overworked salaryman or the student in Seoul’s competitive academic environment, this provides a physiological "reset" that pharmaceutical interventions often fail to replicate.

The rise of the foot bath also reflects a shift in South Korea’s "wellness" economy. As inflation bites, consumers are pivoting away from expensive therapy sessions toward "home-care" routines that cost nearly nothing. Experts like Hong Seong-eun, an Oriental medicine specialist, argue that the most effective health habits are those that are sustainable and low-barrier. By treating the feet as a gateway to the entire circulatory system, Koreans are finding a way to mitigate the effects of a high-stress lifestyle without a significant financial investment.

Risk Management and the "Wellness Ritual"

However, the transition from a simple wash to a medical "ritual" requires specific precautions. For the aging population—a critical demographic in South Korea—the distinction between a full-body soak and a foot bath is vital. Full-body immersion in hot water can put a sudden load on the heart, but foot baths offer the same vasodilatory benefits with minimal cardiac strain. This makes it an ideal entry point for those with hypertension or histories of myocardial infarction, provided they monitor for signs of dizziness or excessive skin flushing.

Ultimately, the foot bath is being rebranded not just as hygiene, but as a "self-care ceremony." The inclusion of aromatherapy oils like lavender or lemongrass and the emphasis on acupressure points—such as the Yongcheon-hyeol (the "gushing spring" point on the sole)—transforms a mundane task into a psychological break. In a country where the "ppalli-ppalli" (hurry-hurry) culture often leads to physical exhaustion, the mandatory 15 minutes of stillness required for a foot bath may be its most potent medicinal quality of all.
