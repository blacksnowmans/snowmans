Political Clashes and Internal Fractures Leave South Korea’s Housing Policy in Chaos Ahead of Elections

November 12, 2026

South Korea’s real estate policy has devolved into political warfare as rising rents and home prices trigger open rebellion within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and aggressive deregulation attacks from the conservative opposition ahead of upcoming general elections.

  • Ruling party revolt: Sixteen metropolitan lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party formed a "real estate pragmatism" group to push for easing housing regulations, highlighting deep internal divisions over proposed property tax cuts that critics note would heavily favor high-end Seoul neighborhoods.
  • Opposition deregulation push: The main opposition People Power Party invited former economic chief Choi Kyoung-hwan to attack strict loan limits—such as the 40% loan-to-value (LTV) cap—while lawmaker Ahn Cheol-soo demanded the total repeal of a tax aimed at clawing back up to 2 trillion won (about $1.5 billion) in urban reconstruction profits.
  • Presidential intervention: President Lee Jae-myung hosted party leaders for dinner to enforce party-government alignment, as the party's think tank launched a real estate task force to reconcile conflicting tax proposals.
  • Impact on residents: Millions of renters and home-seekers in the Seoul metropolitan area face worsening housing affordability and market confusion as political factions prioritize election calculations over coherent housing strategy.

Election Pressure Fractures the Ruling Party’s Housing Line

The sudden surge in pragmatism among ruling Democratic Party lawmakers reflects mounting panic over voter backlash in the crucial Seoul metropolitan area. With monthly rents soaring and lease inventory drying up, 16 lawmakers launched a group aimed at stabilizing the rental market rather than relying strictly on market-suppression measures. However, proposed compromises—such as extending a Comprehensive Real Estate Tax relief threshold of 1.4 billion won (about $1 million) to non-resident single-home owners—have exposed severe internal contradictions. Data shows that roughly 85 percent of the benefits from such relief would flow directly to affluent Seoul districts like Gangnam, Mapo, Yongsan, and Seongdong, directly contradicting the administration's core commitment to curbing asset inequality.

Opposition Capitalizes on Regulatory Fatigue

Capitalizing on public fatigue over stringent regulations, the main opposition People Power Party has launched an aggressive campaign to dismantle key property controls. By hosting former Deputy Prime Minister Choi Kyoung-hwan—famous for his past expansionary housing policies—the opposition is framing strict mortgage limits as an unfair barrier preventing middle-class families from buying homes. Meanwhile, opposition attacks on urban reconstruction clawback taxes highlight genuine policy contradictions, given that the government warns of falling property values while simultaneously attempting to collect tax on anticipated redevelopment gains. As President Lee Jae-myung attempts to enforce unity across his party, South Korea's real estate market remains trapped in uncertainty, dictated far more by short-term electoral strategy than long-term economic stability.
