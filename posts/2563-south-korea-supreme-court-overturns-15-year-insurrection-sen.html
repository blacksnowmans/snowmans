South Korea Supreme Court Overturns 15-Year Insurrection Sentence for Ex-Prime Minister Han Duck-soo

November 20, 2026

On November 19, 2026, South Korea’s Supreme Court overturned a 15-year prison sentence for former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo over his role in the December 2024 martial law crisis, remanding the case to a lower court with instructions that his actions prior to the decree were lawful.

  • What happened: The Supreme Court, South Korea's highest court, ruled 9-3 en banc that Han’s pre-declaration conduct—such as advising a Cabinet meeting and assembling a quorum—constituted a legitimate exercise of official duty rather than treasonous intent.
  • The dissent: Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyoung-mi delivered a fierce dissent, arguing the majority relied on a "logic of impunity" that ignored how self-coups adopt formal administrative procedures as weapons of insurrection.
  • What it changes: The decision vacates Han’s previous convictions—he was sentenced to 23 years at his initial trial and 15 years on appeal—and sets a legal precedent that separates procedural preparation for martial law from its illegal execution.
  • Who is affected: Han faces a retrial at the Seoul High Court, while critics warn the decision lowers criminal accountability for top officials who facilitate unconstitutional orders under bureaucratic pretexts.

A Slice-and-Dice Approach to Treason

The majority centered its ruling on constitutional provisions requiring Cabinet deliberation before declaring martial law. In the court's view, Han’s actions between 9:29 PM and 10:18 PM on December 3, 2024—urgently summoning ministers to reach a legal quorum—were attempts to follow mandatory procedures. By drawing a line at the exact moment martial law was declared (10:18 PM), the justices concluded that Han's prior actions lacked clear intent to disrupt the constitutional order.

This artificial timeline drew sharp criticism from the court's three dissenting justices. Justice Oh Kyoung-mi emphasized that self-coups naturally masquerade as routine state administration. By treating procedural compliance as a defense, she argued, the majority failed to see that quorums and signatures act as tools of rebellion just like military force. Oh also noted that an earlier March 2025 Constitutional Court ruling dismissing Han's impeachment—which the majority cited—took place before presidential CCTV footage exposed how single-mindedly Han worked to assemble the meeting.

Procedure Over Democratic Protection

The ruling highlights a controversial shift in how South Korea's judiciary handles state crimes. In past landmark cases, such as a 2014 insurrection conspiracy trial, the court applied strict standards against even ideological incitement. Yet in Han’s case, where armed troops actually stormed the National Assembly before being halted by unarmed citizens, the court granted broad leniency to the cabinet chief who provided administrative backing for the attempt.

While Yoon Suk Yeol remains imprisoned following his historic April 2025 impeachment and removal from office, the Supreme Court’s logic risks isolating senior executive aides from criminal liability. By shielding formal compliance from the realities of anti-constitutional acts, the court has established a dangerous line of defense for officials who assist in subverting democracy.
