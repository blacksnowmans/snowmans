Global Markets Briefing: AI's Reality Check, Plunging Oil, and South Korea's Structural Stagnation

November 10, 2026

Global markets are attempting a fragile rebound led by a recovery in semiconductor stocks and falling oil prices, but beneath the surface, a brutal reality check is underway as physical energy bottlenecks and regulatory threats loom over the artificial intelligence sector.

  • The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index has reclaimed the 10,000-point mark over two consecutive days of sharp gains, easing panic across South Korean and U.S. markets.
  • WTI crude oil has plummeted below $90 to $86.70 per dollar, driven by easing Middle East geopolitical tensions and cooling inflation pressures.
  • Regulatory crackdowns on AI data centers are intensifying in Washington, led by proposed legislation targeting massive power consumption and threatening tech margins.
  • South Korea's capital market continues to struggle with structural inefficiencies, bureaucratic oversight, and a banking-centric financial culture that penalizes innovation.

The End of Hype and the Demand for Hard Numbers

The recent market bounce offers a welcome reprieve from the brutal tech sell-offs of previous days, but it should not be mistaken for a return to unchecked optimism. The market has shifted its fundamental question: investors are no longer asking who is building the most AI chips, but rather whether language model developers and major tech firms can translate massive capital expenditures into actual, tangible revenue growth. As startups like Anthropic prepare to test public markets, the entire AI thesis hinges on proving concrete financial returns rather than speculative potential.

At the same time, the macroeconomic backdrop has shifted in favor of energy relief. West Texas Intermediate oil dropping below $90 and the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) plunging back below 20 indicate that extreme market panic is subsiding. For South Korea—an economy heavily dependent on energy imports and semiconductor exports—falling oil prices provide vital breathing room. Yet, this relief is counterbalanced by a looming regulatory storm in the United States, where proposed measures like the BAAJAA Act and renewed scrutiny on data center power usage threaten to constrain the physical expansion of AI infrastructure.

Infrastructure Bottlenecks and South Korea's Regulatory Blind Spots

The core vulnerability of the current AI boom is no longer algorithmic capability, but the severe physical mismatch between the speed of software development and the reality of power grids. Data centers require staggering amounts of electricity and water for cooling, and political backlash against rising household energy costs is giving rise to legislative moratoria in the U.S. While this infrastructure squeeze poses a margin risk for Big Tech, it has created a massive backlog for South Korean heavy electrical equipment makers supplying transformers and grid components. However, South Korea's domestic financial ecosystem remains ill-equipped to nurture future-facing industries, weighed down by a rigid banking-centric mindset, bureaucratic exchange oversight, and outdated tax-focused policymaking.
