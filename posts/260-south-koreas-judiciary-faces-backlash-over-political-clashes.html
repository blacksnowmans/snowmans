South Korea’s Judiciary Faces Backlash Over Political Clashes and Misused Expenses

September 29, 2026

South Korea’s top court is facing an unprecedented storm of criticism as Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae battles allegations of political overreach and the misuse of official expense accounts.

    What happened: The Supreme Court and the presidential office clashed over the appointment of a new Supreme Court justice, while separate parliamentary probes revealed that top judges allegedly inflated meal headcounts to bypass corporate card spending limits.
  • Who is affected: Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the National Court Administration, and public trust in South Korea’s independent judicial branch.
  • What it changes: The scandals threaten to strip the judiciary of its moral authority, transforming a constitutionally protected independent branch into a target for corruption investigations.

A Clash of Powers and Questionable Spending

The controversy first erupted when the presidential office rejected the appointment of Judge Son Bong-ki to the Supreme Court, officially requesting the court to restart the nomination process. Rather than complying, the National Court Administration offered evasive responses during a parliamentary Legislation and Judiciary Committee hearing. Officials twisted clear instructions into ambiguous interpretations, prompting accusations that Chief Justice Cho is attempting to veto the executive branch's constitutional appointment powers and turn the judiciary into an independent political faction.

Compounding the institutional friction are explosive revelations regarding official expense accounts. Opposition lawmakers exposed that Supreme Court justices routinely inflated attendance figures for meals at high-end restaurants in neighborhoods like Bangbae-dong. By recording physical capacities that were mathematically impossible—such as claiming 38 attendees in rooms that could not hold them—and splitting expensive meals into smaller increments, officials allegedly manipulated receipts to bypass corporate card spending caps. Critics argue this amounts to using taxpayer money as a private slush fund for gourmet dining.

Erosion of Judicial Independence

In our view, these developments reveal a dangerous drift within South Korea’s legal establishment. Judicial independence is designed to protect judges from political pressure, not to grant the court a blank check to operate as an unaccountable sanctuary. When the head of the judiciary reportedly spars with the executive over appointments while allegedly doctoring restaurant receipts to hide luxury meals, the line between protecting the rule of law and defending personal privilege is completely erased.

These actions go far beyond administrative errors; they point to a systemic rot and a profound sense of entitlement among elite legal figures. If South Korea’s highest court expects citizens to respect the law, its leaders must be held to the same strict ethical standards. Without a transparent investigation into these expense irregularities and an accounting of this political posturing, the judiciary risks losing the public trust it relies on to function.
