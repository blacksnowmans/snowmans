South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Risky Centrist Pivot

November 05, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is alienating his progressive base and weakening his governing momentum by chasing an illusory "structural majority" across the political center instead of delivering tangible economic relief to ordinary citizens.

  • A flawed political diagnosis: After securing 49.42% of the vote following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the administration has concluded that the liberal base alone cannot guarantee reelection, prompting a deliberate shift toward moderate and conservative voters.
  • A narrowing lead: Recent polling shows the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea slipping to 40.6%, leading the main conservative opposition People Power Party (39.5%) by just 1.1 percentage points as progressive voters grow disillusioned over stalled judicial and economic reforms.
  • An illusory honeymoon: The administration’s early approval spikes and a surging KOSPI stock index were largely powered by a global AI-driven semiconductor boom—an external export windfall—rather than substantive domestic policy wins.
  • Persistent inequality: Over 90% of working South Koreans remain trapped under stagnant real wages, with median post-tax earnings at just 2.18 million won (about $1,600) per month and a distorted tax deduction regime that heavily favors top earners.

Chasing the Center at the Expense of the Base

President Lee’s call to build an all-inclusive coalition reflects an old political temptation: attempting to broaden appeal by shedding core ideological commitments. Fearing that relying solely on his progressive base will leave the ruling party as a "structural minority," the presidential office has sidelined vocal reformists and slowed promised crackdowns on entrenched interests. Yet South Korean political history shows that discarding the very activists who stood on the front lines during political crises does not win over the opposition; it merely breeds cynicism on both sides. Moderates perceive hesitation as weakness, while loyalists feel abandoned.

The administration's obsession with crossing the 50% threshold misreads modern South Korean electoral dynamics. In a competitive multiparty environment, liberal presidents rarely claim outright majorities—even former president Moon Jae-in entered office with 41% of the vote. Treating a 49.4% victory as a mandate to purge the base’s influence erodes the political engine required to pass contested legislation, leaving the Democratic Party vulnerable in key metropolitan and regional battlegrounds.

Stock Market Euphoria Masks Working-Class Pain

The White House’s pivot to technocratic, pro-growth pragmatism was supercharged by temporary good fortune. When global tech demand lifted Korean chip exports and pushed the KOSPI to new highs, presidential aides claimed credit, framing the surge as proof that market-friendly policies could win over the public. But export windfalls do not trickle down into the domestic economy. Annual GDP growth has slowed across successive administrations to around 2%, while household consumption and real wages have stagnated even faster. Today, roughly 80% of small business owners earn less than the minimum wage, averaging around 1.44 million won (about $1,050) a month.

The broader failure lies in a broken redistribution mechanism. South Korea’s tax code allows roughly 410 trillion won (about $300 billion)—nearly 40% of total reported income—to vanish into deductions, offering disproportionate tax relief to the wealthiest 0.1% while social transfer payments lag behind international standards. After four separate Democratic administrations over the past quarter-century, low- and middle-income households still report little measurable improvement in their financial security. Unless the Lee administration uses its mandate to overhaul tax deductions and deliver direct economic support to the bottom 90%, its centrist pivot will end neither with a durable majority nor a lasting economic legacy.
