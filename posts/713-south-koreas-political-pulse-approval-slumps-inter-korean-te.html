South Korea's Political Pulse: Approval Slumps, Inter-Korean Tensions, and the Race for 2026

October 05, 2026

  • Approval rating slips: President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating fell 0.5 percentage points to 37.4%, with disapproval hitting 59.8% in a recent Realmeter poll, while vehement opposition ("very bad") reached a record 49.0% on automated surveys.
  • Cross-poll divergence: Major polling outfits show conflicting numbers for the ruling Democratic Party—ranging from a narrow lead over the conservative People Power Party to a wider margin—reflecting a deeply polarized electorate.
  • Inter-Korean friction: A Demilitarized Zone landmine explosion has sparked a war of words, with analysts suggesting North Korea's calculated use of the term "Seoul" signals posturing ahead of potential US-North Korea summits.
  • Early presidential contenders: Early preference polls show a three-way race led by figures like Kim Min-seok on the progressive side, while conservative surveys are heavily anchored by former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon.

South Korea's political landscape is flashing warning signs for the progressive camp. Despite sitting in power following the trajectories of past liberal administrations, the current mood among supporters resembles the anxious burden of marrying off an only daughter rather than celebrating a political victory. This bittersweet sentiment reflects a deeper anxiety within the ruling bloc: an electorate that voted for change is growing weary, fatigued by personnel controversies, uninspiring follow-ups, and a rigid adherence to defensive political habits.

Meanwhile, the opposition People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, is quietly tightening its ranks. While progressive voters fracture across an expanding pool of prospective presidential hopefuls—including Kim Min-seok, Cho Kuk, and Jeong Cheong-rae—conservatives are consolidating around figures like Han Dong-hoon and Seoul Mayor Oh Sehoon. As polling methods reveal stark divides between enthusiastic conservative respondents and cautious progressives, the governing party can no longer rely on coasting through political inertia.
