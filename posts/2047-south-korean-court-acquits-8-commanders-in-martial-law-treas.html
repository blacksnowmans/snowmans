South Korean Court Acquits 8 Commanders in Martial Law Treason Trial

November 06, 2026

A South Korean court has acquitted eight former military commanders charged with playing key roles in the December 3 martial law declaration, ruling that blindly following orders without understanding the illegal intent does not constitute treason.

  • What happened: The Seoul Central District Court acquitted eight military officers—including generals who attended a pre-coup meeting at a fast-food outlet—finding no proven intent to disrupt the constitutional order.
  • What it changes: The verdict establishes a controversial judicial precedent where field commanders who deployed armed troops to constitutional institutions can escape legal accountability by claiming military obedience.
  • Who is affected: High-level orchestrators like former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun face decades in prison, while the operational commanders who executed directives on the ground walk free.
  • Broader context: The ruling comes amid rising foreign policy tensions over government plans to expand the Cheonghae naval unit's operational radius near the Strait of Hormuz.

A Dangerous Precedent for Military Obedience

The "Hamburger Meeting Case" centered on an extraordinary gathering at a Lotteria fast-food restaurant in Ansan hours before the December 3 martial law announcement. Active-duty generals met Noh Sang-won, a retired former intelligence commander acting as a civilian, who outlined plans to form an unauthorized investigation unit targeting the National Election Commission. Despite the illegal nature of a civilian issuing directives to active officers, the court accepted the defense that the commanders were merely "annoyed" and passively obeying their chain of command without grasping the treasonous scheme.

This decision creates a troubling rift in South Korea's judicial accountability. While top decision-makers received severe sentences—former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun received 30 years in his first trial, and Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was sentenced to life imprisonment—the field commanders who dispatched armed units to the National Assembly were cleared. By severing high-level planning from field execution, the court has effectively handed military officers a legal defense: as long as commanders claim they were executing routine superior orders, they can enforce unconstitutional actions without criminal liability.

Security Crises at Home and Abroad

Critics, including Choo Mi-ae of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, have condemned the verdict as an encouraging signal for illegal military compliance. The court's willingness to excuse blind obedience undermines the core obligation of military officers to uphold the constitution over unlawful orders, threatening the democratic safeguards South Korea built over decades.

Simultaneously, South Korea faces delicate security choices abroad, notably plans to expand the Cheonghae naval unit's mission area near the Strait of Hormuz by over 1,000 kilometers. Adding 75 personnel, maritime patrol aircraft, and specialized equipment risks entangling South Korean forces in regional conflicts under U.S. pressure. Whether addressing internal accountability for martial law or managing overseas naval operations, South Korea's defense and legal frameworks face severe tests in maintaining constitutional integrity and strategic independence.
