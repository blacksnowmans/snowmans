South Korea's Opposition Struggles with Legal Strategy Amidst Stagnant Approval Ratings

September 03, 2026

South Korean opposition parties are increasingly resorting to reactive, high-risk political maneuvers to combat legal challenges against their leaders, but these tactics risk backfiring by undermining their own credibility and empowering the prosecution.

  • The Strategy: Opposition figures are pushing for the "cancellation of indictments" for key party members, a move critics argue ignores established judicial processes.
  • The Risk: By demanding the dismissal of legal cases without robust evidence, the opposition is providing the prosecution with ammunition to claim they are being unfairly pressured, thereby strengthening the prosecution's internal resolve.
  • The Stakes: With government approval ratings hovering in the low 30% range, both the ruling and opposition camps are entering a phase where survival, rather than policy, has become the primary political objective.

The Limits of Political Pressure

The push to cancel indictments—particularly in high-profile cases like the Daejang-dong development scandal or North Korean remittance allegations—is largely viewed as a non-starter. These matters are already deep within the judicial system, with some cases reaching the Supreme Court. Expecting the courts to simply drop these charges is a fantasy that ignores the reality of South Korea's judicial independence. As seen in the failed attempts by lawmakers like Jung Sung-ho to negotiate with prosecutors, trying to trade procedural favors for legal leniency only leads to political embarrassment and further legal setbacks.

Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive, anti-prosecution reformist party), has offered a more pragmatic critique. He argues that rather than engaging in "political whining," the opposition should adopt a rigorous legal strategy: exposing instances of prosecutorial overreach through parliamentary hearings or independent counsel investigations. Without concrete evidence to challenge the prosecution’s narrative, the opposition’s current approach risks looking like an attempt to evade the law rather than a principled stand against systemic bias.

A Double-Edged Sword in Law Reform

The proposal to amend the Public Official Election Act—specifically by removing certain language regarding "false statements"—is another dangerous gambit. While proponents hope this would clear the legal path for Democratic Party leader Lee Jae-myung, it could paradoxically lead to the dismissal of charges against President Yoon Suk-yeol as well. This creates a "Pandora’s box" scenario: a legislative tool designed to save one leader could inadvertently protect their political rival.

The fact that members of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal opposition) are now openly debating these "judicial risks" confirms a growing sense of panic. When a government’s approval drops toward the 30% floor, the executive branch loses its momentum for state affairs. For the opposition, the challenge is clear: if they continue to rely on emotional appeals rather than a disciplined, fact-based legal defense, they risk being remembered by history as a group that failed to recognize the limits of political influence in a functioning democracy.
