South Korea’s Bold 100,000-Home Plan: Supply Shock or Pipe Dream?

November 06, 2026

The government of President Lee Jae-myung has announced a massive plan to supply 100,000 new homes in the Seoul metropolitan area, signaling a hard-line effort to break the country's long-standing "real estate invincibility" myth.

  • Targeted expansion: The government will develop major residential sites just 5km from Seoul's boundary, including Gimpo, Hanam, Guri, and Uijeongbu.
  • The "4th City" scale: Gimpo’s "Hangang 2" project will see an additional 37,000 units, essentially creating a new city on the scale of previous large-scale suburban developments.
  • Infrastructure gap: Critics warn that without immediate, drastic improvements to transportation, these areas will face "residential hell" similar to the current congestion on the Gimpo Goldline metro, which currently operates at 290% capacity.
  • Long-term horizon: While the announcement aims to curb prices, experts caution that the "paperwork phase"—the initial permits—can take up to 10 years to translate into actual move-in-ready housing.

A Shift from Price Control to Supply Overload

President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is moving beyond market management, explicitly aiming to force housing prices down. This strategy marks a sharp departure from the policies of the previous administration led by the now-imprisoned Park Geun-hye and later Yoon Suk-yeol (referred to here as "Mr. Yoon" due to his removal from office). The government is betting that by saturating the market with supply near the capital, it can shatter the psychological expectation that property values will only ever rise.

Currently, the market is bifurcated. While the "Gangnam 3" districts (Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa)—the wealthiest areas in Seoul—are seeing significant price dips, other parts of the capital region remain stuck at or near record highs. By deploying this 100,000-home "supply bomb," the government is attempting to turn this localized cooling into a regional trend, even hinting that it may step in to purchase properties itself should prices collapse too rapidly, thereby securing public rental stock.

The Risk of "Residential Hell"

The reaction in cities like Gimpo has been fierce. Residents, who have already weathered years of under-serviced growth, see this new injection of housing as a "bolt from the blue." The Gimpo Goldline is notorious for being a "hell train," where overcrowding is so severe that passengers frequently lose consciousness during commutes. Adding 37,000 households to an area that cannot currently support its existing population is being viewed by many as state-mandated misery rather than urban development.

The core issue remains the disconnect between planning and reality. While the government points to future transportation projects—such as the GTX-A high-speed rail and extended subway lines—these projects are plagued by budget delays, safety scandals, and bureaucratic infighting. In the eyes of many commuters, promising rail service a decade from now while forcing dense construction today is a hollow gesture. For this supply plan to be anything more than a political maneuver, the government must prioritize the "quality of life" infrastructure before the first brick is laid. Without a radical fix for the transit nightmare, these new developments risk becoming high-density islands of frustration.
