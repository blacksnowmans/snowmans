South Korea's Democratic Party is Bleeding Core Supporters in a Misguided Quest for the Center

August 30, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's ruling Democratic Party is facing a severe polling slump, with approval ratings hovering near 50% despite a new party leadership under Kim Min-seok, and core support from voters in their 40s and 50s splintering into the uncommitted "no party preference" block.
  • What it changes: The party's strategy of chasing mythical centrist voters—dubbed the "New ABC Strategy"—is backfiring by alienating its traditional progressive base and halting key reform legislation like the bill on fabricated prosecutorial indictments under the guise of prioritizing public livelihood.
  • Who is affected: The Democratic Party leadership, core progressives feeling betrayed, and working-class voters who are seeing political survival tactics replace genuine grassroots engagement.

The Numbers Don't Lie: Core Bases are Walking Away

Political polling in South Korea often hides subtle shifts, but the current data surrounding the Democratic Party tells an unmistakable story of self-inflicted decline. The 40s and 50s demographic—long considered the unbreakable, concrete bedrock of progressive power—is slipping. Approval among these age groups is barely clinging to a 52% baseline. Rather than crossing over to the conservative People Power Party, these disillusioned voters are dropping out altogether, swelling the ranks of the politically homeless. Over 12% of progressive respondents now report having no preferred party, a silent protest against a leadership they no longer trust.

Compounding this is the absence of any traditional honeymoon bounce for new party leader Kim Min-seok, whose approval sits split at 50% positive and 37% negative. When a leadership change fails to energize even the party's own internal machinery, it signals a deep disconnect. Meanwhile, the leadership continues to push an airy "New ABC Strategy" focused on public livelihood, broad appeal, and competence—metaphorically shopping for a new apartment while the living room floorboards are actively on fire. Chasing elusive moderates while your own base's loyalty burns to ash is not political expansion; it is abandonment.

Reform Delayed is Reform Denied

The party's woes are further compounded by legislative timidity. Voters handed the Democratic Party a commanding mandate specifically to dismantle systemic prosecutorial overreach and enact aggressive reforms. Yet, leadership keeps pushing back crucial measures like the special bill on fabricated indictments, hiding behind the shield of "livelihood first." This perpetual cycle of reading the room, panicking at media pushback, and retreating when public friction occurs has left core supporters exhausted and furious. Using everyday economic hardships as an excuse to stall structural justice is a fast track to losing the moral authority required to govern.

Ultimately, a political party cannot manufacture outward appeal while its internal house is in ruins. The Democratic Party's current fixation on top-down mergers announced via media leaks rather than consultation—such as the awkward misfire with the Rebuilding Korea Party—alongside a reliance on superficial social media messaging, shows a dangerous detachment from the street-level reality. Real politics is about keeping faith with the people who brought you to power. Until the leadership steps out of their parliamentary offices and listens to the merchants in the traditional markets rather than their own echo chambers, no amount of strategic branding will bring their base back.
