South Korea’s Political Crisis: A Deepening Crisis of Confidence

September 01, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is currently defined by a stagnant presidential approval rating stuck in the 30% range—a figure that, while technically familiar, signals a dangerous shift in the quality of the administration's support base.

  • Erosion of the base: The decline is not just in numbers; core, unconditional supporters are turning critical, signaling that the administration has lost its primary political engine.
  • Internal strife: Within the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the main opposition party, leadership changes under Representative Kim Min-seok have focused on aggressive consolidation of power rather than unification.
  • A culture of performative politics: Both the government and the opposition are increasingly viewed as prioritizing “political theater”—such as creating new task forces or media channels—over substantive policy outcomes.

The 30% Threshold: A Warning Light, Not Just a Statistic

For years, a 30% approval rating was considered the "concrete floor" for South Korean presidents—a reliable core that would hold regardless of controversy. That foundation is now cracking. The fact that the most dedicated supporters have shrunk to one-third of their peak size suggests the administration’s ability to govern is effectively running on empty. This is no longer about specific grievances over real estate or stock market volatility; it has morphed into a "veto sentiment," where voters have fundamentally lost faith in the government's ability to deliver, rendering new policy announcements irrelevant.

The DPK’s Leadership Trap

The Democratic Party is facing a similar crisis of legitimacy. Since Kim Min-seok took the helm, the party has prioritized sweeping personnel changes to solidify control, ousting key staff members within months. While the party claims these moves are for strategic strengthening, they are widely perceived as a move to crush internal dissent. By silencing moderate voices and prioritizing a "single-minded" approach, the leadership is isolating itself. Despite creating over 30 task forces and launching new media initiatives to expand its reach, these acts of "political engineering" are failing to impress a public that is weary of optics over action.

Ultimately, both the ruling and opposition parties are suffering from a "bankruptcy of trust." Leaders seem to believe that the appearance of constant activity—constant re-shuffling of roles and flashy PR—is a substitute for genuine reform. However, voters are increasingly sophisticated. They are not interested in the internal power dynamics of a party or the sheer number of committees formed. They are looking for a party that addresses the daily economic anxieties of the average citizen. Without a fundamental shift from "looking busy" to achieving tangible results, both sides risk falling into irrelevance as their perceived disconnection from reality grows.
