Why South Korea's Helicopter Parents Need to Back Off

October 10, 2026

  • Prominent South Korean pediatrician Ha Jung-hoon is warning parents that hyper-fixated, round-the-clock parenting—known locally as "all-in parenting"—is harming children by breeding entitlement and a lack of resilience.
  • The piece argues that excessive protection and a failure to set firm boundaries early in life are eroding children's self-reliance, feeding into broader social friction, and straining South Korea's education system.
  • Parents are urged to implement "interest diets"—stepping back, setting clear rules by the toddler years, and prioritizing their own happiness over total self-sacrifice.
  • The warning targets overprotective parents and families navigating intense child-rearing pressure in South Korea.

South Korean child-rearing culture has long been defined by intense devotion, where entire extended families rally around a single child and parents deploy armies of private tutors and support staff. But according to prominent pediatrician Ha Jung-hoon, this relentless focus has crossed the line from care into suffocation. In a series of recent warnings, Ha argues that treating children as the undisputed center of the universe creates a psychological obesity of entitlement, leaving them unequipped for a harsh, unyielding world.

The core issue, the commentary suggests, is a cultural aversion to letting children experience discomfort or failure. When parents cater to every whim, shield kids from negative emotions, and refuse to set hard limits during their toddler years, children fail to develop basic self-control. This modern brand of overprotection has direct real-world consequences: sheltered children enter the school system expecting to be treated like royalty, clashing with teachers and straining classrooms already grappling with a breakdown of authority.

Ultimately, the advice is simple yet radical for a society as intensely competitive as South Korea: parents need to put themselves first. By aiming to raise children who are "5% lacking"—meaning kids who face natural shortages, boundaries, and minor frustrations—parents can reclaim their own lives while teaching their children true independence. After all, as the argument goes, a child cannot learn how to navigate the jungle of society if their parents spend every waking hour paving the road ahead of them.
