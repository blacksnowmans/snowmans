Lee Jae-myung's Push for a "Structural Majority" Alarms His Own Base

October 20, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has triggered fierce pushback from his progressive base after declaring that the liberal bloc remains a "structural minority" that cannot win future elections without embracing moderates and reasonable conservatives.

  • The pivot: Speaking at a press conference, President Lee cautioned his supporters against acting like "occupiers," arguing that relying solely on the progressive camp will guarantee defeat against a united center-right opposition.
  • The pushback: Core supporters and civil society groups contend that Lee already holds a commanding mandate, having secured 17.28 million votes (49.42%) in the June 2025 election and a parliamentary super-coalition of over 180 seats led by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.
  • Stalled reforms: Critics warn that prioritizing cross-aisle appeasement will derail key institutional pledges, including sweeping prosecution overhauls and holding accountable the figures behind the unconstitutional martial law decree under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • Internal party friction: The administration’s outreach to external conservatives sharply contrasts with its treatment of internal party dissenters, who have faced loyalty tests and conspiracy-laden smears instead of genuine engagement.

Misreading the Moderate Mandate

President Lee's call for a "structural majority" relies on a questionable political diagnosis: that winning over the center requires diluting the reform agenda. Public opinion data tells a different story. The swing voters and moderates who delivered Lee’s election victory following the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol were not asking for conservative compromise—78% of self-identified moderates condemned Yoon’s martial law decree as unconstitutional, and 74% supported his removal from office. Their support was an explicit demand to restore constitutional order and purge corrupt state machinery, not an endorsement of timid governance.

With the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, reeling at historic lows near 15% support, the Lee administration enjoys a rare window of legislative leverage. Yet by preemptively citing electoral vulnerability to justify policy caution, the administration risks wasting the political capital handed to it by nearly half the electorate.

The Danger of Discarding the Core

Historically, broad-tent politics succeeds only when backed by tangible results that demonstrate governing competence. Extending an olive branch to traditional opponents while treating passionate party members as factional liabilities creates a double standard that invites backlash. When voters who stood in the cold to overturn an authoritarian power grab find their demands dismissed as "camp tribalism," disillusionment inevitably follows.

If President Lee seeks to redefine South Korea’s political map, he must do so through unapologetic, decisive reform rather than ideological hedging. Courting the center by muting the voices that put him in power does not build a sustainable majority—it merely dismantles his own foundation.
