South Korea’s Ruling Camp Faces Backlash Over Social Media Governance and Factional Feuds

October 18, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing mounting public and internal blowback as President Lee Jae-myung’s unfiltered social media habit and party leadership’s weaponization of public tragedies ignite alarm over the decay of formal state institutions.

  • Exploiting public grief: The death of a firefighter on duty was hijacked by ruling party figures, including Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok, as fuel for factional attacks against intra-party rivals like Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae, sparking disgust across the political spectrum.
  • Online intimidation campaigns: Critical voices, such as dissident economist Choi Bae-geun, have faced coordinated online character assassination by militant loyalists following direct criticisms or cues originating from the president’s personal social media accounts.
  • Scrutiny on shadow confidants: Parliamentary audits have zeroed in on the "Seongnam line"—President Lee’s longtime inner circle, including key aide Jeong Jin-sang, linked to the historic left-wing Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance—for operating as an opaque, informal power center that bypasses official government channels.
  • Voter backlash: A sharp 7-percentage-point drop in the Democratic Party’s approval ratings signals widening disaffection among moderates and grassroots party members ahead of key political milestones.

The Mechanics of Digital Retaliation

South Korean politics has long been fiercely contested, but the displacement of institutional briefings by late-night personal social media posts introduces a destabilizing new dynamic. Arbitrary executive signaling on digital platforms has replaced disciplined policymaking. While executive overreach marked the turbulent administration during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency before his impeachment and imprisonment, the current administration relies on a different form of coercion: presidential anger posted online functions as a target marker for digital loyalists, who systematically harass dissenters and terrify sitting lawmakers into silence.

This dynamic has proved particularly toxic when applied to national mourning. Rather than investigating institutional failures and reforming safety procedures after a frontline firefighter died in the line of duty, ruling camp figures reduced the loss to factional maneuvering. The cynical repurposing of public sacrifice into an offensive political weapon reveals a leadership prioritizing internal discipline and rival purges over executive stewardship.

Erosion of the Formal State System

Compounding this digital pressure is the entrenchment of informal power brokers. The prominent profile of the Seongnam line—a cadre of loyalists dating back to President Lee’s days as mayor of Seongnam—threatens to eclipse cabinet deliberation and party consensus. The involvement of figures tied to ideological activist networks such as the Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance raises persistent questions about who truly shapes executive decisions behind closed doors.

When informal cliques displace standard administrative machinery, statecraft gives way to loyalty tests. Grassroots party members and independent observers increasingly warn that unless the ruling bloc abandons factional score-settling and restores procedural governance, it risks alienating its broader democratic coalition entirely.
