South Korea's "Cabinet Leak" Problem and the Politics of Prosecution Politics

September 11, 2026

South Korean politics remains trapped in a familiar, toxic cycle where selective leaks from investigative agencies are weaponized by politicians and conservative media to shape public opinion before any trial even begins.

  • The Leak Pipeline: Investigative files consistently find their way to favored politicians and reporters, who selectively publish damaging details against targets.
  • Zero Accountability: Despite hundreds of complaints regarding the illegal disclosure of suspect facts over the past decade, the prosecution's strict monopoly on indictment has resulted in a 0% conviction rate for prosecutors leaking data.
  • The Asymmetrical Game: Sensational leaks instantly capture public attention and dominate the news cycle, while complex, defensive truths from the targets are ignored.
  • Democratic Failure: Liberal administrations with parliamentary majorities have repeatedly failed to institutionalize reforms to stop this deep-seated practice.

The Anatomy of an Asymmetric Information War

In South Korean political combat, sensationalized disclosures always defeat sober defense. Investigative journalists like Chang In-soo have repeatedly highlighted how targeted document leaks—reminiscent of past battles involving high-profile political figures like former President Lee Jae-myung and former Justice Minister Cho Kuk—create an inherently rigged playing field. The public is fed cherry-picked transcripts and salacious details, while the accused are left trying to unpack complex denials that media consumers simply ignore.

Worse, this is enabled by institutional impunity. South Korea’s prosecution service guards its monopoly on indictments, effectively shielding its own from criminal charges over illegal leaks. While advanced democracies like the United States and Germany enforce strict rules against pre-trial disclosures to protect the presumption of innocence, South Korean media often acts as a megaphone for whichever faction holds institutional leverage.

Beyond Legal Loopholes: The Cost of Political Trickery

Solving this dilemma requires more than just punitive laws against leaking; it demands structural transparency, such as complete confidentiality during active investigations followed by official, public briefings once inquiries conclude. Without such systemic overhauls, the cozy relationship between investigative agencies and press leaks will persist across successive administrations.

Yet the responsibility also lies with politicians themselves. Relying on clever spin and short-term tactical maneuvers to survive public scrutiny ultimately burns through the most valuable currency in politics: trust. When leaders attempt to talk their way out of uncomfortable realities instead of leaning into radical transparency and institutional reform, they deepen the very public cynicism that undermines democratic governance.
