Trump’s Wild Final Sprint Ahead of U.S. Midterms Sparks Alarm in Seoul

November 01, 2026

  • Donald Trump abruptly resumed campaign rallies just three days before the U.S. midterm elections, furious over reports that he was physically exhausted.
  • To woo voters, Trump promised $5,000 cash payouts (about 6.9 million won) for adult Americans if Republicans win, while explicitly warning recipients not to spend the money in South Korea.
  • With Democrats holding a solid lead in polls and early voting hitting record highs, the chaotic final stretch is raising serious concerns for South Korea’s export and trade strategies under President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

The final days of the U.S. midterm elections have descended into personal grievances and populist theater. Stung by media coverage questioning his stamina, Donald Trump reversed course on cancelled rallies in Republican strongholds like Kansas and Iowa, lashing out at reporters with personal insults. Meanwhile, his campaign has relied on increasingly erratic stunts, including a low-altitude Halloween flyover of Washington by Air Force One that startled residents, and the deployment of air defense missile systems near his Mar-a-Lago resort in Florida.

For international observers, the more immediate concern is Trump's brazen economic posturing. His $5,000 voter-payout promise came hand-in-hand with an overt protectionist warning targeting South Korea, cautioning voters to keep their spending strictly within U.S. borders. This rhetoric underscores how vulnerable South Korean trade and major U.S. investments are to shifting American political winds, especially as protectionist pressures mount.

Trade Uncertainty and the Stakes for Seoul

As early voter turnout shatters records and polls favor the Democrats, the chaotic backdrop of American politics serves as a stark reminder for South Korea. The Lee Jae-myung administration faces a delicate balancing act. If a hostile or unpredictable Washington pushes forward with aggressive tariffs and subsidy rollbacks, South Korean companies heavily invested in the U.S. market could face immediate financial turbulence.

Ultimately, the spectacle of U.S. election grandstanding—complete with preemptive claims of rigged polls and election denialism—highlights a broader degradation of democratic norms. For Seoul, navigating these turbulent international waters will require a disciplined, pragmatic foreign policy that prioritizes national interest over shifting foreign personalities.
