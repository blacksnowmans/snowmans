South Korea’s Ruling Party Pushes Judicial Overhaul Amid Showdown with Conservative Courts

October 26, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is accelerating a high-stakes overhaul of the nation's judiciary, framing recent court decisions and procedural delays as an organized political rebellion by entrenched judges.

  • Legislative blitz: Representative Jung Chung-rae successfully drove the first wave of judicial reform measures—including plans to expand the Supreme Court bench—through the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee, bypassing fierce opposition from court leadership.
  • Controversial ruling for Seoul mayor: An appellate court spared conservative Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon from disqualification by keeping his fine below the 1 million won (about $750) threshold under the Public Official Election Act, sparking allegations of double standards.
  • Constitutional delay tactics: Presiding Judge Lee Jae-gwon of the Seoul High Court indefinitely postponed hearings in an election-law retrial by invoking Article 84 of the Constitution—which covers presidential immunity—right before a routine reshuffle of judicial personnel.
  • High-level coordination: A four-hour dinner between President Lee Jae-myung and Jung signaled solid executive-legislative alignment ahead of the next round of reform bills.

Judicial Resistance and the Battle for Democratic Control

The intensifying confrontation between the ruling party and the court system under Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae marks a critical flashpoint for the Lee Jae-myung administration. Following the institutional overreach and abuses seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, the Democratic Party views stripping the judiciary and prosecutors of their entrenched privileges as an urgent mandate. Ruling lawmakers argue that the courts are no longer acting as neutral arbiters, pointing to the lenient treatment granted to Mayor Oh and strategic trial delays as evidence of a coordinated judicial pushback.

The use of procedural tools—such as dangling live trial broadcasts before postponing hearings under Article 84—has reinforced suspicions within the ruling camp that court leadership is timing rulings to influence political dynamics. For the administration, curbing this pushback is seen as essential to protecting its governance momentum and preventing unelected judges from dictating the political landscape.

Why the Ruling Coalition Is Doubling Down on Speed

The Democratic Party’s uncompromising approach in the parliamentary judiciary committee reflects a calculated strategic lesson: incremental concessions to the legal establishment often result in derailed reforms. By maintaining party unity and leveraging direct backing from the presidential office, reform leaders like Jung intend to advance the next phase of restructuring without leaving room for judicial pushback.

The judiciary, however, shows few signs of retreating quietly. With the conservative opposition rallying behind judicial autonomy and senior judges actively contesting the legislative drive, South Korea is heading toward an unprecedented trial of strength between democratic authority and judicial power.
