Korea’s Housing Trap: Why Tackling Jeonse Loans Is Crucial to Cooling Home Prices

October 16, 2026

Skyrocketing jeonse (lump-sum lease deposit) prices are pushing South Korean renters into debt-driven panic buying, yet politicians remain reluctant to tighten the subsidized lending programs fueling the cycle.

  • The panic cycle: Rather than speculative investors, genuine end-users are driving the latest borrowing spree, taking on risky debt to buy homes out of fear of rising lease deposits.
  • Political paralysis: While President Lee Jae-myung has warned of the systemic danger of debt-fueled home purchases, the ruling Democratic Party and housing bureaucrats hesitate to implement harsh loan caps ahead of elections.
  • Proposed curbs: Policy discussions include lowering mortgage limits on homes valued under 1.5 billion won (about $1.1 million) from 600 million won (about $440,000) down to 400 million won (about $290,000).
  • The overlooked driver: Critics argue that capping purchase mortgages alone will strand middle-class buyers unless authorities simultaneously curb cheap state-backed jeonse loans and subject them to strict Debt Service Ratio (DSR) caps.

The Flaw in 'Benevolent' Policy Lending

Under Korea’s unique jeonse system, tenants hand over a large lump-sum deposit instead of paying monthly rent, which landlords return at the end of the lease. Designed to protect ordinary citizens, low-interest state-backed jeonse loans have instead created an artificial liquidity bubble. Because renters can easily borrow cheap funds, landlords raise deposit demands, which in turn fuels leveraged gap investment. Desperate tenants, unable to afford rising deposits, end up taking out high-risk mortgages to purchase homes—effectively drinking seawater to quench their thirst.

Recent tax measures have proved too mild to cool the market. Cutting mortgage caps on modestly priced homes without addressing lease financing simply pulls the ladder away from first-time buyers. Real estate agents routinely push tenants to max out subsidized deposit loans, feeding the very market frenzy the government aims to suppress.

Why Coordinated Curbs Are Non-Negotiable

Stabilizing property prices requires treating the lease and purchase markets as a single ecosystem. If regulators only choke mortgage lending, capital and demand rush back into the rental market, driving up deposits and reigniting home prices downstream. Real estate analysts warn that any meaningful stabilization requires reducing maximum deposit guarantees and integrating jeonse financing into national DSR calculations.

President Lee has made housing stability a priority, but warnings from the top mean little without backing from the ruling party and economic ministries. Capping credit carries immediate political pain and voter backlash, yet delaying hard reforms leaves working-class families bearing the ultimate risk of a debt-fueled market correction.
