A Tale of Two Polls: Why President Lee’s Party is Seeing Mixed Signals

November 17, 2026

Recent polling data in South Korea reveals a sharp divergence in public sentiment toward the ruling Democratic Party, with support surging in some regions while core supporters show signs of growing frustration.

  • Polling Split: The Democratic Party, South Korea’s ruling liberal party, rose to 47% in telephone interview polls but dipped to 41.9% in automated response (ARS) surveys.
  • The Samsung Bump: Support in the swing region of Chungcheong jumped by 4 percentage points following President Lee Jae-myung’s attendance at a Samsung Electronics groundbreaking ceremony.
  • Base Anxiety: In the party’s traditional stronghold of Honam, support fell in ARS polls as voters expressed skepticism over delays in large-scale infrastructure projects.
  • Opposition Rebound: The People Power Party, the main conservative opposition, briefly overtook the ruling party in certain ARS metrics, leading 41.7% to 39.9%.

Methodology vs. Reality

The discrepancy between polling methods—live telephone interviews versus robocalls (ARS)—suggests a complex landscape for President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. Telephone interviews, which capture a broader range of moderate and passive voters, show a steady upward trend for the Democratic Party. However, ARS polls often reflect the "heat" of highly politically engaged voters. The fact that the ruling party is struggling in ARS suggests that its most vocal supporters are becoming impatient with the pace of policy execution.

This "temperature gap" is most visible among progressive voters. While 70% of progressives supported the president in live interviews, that figure dropped to 60% in automated polls. This 10-point difference indicates that while the base remains loyal, a significant minority is beginning to voice dissatisfaction, likely due to perceived stagnation in the government's flagship "Mega Projects."

Industrial Events and Regional Skepticism

President Lee’s recent visit to the Samsung Electronics Onyang Campus for the "Chungcheong Mega Project" groundbreaking proved that "ribbon-cutting" politics still moves the needle in South Korea. The 4% bump in the Chungcheong area—a crucial mid-country region that often decides elections—highlights how sensitive voters are to industrial investment and job creation. When the president physically appears at a tech hub to promise growth, the local electorate responds immediately.

However, this regional success has created a secondary problem in Honam, the southwestern region that serves as the Democratic Party's heartland. Voters there appear to be viewing the progress in Chungcheong with a degree of jealousy or suspicion, fearing their own regional development plans are being sidelined. For the Lee administration, the challenge is no longer just announcing grand visions, but proving it can deliver them simultaneously across the country without favoring one region over another. In today's volatile polling environment, a single photo-op at a semiconductor plant is a double-edged sword: it wins over the swing vote but risks alienating the base if the follow-through is missing.
