South Korea's Ruling Democratic Party Faces Panic Over General Election Prospects

October 20, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party, the liberal party supporting President Lee Jae-myung, is facing intense internal pressure to dissolve its current leadership and install an emergency steering committee as fears of a landslide defeat in the upcoming general elections sweep through party ranks.

  • Plunging approval margins: Recent Gallup polling shows the gap between voters supporting the government (34%) and those wanting to check the opposition (47%) has narrowed to just 13 percentage points, threatening the ruling party's constitutional defensive line.
  • Leadership backlash: Lawmakers are openly criticizing the leadership under Kim Min-seok for repeated political blunders and a lack of effective strategy.
  • Friendly fire: A bitter legal and truth dispute has erupted between Democratic lawmaker Park Sun-won and former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook over alleged phone calls tied to the controversial religious sect Shincheonji, exposing deep factional fractures.

For an administration that came to power following the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Seok-youl, the mood in Yeouido—Seoul's political heart—has quickly curdled from confidence to sheer panic. Senior Democratic lawmakers are warning that if current trends hold, the party risks losing its status as the parliamentary majority in the next general election. Historical data in South Korea shows that ruling parties rarely win the crucial capital region when presidential approval sits below the mid-40% mark, turning what was once quiet grumbling into open demands for a radical shakeup.

Compounding these macro-level fears is a self-destructive public feud between party insiders. The defamation complaint filed by Park Sun-won against former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook over disputed communications regarding Shincheonji—a group the party previously targeted during past political campaigns—makes the ruling camp look hopelessly distracted. Instead of presenting a unified front to push through key reforms for President Lee Jae-myung's administration, key figures are trading accusations and locking horns in legal battles.

Ultimately, this panic is a stress test for the Democratic Party's survival. Clinging to the current leadership structure out of institutional inertia will only accelerate voter backlash in the crucial Seoul metropolitan area. If the party wants to retain the trust that voters handed them after the tumultuous exit of Yoon Seok-youl, it must choose painful self-reflection over comfortable silence before public anger turns irreversible.
