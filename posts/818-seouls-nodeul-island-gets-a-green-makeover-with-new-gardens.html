Seoul's Nodeul Island gets a green makeover with new gardens and walkways

September 19, 2026

Seoul has thrown open newly revamped waterfront spaces on Nodeul Island, trading old parking lots and garbage dumps for ecological gardens, playgrounds, and walking paths along the Han River.

  • What happened: The Seoul Metropolitan Government opened new themed areas on Nodeul Island—including rain, hill, and forest playgrounds, and a 1.5-kilometer circular walking path.
  • What changes: A once-neglected concrete transit stop has been turned into a family-friendly cultural hub featuring weekend pop-up libraries, picnic rentals, and evening media art displays.
  • Who is affected: Seoul residents and visitors looking for accessible urban green space, families with young children, and weekend leisure-seekers.

Turning concrete into community space

For years, Nodeul Island sat in the middle of the Han River as an underutilized transit point—a place people passed through rather than stayed. The city's latest overhaul tackles this by transforming asphalt and waste sites into naturalistic play areas built with tree logs, grass hills, and natural stone, alongside a wider 1.5-kilometer loop trail.

The city has also paired these physical updates with programming. Saturdays now feature pop-up libraries, while Sundays offer picnic gear rentals, food trucks, and busking performances. Down by the water near the Hangang Bridge, "Atelier Nodeul" uses the underside of the bridge to project evening media art from prominent Korean artists.

Ambitious blueprints for the Han River

While the immediate changes make for a solid weekend destination, Seoul has much larger, long-term plans for the island. The city aims to complete a floating "Waterfront Garden" on the Han River by December 2027—complete with stage spaces and ferry terminal connections—followed by a futuristic "Sky Art Garden" connecting the island's east and west sides by 2030.

These grand visions carry the usual risks of bureaucratic overreach or maintenance neglect. Public spaces in Seoul often look stunning on launch day only to suffer from poor upkeep later on. Still, by balancing development with the preservation of local wildlife habitats—such as protecting urban frog populations in the eastern woods—Seoul is showing a rare commitment to sustainable urban renewal. If the city can maintain these spaces without letting them turn into sterile tourist traps, Nodeul Island could finally become the world-class urban sanctuary it was always meant to be.
