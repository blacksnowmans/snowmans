The Empty Promise of South Korea’s Prosecution Reform

October 03, 2026

South Korea’s long-standing quest to reform its powerful prosecution service—a perennial political battleground—is once again hitting a wall. Despite the structural separation of investigation and prosecution duties into the new Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA) and the Public Prosecution Service, critics argue that the core of the issue remains untouched: a deeply entrenched "prosecutorial" culture that transcends institutional labels.

  • Structural Incoherence: The launch of the new agencies has been marred by administrative chaos, with offices opening without basic infrastructure or clear staffing strategies.
  • The "Expertise" Trap: Critics argue that the tendency to fill these new roles with former prosecutors—under the guise of "expertise"—is merely allowing the old guard to maintain its grip under new titles.
  • The Personnel Paradox: The continued reliance on "prosecutor-turned-officials" for reform leadership is being met with deep skepticism, as many of these individuals previously fought tooth-and-nail against these very changes.

The "Prosecutor-First" Mentality Survives

The core problem isn't just the law; it's the people. During the administration of former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae, the conservative establishment and the media were quick to label personnel moves as politically biased. Yet, under the current administration of President Yoon Suk-yeol—a former prosecutor himself—a similar, if not more blatant, consolidation of power is often excused by the same groups as mere "professionalism." This double standard confirms the suspicion that the prosecution functions less as an impartial arm of the law and more as a self-serving interest group protecting its own status.

This culture is reinforced by the "banality of evil"—a concept brought up by critics to describe how mid-level prosecutors participate in systemic abuses simply by following orders or hiding behind majority votes. The institutional practice of keeping an "objection record" (a document used by prosecutors to claim they disagreed with an unethical order later, to avoid liability) is a perfect example of this cowardice. It is a cynical "insurance policy" rather than a tool for justice.

Reform Without "Cleaning House" is Futile

The recent launch of the Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA) highlights a glaring failure in political judgment. Entrusting the leadership of reform to individuals who spent years defending the very system that needed dismantling is, in the eyes of many, a strategic error. Even when leadership choices include more reasonable figures, the lack of a true "personnel purge" remains a critical weakness.

The irony is not lost on the public: former prosecutors, now out of power due to the new agency's formation, are reportedly lobbying their former juniors to stall investigations and sow discord, hoping for a political pendulum swing that returns them to their former glory. If the current administration fails to break this cycle by placing genuinely independent, non-prosecutorial figures at the helm—such as those with judicial backgrounds who are less beholden to the "prosecutor's club"—the systemic rot will persist.

Ultimately, administrative re-branding and new office signs cannot replace a fundamental shift in ethics. Without a complete departure from the "expertise" myth and a genuine purge of those who prioritize organizational survival over the rule of law, these reforms risk becoming nothing more than a hollow shell.
