Han Dong-hoon and the Prosecutor's Files: Anatomy of a Political Slur

September 15, 2026

Leaked investigative records involving South Korea's conservative politician Han Dong-hoon are exposing how sensitive judicial files are allegedly being weaponized by political actors to frame opposition figures.

  • What happened: Conservative politician Han Dong-hoon publicized an edited transcript regarding political figures, triggering intense speculation that the material originated directly from investigative agencies.
  • The pattern: Analysts reviewing the full investigative files note a heavy, selective focus on opposition figures linked to the Democratic Party, while ruling-party names appearing in the same documents were excluded from keyword searches.
  • The method: The controversy also highlights the continued use of "D-NET," an electronic repository system that allows prosecutors to store seized digital data without the owner's consent and use it to expand unrelated investigations.
  • Who is affected: Opposition politicians targeted in prosecutors' keyword searches, as well as public trust in the neutrality of South Korea's judicial and investigative systems.

Unpacking the Leaks and Selective Targeting

The controversy began when Han released an edited transcript concerning a candidate, raising immediate questions about how such sensitive and voluminous documents fell into a politician's hands. While Han claimed he received the material from a whistleblower, the named individual flatly denied having or sharing the documents. Observers argue that such records could only have emerged through prosecutorial channels, pointing to a familiar playbook where investigative power is deployed to shape public narratives.

A comprehensive review of the case files reveals a stark bias in how the investigation was conducted. From the earliest reports surrounding a pharmaceutical testing controversy—which Han inflated into dramatic accusations of deliberate harm—investigators heavily emphasized keywords related to opposition leaders like Lee Jae-myung and various Democratic Party lawmakers. Meanwhile, references to ruling-party politicians found in the same source materials were quietly ignored.

The D-NET Controversy and Distorted Facts

At the heart of the broader systemic critique is D-NET, an electronic evidence database operated by investigative agencies. Critics point out that seizing digital devices and uploading their contents to D-NET without the owners' knowledge enables wide-ranging "fishing expeditions" and secondary investigations far removed from the original warrants. This practice bypasses basic due process protections and gives authorities a tool to keep targets under perpetual pressure.

Beyond the procedural abuses, the case demonstrates a wide gap between political rhetoric and actual court records. While conservative figures sensationalized the trial to imply a malicious conspiracy involving lethal human experiments, the actual 1st-instance court ruling centered on a different issue: submitting data based on dead viruses rather than active ones to the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. As this saga shows, the weaponization of judicial records continues to turn legal proceedings into instruments of political theater.
