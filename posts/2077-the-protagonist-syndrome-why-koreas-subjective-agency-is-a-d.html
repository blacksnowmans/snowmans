The Protagonist Syndrome: Why Korea’s "Subjective Agency" is a Double-Edged Sword

November 07, 2026

Professor Kim Kyung-il, a prominent cognitive psychologist often featured in national media, argues that the unique Korean sense of "subjective agency"—the feeling that one is the protagonist of society—is a double-edged sword that fuels both the nation’s democratic dynamism and its chronic social anxiety.

  • High Agency Culture: Unlike neighboring Japan, which often prioritizes "staying in one's place," Koreans possess a high level of "subjective agency," leading to intense social engagement but also extreme sensitivity to nunchi (the ability to read social cues and context).
  • Constants vs. Variables: Kim identifies the root of modern unhappiness as the failure to distinguish between "constants" (unchangeable temperament or the past) and "variables" (manageable factors like sleep habits or social distance).
  • Digital Threat: The rise of short-form media is flagged as a systemic threat to democracy, as it fosters an "illusion of understanding" that replaces deep deliberation with impulsive confirmation bias.
  • Political Shift: The current administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal party) is seen by some as a return to "slow," deliberate governance following the impulsive and often erratic decision-making that characterized the era of the now-imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The Survival Skill of Contextual Intelligence

In the Korean context, nunchi is far more than mere politeness; it is a high-level "contextual intelligence" honed through a turbulent modern history. While Western communication tends to be explicit, Korean society is "high-context," requiring citizens to read subtle non-verbal cues. This ability allows Koreans to be incredibly dynamic and responsive to change. However, when this agency is not balanced by self-reflection, it devolves into a "battle of pride" where individuals feel the need to belittle others to maintain their status as the "protagonist."

This psychological friction was most visible during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. His tenure was marked by a lack of the "slow" deliberation and self-restraint necessary for a leader. In cognitive psychology terms, his impulsivity—often marketed as "boldness"—destabilized the national system. The source suggests that true leadership requires a "humble agency," where the leader respects the public’s nunchi rather than forcing their own will through impulsive edicts.

The Erosion of Deliberation in the Age of Shorts

The briefing highlights a growing "intellectual polarization" driven by SNS algorithms and short-form content. When the brain consumes 60-second summaries of complex geopolitical or social issues, it experiences a dopamine-driven "aha!" moment, creating a false sense of mastery. This kills the "muscles of thought" required to sustain a healthy democracy. Those who lose the ability to sit with complexity often retreat into extremist camp logic to soothe their intellectual loneliness.

Ultimately, the path to psychological and social stability in Korea lies in reclaiming the "slow" virtues. Whether it is the patient governance currently being attempted by the Lee Jae-myung government or the personal habit of distinguishing what one can actually control, the goal is to move beyond the "impulsivity trap." Real agency is not about reacting the fastest; it is about having the self-possession to wait, deliberate, and recognize that everyone else is also the protagonist of their own story.
