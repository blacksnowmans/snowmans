South Korea's Cabinet Meetings Risk Becoming a Grammar Class of Errors

October 07, 2026

  • President Lee Jae-myung sparked public backlash during a recent Cabinet meeting by policing officials' vocabulary, criticizing the use of common terms like floral wreaths.
  • Critics quickly pointed out that the president's own vocabulary was riddled with errors, including using the non-existent slang term "jwahage" on social media and misusing the word for a magnanimous person.
  • The incident has fueled accusations of hypocrisy and raised concerns that high-level state meetings are degenerating into trivial linguistic disputes instead of addressing urgent economic and livelihood issues.

South Korea's top policy-making body is facing mockery after a Cabinet meeting descended into an impromptu vocabulary lesson. When Fair Trade Commission Chairperson Joo Byung-ki reported on new standard terms to prevent funeral homes from unjustly discarding or reselling floral wreaths sent by mourners, the meeting took an unexpected turn. Instead of addressing the critical consumer protection measure, President Lee Jae-myung fixated on semantics, claiming that people should use a different word for floral arrangements sent to funerals rather than weddings.

As it turns out, the presidential correction was completely off the mark. The term in question, hwahwan, correctly refers to circular floral decorations used in both celebratory and mournful settings. Observers were quick to note the irony, especially given the president's own spotty record with Korean phrasing. Beyond the funeral wreath dispute, critics highlighted his frequent use of "daeinbae"—a contradictory term that awkwardly combines a word for a great person with a suffix typically used to demean a group—alongside his use of an entirely fabricated slang word, "jwahage," on social media while simultaneously lecturing others on proper grammar.

When Nitpicking Overshadows National Governance

The episode underscores a growing frustration among critics regarding the tone of the current administration. Important policy initiatives, such as reforming funeral home contracts to protect grieving families from exploitative practices, require serious administrative focus. Instead, officials find themselves navigating petty semantic debates. Turning the Cabinet into a venue for linguistic gatekeeping—especially when the leader's own grammar is flawed—diminishes the gravity of state affairs.

Observers argue that this pattern reflects a broader issue of misplaced priorities within the presidential office. When top leaders spend their time manufacturing debates over dictionary definitions rather than delivering substantive livelihood policies, the public loses faith in the seriousness of governance. True leadership requires self-reflection and accountability rather than outward lectures built on shaky linguistic foundations.
