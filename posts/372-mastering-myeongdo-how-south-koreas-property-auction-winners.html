Mastering Myeongdo: How South Korea's Property Auction Winners Evict Occupants Without Losing Their Minds

September 08, 2026

Winning a South Korean real estate auction is only half the battle; the real test begins with myeongdo—the legal and psychological process of clearing out the property's previous occupants.

  • The Eviction Hurdle: Beginners often assume buying a property gives them immediate possession, but uncooperative occupants frequently demand unreasonable moving fees or refuse to leave.
  • Legal Leverage: South Korean law provides powerful tools like the Real Estate Delivery Order (indomyeongnyeong), giving buyers a clear statutory path to reclaim their property.
  • The Golden Rule: Emotional confrontations fail; success relies entirely on calm adherence to legal procedures, predictable timelines, and zero tolerance for unlawful self-help.

For novice investors stepping into South Korea's property auction market, the hardest psychological barrier isn't winning the bid—it is standing outside the front door of a newly acquired apartment and realizing someone else is still living inside. Occupants often resort to dramatic tactics: bursting into tears, claiming extreme financial hardship, demanding millions of won in moving fees, or simply cutting off all communication.

The cardinal sin at this stage is panic. Unprepared buyers frequently lose their cool, matching the occupant's emotional outbursts or—worse—attempting to force entry when a property appears vacant. The latter can backfire disastrously. Under South Korean law, entering a property without authorization or disposing of even a minor item left behind by the former owner can result in criminal charges for trespassing and property damage, turning the property buyer into a criminal defendant.

Instead, seasoned investors treat eviction not as a personal confrontation, but as a systematic legal procedure. The law is firmly on the side of the legally registered owner. Buyers must act with polite but unyielding resolve, setting clear boundaries regarding moving fees—which should roughly align with professional forced-eviction costs (typically around 100,000 to 150,000 won per pyeong, or roughly $70 to $110 per 36 square feet)—while initiating formal legal filings simultaneously.

Ultimately, dealing with stubborn occupants is a waiting game governed by court systems rather than willpower. By filing for a Delivery Order within the strict six-month window after full payment and securing an injunction against transferring possession, buyers strip away the occupant's leverage. When court bailiff notices and forced eviction warnings start arriving, even the most defiant holdouts typically come to the negotiating table. The takeaway for foreign observers watching Korea's property market is clear: patience, cold logic, and strict compliance with the legal framework always trump brute force.
