The Merger Deadline: Why Korea’s Ruling Party Must Act by February

November 02, 2026

The success of President Lee Jae-myung’s reform agenda hinges on a swift merger between the ruling Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party by early next year to secure a unified front for the upcoming general elections.

  • The Golden Window: Formal integration must be finalized between January and February to establish a fair primary system and allow newly recruited members to participate in the candidate selection process.
  • Leadership Inertia: Critics are calling on Kim Min-seok, the chairman of the Democratic Party (DP), to break his silence and provide a clear roadmap for the merger to avoid accusations of protecting his own faction's interests.
  • Fragmentation Risks: Postponing the merger until the summer risks a "backroom deal" narrative, which could lead to a split liberal vote in competitive districts and hand an easy victory to the opposition.
  • Neutralizing the Opposition: A unified party is viewed as the only way to overcome legislative obstruction from the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, and remnants of the administration of Yoon Suk-yeol ssi, who is currently imprisoned following his removal from office.

Overcoming the 'Vested Interests' within the Ruling Party

The primary obstacle to this merger is the survival instinct of established lawmakers within the Democratic Party. For many DP veterans, a merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP)—a smaller, ideologically sharp liberal party—is seen as a threat to their guaranteed nominations. However, the current political climate demands "party member sovereignty," a system where the base of the party, rather than its elite, dictates the direction of the movement. If the DP leadership continues to hesitate, they risk alienating a motivated base that sees this merger as the only way to protect the "Lee Jae-myung government" from conservative pushback.

The silence from Chairman Kim Min-seok is particularly concerning to reformers. By failing to present a concrete plan for integration, the leadership appears to be "muddling through" the issue rather than leading it. This ambiguity fuels internal friction and allows the opposition to frame the liberal camp as disorganized. To maintain the mandate given by the public, the DP must move beyond narrow factional calculations and embrace a "plus-sum" political strategy that absorbs the RKP’s reformist energy.

Avoiding the Trap of Backroom Politics

Timing is not just a logistical concern; it is a matter of democratic legitimacy. If the merger is delayed until the summer, the window for a fair, open primary vanishes. This would likely force the party into a "sidecar" arrangement where the RKP is treated as a junior partner in a secret seat-sharing deal. Such a move would be a disaster for public relations, providing the conservative media with ample material to attack the merger as a "collusion for power" rather than a union of principles.

The only way to avoid this trap is to open the doors early and let the party members decide. A transparent primary process conducted in early 2024 would allow the two parties to achieve a "chemical fusion" of their voter bases. This would create a powerful legislative engine capable of completing the reforms initiated after the fall of the previous administration. In the face of a defiant opposition and the historical weight of the current presidency, the DP cannot afford the luxury of waiting until the last minute.
