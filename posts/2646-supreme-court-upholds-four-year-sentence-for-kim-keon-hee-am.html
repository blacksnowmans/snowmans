Supreme Court Upholds Four-Year Sentence for Kim Keon-hee Amid Courtroom Clash and Media Debate

November 23, 2026

South Korea's Supreme Court has finalized a four-year prison sentence for Kim Keon-hee over a stock manipulation case involving the company Deutsch Motors, prompting fierce courtroom protests from the defendant and sharp criticism from a South Korean political commentator over how local media covered her reaction.

  • The ruling: Sitting as a full bench, South Korea's highest court finalized a four-year prison term for Kim Keon-hee on charges including Deutsch Motors stock price manipulation, rejecting defense arguments that the prosecution was politically driven.
  • The courtroom outburst: Appearing the very next day at the Seoul Central District Court for a separate trial over alleged violations of the Political Parties Act, Kim rejected the Supreme Court verdict and accused the special counsel, judges, and the public of being swayed by politics.
  • Media framing dispute: Several South Korean outlets described Kim's remarks using the Korean term "ulbun"—a word implying righteous indignation—a characterization the source commentary blasts as a factual distortion that sympathizes with a convicted figure.
  • Political shift: According to the commentary, the verdict marks a belated step toward accountability following years of stalled investigations and non-indictment decisions during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, unfolding now under the current Lee Jae-myung administration.

What Happened in Court

During the Seoul Central District Court hearing on the Political Parties Act case, Kim openly disputed the Supreme Court's decision, telling the courtroom she could not understand why she was convicted over Deutsch Motors and questioning whether the justices had even examined her case. She also shouted toward the court, asking how long officials would keep watching the political winds.

In its analysis of the hearing, the Korean blog commentary argues that the four-year sentence rests on concrete evidence—including financial account transaction records and witness testimony—rather than political targeting. The author contrasts Kim's courtroom defiance with earlier videos showing her jumping rope and mopping at home, characterizing those clips as a failed public-relations campaign aimed at winning sympathy and securing release before sentencing.

The Media and Accountability Debate

Beyond Kim's legal fate, the commentary focuses heavily on South Korean journalism. It contends that applying the word "ulbun" to a convicted defendant's outburst misuses language meant for wronged workers or victims of state abuse, framing the coverage as overly deferential to former power holders and calling for stricter reporting standards.

From a broader institutional perspective, the piece views the ruling as a test of judicial independence after prosecutors previously delayed probes or declined to indict Kim during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. While Kim maintains the proceedings are politically tainted, the commentator argues that enforcing the law without exception under the Lee Jae-myung government is essential to restoring public trust in South Korea's courts and press.
