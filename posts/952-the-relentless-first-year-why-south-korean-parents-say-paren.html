The Relentless First Year: Why South Korean Parents Say Parenting Gets Harder, Not Easier

October 10, 2026

Every expectant parent in South Korea is met with the same comforting lie: just survive the first 100 days, or wait until the baby walks, and things will finally get easier.

  • The myth of the milestone: Parents are told that sleep, crawling, and walking bring relief, but each stage only trades one exhausting challenge for another.
  • The biological reality: Newborns lack the melatonin needed for sleep training, making early routines a battle of survival rather than a skill to be taught.
  • Mounting financial and social pressure: From navigating housing safety hazards to spending thousands of dollars on first-birthday parties (돌잔치), Korean parents carry an immense physical and economic burden.
  • The systemic trap: Society often frames parenting struggles as individual failures of "mothering skills," ignoring the structural lack of support.

The first twelve months of raising a child are often framed as a series of levels in a video game—clear the newborn phase, unlock the 100-day miracle, survive teething, and graduation awaits. Yet, as veterans of South Korea’s intense parenting communities note, the total volume of suffering remains constant. You simply trade midnight colic for crawling toddlers who treat electrical sockets like targets.

This reality hits hard in a society where child-rearing is treated as an individual achievement test rather than heavy public labor. While the administration under President Lee Jae-myung faces pressure to address the country's demographic crisis, everyday parents are left wrestling with exorbitant child-proofing costs, rigid housing restrictions in rental apartments, and the quiet loneliness of losing their own lives to round-the-clock care.

From Sleep Deprivation to the Toddler Run: A Year of Endless Pivots

The newborn phase is a relentless loop of feeding, changing, and crying, complicated by the biological fact that babies do not produce melatonin for their first two to three months. Despite this, parenting manuals push aggressive sleep training, leaving exhausted mothers and fathers drowning in guilt over normal infant biology. When that phase finally blurs into memory, it is romanticized only because the child survived—replaced immediately by the hazards of the "oral stage," where infants investigate the world by chewing on television remotes and living room power strips.

As babies graduate to crawling and walking around their first birthday, the physical danger escalates. Protecting a child requires expensive home modifications—silicone edge guards, safety gates, and padded floor mats. For young couples renting homes under South Korea's high-priced housing market, even drilling a hole to secure a safety gate can be a landlord-mandated impossibility, turning everyday home life into a high-anxiety obstacle course.

By the time the child reaches their first birthday—celebrated with lavish banquet parties costing anywhere from $2,000 to $4,000 (300만~500만 원) despite economic slowdowns—parents are running on fumes. The arrival of unyielding toddler self-assertion, known locally as the dreaded "no-saying" phase around 18 months, shatters any lingering illusion of a peaceful routine. Relief does not magically appear at a certain age; rather, parents simply grow stronger muscles to carry the weight.
