South Korea's Moon-to-Earth Slump: President Lee Jae-myung's Approval Plummets to 35.9%

September 16, 2026

  • What happened: A new poll by the Korea Society Opinion Institute (KSOI) shows President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating has crashed to 35.9%, with negative reviews hitting 59.4% amid growing public fury over stalled reforms and controversial appointments.
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party) is bleeding support in its core strongholds, with young voters aged 18–29 and residents in the vital Seoul metropolitan area turning away in droves.
  • What it changes: The collapse in numbers signals the loss of political momentum for an administration just months into its term, triggering panic within party ranks ahead of upcoming by-elections and forcing progressives to confront a profound structural crisis.
  • The policy failure: Nearly 41% of respondents said the government is doing "nothing well," while key promises like prosecution reform and economic revival have faltered under recycled personnel and perceived political hypocrisy.

South Korean politics has a brutal way of punishing leaders who lose their narrative, and President Lee Jae-myung is currently learning that lesson the hard way. The numbers from the KSOI survey are not just routine political fluctuations; they represent a deep, visceral betrayal felt by the very base that brought this administration to power. When 20-somethings drop their approval of a progressive president into the 20% range and voters in Seoul and surrounding Gyeonggi province register over 60% disapproval, the social contract of the regime is fracturing at its foundations.

The root of this disillusionment lies in a creeping sense of political deception. Reform-minded voters expected a clean break from past practices, particularly regarding the powerful and opaque prosecution service. Instead, they have watched prosecutors from previous administrations—including figures associated with controversial raids and investigations—get promoted, while key legislative overhauls drift into bureaucratic limbo. Appointments like the contested nomination of Kim Seung-won for justice minister have become lightning rods, functioning less as debates over individual merit and more as a referendum on an administration that appears tone-deaf to its own foundational promises.

The Danger of Silence and the Need for Pressure

The most telling symptom of this political malaise is not just the vocal anger of the public, but the quiet resignation of the president's traditional supporters. Many progressives have slipped into a habit of passive defense or stunned silence, assuming the administration will eventually course-correct on its own. This abdication of civic duty has only encouraged executive arrogance and a dangerous cycle of poor communication, often derided by critics as an attempt to muzzle dissenting internal voices.

Yet, the takeaway from this crisis is not to abandon the political project, but to reclaim it. Democracy in South Korea has never been a finished product handed down from the Blue House; it is an ongoing negotiation forged through constant, often exhausting public pressure. As the administration's approval ratings sink toward dangerous territory, the onus falls back on the conscious citizenry. Only by loudly demanding accountability on prosecution reform, economic recovery, and fair personnel choices can progressives force an adrift government back onto the rails before it completely alienates the majority that elected it.
