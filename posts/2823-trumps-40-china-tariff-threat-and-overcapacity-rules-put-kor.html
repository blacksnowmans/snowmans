Trump’s 40% China Tariff Threat and Overcapacity Rules Put Korean Exporters on Edge

November 28, 2026

U.S. President Donald Trump is wielding a threatened 40% tariff hike on Chinese imports and new "overcapacity tariffs" targeting 14 major economies—including South Korea—to gain leverage ahead of U.S.-China trade talks, according to a Nov. 27 briefing by Korean political commentary site Black Snowman citing Bloomberg and major South Korean media.

  • Trade pressure widens across Asia: Washington’s overcapacity tariff plan keeps caps at 15% for autos and 50% for steel, drawing sharp protests from China, the European Union, Japan, and India while squeezing alternative manufacturing hubs like Vietnam and forcing South Korean exporters to reassess U.S. tax credits.
  • White House rift over Nvidia: Trump publicly backed Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s return as posing no national security threat, triggering pushback from former U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission Chair Gary Gensler, "Make America Great Again" (MAGA) hardliners, and Secretary of State nominee Marco Rubio, even after Treasury nominee Scott Bessent pivoted to support the president.
  • Disputed GDP numbers and inflation fears: Following midterm election losses, Trump called third-quarter U.S. GDP growth data "fake" and demanded an investigation, prompting warnings from market analysts and South Korean outlets—such as progressive daily Hankyoreh and cable network JTBC—that imposing fresh tariffs amid persistent inflation risks domestic "economic self-harm."
  • Competing signals in Beijing and Hanoi: Huawei founder Ren Zhengfei argued U.S. sanctions ironically spurred China’s tech self-reliance—even as local AI researchers complain progress is stalled—while Trump Organization partners are pursuing a 60-plus-story Trump Tower in Hanoi despite looming U.S. tariffs on Vietnam.

What It Means for Seoul and Global Markets

For South Korea’s export-driven economy, the immediate worry is less the 15% auto tariff ceiling itself than the shrinking room to maneuver around it. Korean conglomerates spent years shifting assembly lines to Vietnam to hedge against U.S.-China friction, only to find Washington’s overcapacity framework turning that detour into a liability. Combined with looming questions over U.S. export tax credits and potential non-tariff barriers, Seoul’s trade negotiators face a much narrower path than during previous tariff rounds.

Meanwhile, the infighting in Washington signals that U.S. economic policy is becoming harder for allies to price in. A split between security hawks like Rubio and supporters of Trump’s stance on Nvidia’s access leaves Asian semiconductor suppliers guessing where U.S. red lines actually sit. Even more unsettling for bond and currency markets, as analysts cited in the briefing note, is the prospect of official U.S. growth statistics becoming politicized—a shift that raises risk premiums far beyond trade.

Noticeably, the gap between public rhetoric and dealmaking remains wide across the region. Huawei’s leadership can project confidence in domestic chip self-sufficiency while Vietnamese partners court Trump-branded real estate, yet both underscore how deeply private interests and national security now overlap. For South Korean policymakers and boardrooms, navigating the next phase of U.S. trade policy will require preparing for sudden tariff demands rather than counting on alliance exemptions.
