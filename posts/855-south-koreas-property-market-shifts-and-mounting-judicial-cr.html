South Korea’s Property Market Shifts and Mounting Judicial Crisis

October 09, 2026

South Korea is currently navigating a period of sharp contradictions: while the property market is showing signs of long-awaited stabilization, the judiciary is facing intense scrutiny over alleged political bias and systemic opacity.

  • Real Estate Normalization: Government policies targeting "Gangnam"—the affluent district in Seoul that serves as the nation's speculative epicenter—are successfully cooling the market, shifting the focus from speculative investment to actual residency.
  • Judicial Controversy: The Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive minor party) is pushing to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, citing administrative manipulation and "secretive" case allocation systems.
  • Political Fallout: Potential legal outcomes for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon (a member of the conservative People Power Party) are creating uncertainty, with the timing of a possible by-election becoming a focal point for partisan strategy.

Property Market: The End of the "Speculation Republic"

The success of the current administration’s real estate policy is rooted in a singular, aggressive strategy: breaking the speculative fever in Gangnam. For years, the market suffered from a "distorted feedback loop" where rising prices attracted even more demand, regardless of actual utility. By tightening credit and regulations specifically targeting the top tier of the market, the government is effectively purging the speculative frenzy that once forced ordinary citizens to over-leverage themselves (the "young-geul" or "pulling from the soul" phenomenon) just to secure housing.

While critics and media outlets linked to the establishment argue that the market is crashing, the reality is a transition toward a sustainable, residency-focused model. This is a painful but necessary correction. The era where real estate acted as a primary engine for unearned wealth is closing, a shift essential to ensuring that hard work—not debt-fueled speculation—becomes the standard for economic stability.

Judicial Reform and the Push for Impeachment

The judiciary, however, remains a theater of deep distrust. The Rebuilding Korea Party has accused the current leadership, led by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, of allowing the court to function as a "shield for the establishment." Central to this is the opaque case allocation system, which allows judges to avoid or delay sensitive cases by keeping them in "closed-door" channels. Critics argue that judges are weaponizing the "presumption of innocence" to stall proceedings against political figures, mirroring the perceived obstructionism seen during the downfall of the previous administration.

In response to this, the Rebuilding Korea Party is calling for a formal impeachment of the Chief Justice and the abolition of the National Court Administration, a body long accused of being a central hub for judicial corruption. The goal is to force a system-wide overhaul: moving away from a culture where judicial careerism and political alignments dictate legal outcomes. For the public, the stakes are existential—a judiciary that operates as a tool for political maneuvering rather than an arbiter of justice effectively nullifies the rule of law.

As Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon faces his own legal challenges, the political landscape remains on edge. Should the court deliver a conviction that results in a loss of his eligibility to hold office, the resulting by-election would force a massive reallocation of political capital between the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party. Whether the courts can act with impartiality or will once again be swayed by political calculations remains the defining question for South Korea’s democratic integrity.
