Can You Really Become a Landlord in South Korea for Just $100,000?

September 08, 2026

  • The Deal: A three-story multi-family residential building in Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi Province—near South Korea's tech manufacturing hub—has seen its auction price plummet from an initial appraisal of about $5 million (7 billion won) down to roughly $130,000–$150,000 (1억 to 2억 won).
  • The Catch: Previous auction winners defaulted on their final payments, signaling hidden risks. Furthermore, the property sits in a rural township experiencing population decline and rapid aging.
  • The Outlook: While the low entry barrier and potential rental yields look tempting on paper, investors face heavy vacancy risks and a lack of public transit unless they plan to live there themselves.

South Korea’s sensationalized real estate culture loves a good bargain. A recent auction listing for a multi-family building in Oseong-myeon, a rural district in Pyeongtaek, has captured attention precisely because it promises the title of "building owner" for the price of a cheap villa. With a total floor area of around 120 pyeong (about 4,300 square feet) sitting on 130 pyeong of land, the math suggests absurdly high potential yields if units are rented out.

Yet a closer look strips away the glossy appeal. The property's rock-bottom price stems from a previous bidder failing to complete the purchase after winning the auction, forfeiting their deposit. That kind of cold feet usually points to a miscalculation of local demand. While the broader city of Pyeongtaek booms thanks to Samsung Electronics' massive semiconductor investments, its outlying farming townships are telling a completely different story. Government and demographic data show local populations shrinking, economic activity declining, and elderly residents making up over 35% of the area.

Weighing the Reality of Rural South Korean Real Estate

Buying cheap property in an aging countryside district is less about quick capital gains and much more about long-term property management. From a legal standpoint, the auction item is clean: prior mortgages are wiped out, and there are no senior tenants demanding unpaid deposits. That makes the actual eviction and takeover process relatively straightforward. However, an easy takeover does not equal a steady stream of tenants. Without a car, getting to hospitals, supermarkets, or public transit is practically impossible, making it a tough sell for anyone who isn't retired or working locally.

Ultimately, this auction highlights the trap of chasing high-yield percentages without looking at the underlying demographics. If an investor views this strictly as a speculative play to flip for a profit, they are likely walking into a trap. But for a buyer seeking an affordable rural lifestyle—someone willing to shoulder remodeling costs and manage day-to-day upkeep—it offers a hands-on lesson in property ownership far away from the overheated Seoul housing market.
