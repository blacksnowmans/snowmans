South Korea’s Local Governments Race to Demystify New High School Credit System

November 24, 2026

Municipal governments and regional education offices across South Korea are launching a wave of free briefings to help families navigate the transition to a credit-based high school curriculum and the 2028 college admissions overhaul.

  • Simultaneous regional rollouts: Around November 23, local authorities ramped up public seminars—Uiwang, south of Seoul, drew roughly 100 parents and students and scheduled a follow-up for November 28, while southeastern Daegu opened pre-registration for traveling lectures starting December 4.
  • What the reform changes: Under the high school credit system, students choose electives tied to intended university majors rather than following a uniform track, making early career planning in middle school—highlighted in recent briefings by Seoul’s Southern District Office of Education—a prerequisite for college strategy.
  • Beyond elite university prep: In the southwestern Gwangju and South Jeolla region, officials paired regular-admission counseling after the national college entrance exam (Suneung) with civil service interview coaching for vocational high schoolers, alongside a 100-person strategy session in Gwangyang.
  • Community and industry links: Career teachers in South Chungcheong’s Dangjin and Seosan are training to connect classrooms with local tax, recycling, and legal professionals, while Guri and Geumsan run campus mentoring and youth workshops and the Korea Institute of Energy Technology Evaluation and Planning (KETEP), a state energy agency, develops K-12 energy career materials.

Breaking Seoul’s Monopoly on Admissions Strategy

Whenever South Korea alters its college entrance rules, anxious parents typically turn to expensive private admissions consultants. By packing municipal halls and bringing star instructors from EBS, the country’s public educational broadcaster, to regional cities like Pohang, local officials are working to ensure families outside affluent Seoul neighborhoods receive credible guidance at zero cost.

The deeper tension behind these crowded seminars is cultural. Granting teenagers control over their own course schedules is meant to foster self-directed learning, yet in a hyper-competitive education market, wider choice quickly breeds parental fear of picking the wrong subject for a university application.

Public briefings alone will not dismantle South Korea’s massive private tutoring industry. Even so, the coordinated push across city halls, provincial education offices, and state agencies signals a growing consensus that steering students through the 2028 admissions shift—and supporting vocational routes alongside university tracks—is a public duty.
