Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon Faces Scrutiny Over Alleged $1.1M Kickback and Cover-Up in Han River Bus Project

October 15, 2026

An audit into Seoul’s flagship Han River Bus project has triggered explosive political fallout after allegations surfaced of a 1.5 billion won (about $1.1 million) kickback scheme and an internal cover-up attempt ordered by city leadership.

  • What happened: Lawmaker Park Joo-min of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea revealed during a parliamentary audit that 1.5 billion won was siphoned from shipbuilder Gadeok Heavy Industries, allegedly funding kickbacks to a senior official at the city-run Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation (SH).
  • The cover-up allegations: A whistleblower testified that after drafting a formal police investigation request regarding the corruption, Seoul City officials were pressured to destroy the records, forcing the whistleblower to lock and seal the digital files on a computer to preserve evidence.
  • The political fallout: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon deflected questions during parliamentary questioning by claiming he could not recall key briefing details, sharpening public criticism as he faces separate legal hurdles.
  • Who is affected: Seoul taxpayers, city transit authorities, and Mayor Oh, whose signature municipal infrastructure project now faces potential criminal investigations.

A Growing Corruption Scandal in Seoul City Hall

The allegations surrounding the Han River Bus project strike at the heart of public administration in Seoul. What began as a routine audit into a sole-source contract with Gadeok Heavy Industries uncovered an unexplained 1.5 billion won (~$1.1 million) pool of money. Suspicion that these funds were funneled through brokers to high-ranking officials at SH—the public enterprise responsible for operating the water buses—transforms this from an accounting discrepancy into a major municipal corruption case. Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s decision to refer the matter to the Board of Audit and Inspection rather than immediately handing it over to police appears to critics as a deliberate stalling tactic.

Even more troubling is the alleged effort to erase the paper trail. According to whistleblower testimony, city audit officials who drafted a formal request for a law enforcement investigation received implicit pressure to destroy the document. Had the whistleblower not password-protected and locked the files on a office computer, crucial evidence would have been destroyed. Attempting to destroy official public records constitutes a severe crime under South Korean law, involving abuse of authority and destruction of evidence. Mayor Oh’s repeated defense during audit hearings—stating that "investigations will clarify everything" while deflecting direct responsibility—has done little to quiet demands for accountability.

Broader Institutional Stalemates and Government Reforms

This municipal crisis unfolds amid wider institutional tension across South Korea's legal and political system. At the highest judicial level, Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae faces criticism over delayed proceedings in major cases and a 200-plus-day vacancy on the bench after declining to re-recommend a Supreme Court justice nominee to President Lee Jae-myung. The friction highlights an ongoing tug-of-war between the judiciary and the executive branch.

At the same time, President Lee’s administration is moving forward with key structural changes. Parliamentary committees recently processed the confirmation report for Kim Ji-yong to head the newly established Major Crimes Investigation Bureau, marking a key step in separating investigative and prosecutorial powers. President Lee also appointed former National Tax Service chief Lim Kwang-hyun as his new Chief of Staff, signaling a pivot toward economic governance and public welfare over partisan political battles. However, as the Han River Bus scandal demonstrates, maintaining integrity across city administrations remains as challenging as reforming central government institutions.
