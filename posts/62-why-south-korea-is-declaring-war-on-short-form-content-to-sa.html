Why South Korea is Declaring War on Short-Form Content to Save Literacy

September 27, 2026

  • The Crisis: A growing body of research indicates that excessive consumption of short-form content among children is physically altering the brain’s prefrontal cortex, leading to severe declines in attention span and reading comprehension.
  • The Data: Meta-analysis across 30 countries shows that reading printed materials remains 6 to 8 times more effective for comprehension than digital reading.
  • Government Response: The South Korean Ministry of Education, the government body overseeing national education policy, is shifting its strategy from optional reading programs to mandatory deep-reading and discussion-based curricula across all subjects.
  • The Social Impact: With one-third of South Korean youth classified as being at high risk of smartphone dependency, educators report a growing inability among students to grasp complex sentences or sustain focus.

The Neurobiology of Reading

Reading is not a natural human instinct; it is a cognitive "surgery" that the brain performs by repurposing circuits originally designed for vision, hearing, and motor control. Because the human brain did not evolve specifically to read, it requires significant effort to build the neural pathways necessary for deep comprehension. When we abandon long-form reading for the rapid-fire stimuli of short-form videos, we effectively shut down the "operating room" where this critical mental development takes place. This is especially concerning in the digital age, where "skimming" has become the default mode of information consumption. The fragmented, rapid-fire nature of social media clips starves the brain of the resources needed to build complex cognitive structures. Research from Hong Kong suggests that screen exposure during the critical developmental window (under age 12) damages the brain’s executive functions—the very systems needed for planning, impulse control, and logical reasoning.

A Shift Toward "Analog" Education

The South Korean government is finally treating literacy as a matter of national infrastructure rather than a private, parental concern. By revising the Framework Act on Education, authorities aim to make reading a core component of the public school experience. The goal is to force a move away from passive information consumption toward active, critical engagement. For busy professionals and parents, the takeaway is clear: the decline in literacy is not merely a lack of effort but a physiological consequence of our media environment. In a "dopamine desert" created by algorithms, the return to the physical act of reading printed books—which forces the brain to remain present and engaged—has become a radical, necessary act of cognitive preservation. If the next generation is to retain the ability to think critically and challenge the status quo, the digital shortcut must be abandoned in favor of the slow, deliberate work of the page.
