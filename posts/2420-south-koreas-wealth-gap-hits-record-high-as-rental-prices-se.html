South Korea’s Wealth Gap Hits Record High as Rental Prices Set to Surge in 2025

November 17, 2026

South Korea is facing a critical housing "mismatch" as a record wealth gap and a severe shortage of new construction threaten to drive rental prices up more than twice as fast as property values next year.

  • The wealth gap between the top and bottom 10% of homeowners has widened to a record 52-fold difference, driven by the appreciation of high-value properties.
  • A 4.5% surge in jeonse (South Korea’s unique lump-sum deposit rental system) is forecast for 2025, significantly outstripping the projected 2% growth in home sale prices.
  • The "construction cliff"—a sharp drop in new housing starts due to rising material costs and interest rates—is creating a severe supply shortage that will peak early next year.
  • President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the ruling Democratic Party are struggling with a policy deadlock, hesitant to ease regulations for fear of reigniting price volatility in the capital.

A Widening Divide in Real Estate Assets

New data from Statistics Korea reveals that the polarization of housing wealth has reached its most extreme point to date. The top 10% of homeowners now hold assets worth 52 times more than the bottom 10%, a gap fueled by the rising value of premium apartments in Seoul. Simultaneously, the number of households owning homes valued at over 1.2 billion KRW (about $860,000) has grown at its fastest rate ever. Despite aggressive tax and loan regulations intended to curb speculation, there are still over 2.42 million multi-home owners in the country, signaling that the wealthy are doubling down on "blue-chip" properties in prime locations rather than selling off their portfolios.

This concentration of wealth suggests that the "housing ladder"—the ability for lower-income residents to eventually purchase their own homes—is effectively breaking. As the wealthy consolidate their hold on the most desirable real estate, first-time buyers are being priced out and pushed into a tightening rental market. This structural imbalance is not just a social issue; it poses a risk to the broader economy as a significant portion of household wealth remains locked in unproductive real estate assets, potentially stifling consumer spending.

The 2025 Rental Squeeze and Policy Paralysis

The market is bracing for a "jeonse crisis" in early 2025. While home sale prices are expected to remain relatively stable with a 2% increase—largely due to strict debt-to-income limits like the Stress DSR (Debt Service Ratio)—the rental market is under immense pressure. Demand is shifting away from purchasing and toward rentals, just as the supply of new apartments hits a multi-year low. This "construction cliff" is the result of years of high labor and material costs that have deterred developers from breaking ground. In major cities like Busan, rental prices have already risen for 32 consecutive months even as sale prices have fallen, creating a dangerous environment for tenants who may face difficulties recovering their deposits later.

Politically, the Democratic Party, South Korea's ruling party, finds itself in a difficult position. The government of President Lee Jae-myung had initially signaled a desire to move away from the heavy-handed regulations of the previous era, but internal party concerns are mounting. Many lawmakers fear that any significant relaxation of loan limits or property taxes will be blamed for a new spike in Seoul apartment prices, which could damage the party’s standing ahead of future elections. Consequently, the market remains in a state of "wait-and-see," with temporary measures like the resetting of bank loan quotas in January providing only minor, short-term relief to a fundamentally undersupplied market.
