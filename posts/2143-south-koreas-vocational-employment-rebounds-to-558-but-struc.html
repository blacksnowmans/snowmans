South Korea’s Vocational Employment Rebounds to 55.8%, but Structural Flaws Persist

November 09, 2026

South Korea’s vocational high school employment rate rebounded to 55.8% this year, yet roughly half of all graduates still choose university enrollment over entering the workforce directly due to persistent pay gaps and safety concerns.

  • Regional bright spots: Daegu topped national vocational high school employment at 68.7% for the second straight year, while North Gyeongsang Province led net employment at 40.2% for seven consecutive years.
  • Severe equipment and safety deficits: Local lawmakers pointed out that outdated training machinery and inadequate safety oversight during field internships continue to endanger student workers.
  • Stipend shortfalls: Low government training allowances fail to cover basic living costs, forcing young trainees to abandon skill programs for immediate part-time work.
  • Expanding adult vocational channels: Programs ranging from shipbuilder Hanwha Ocean’s employee-led training to Korea University of Technology and Education’s (KOREATECH) online STEP platform are offering alternative upskilling pathways.

A Flattering Headline Masking Systemic Weakness

On paper, a 55.8% employment rate for vocational high schools looks like a welcome recovery. However, the fact that nearly half of graduates immediately pursue higher education reveals deep skepticism toward high-school-graduate jobs in South Korea. Regional hubs like Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province show that tailored industry-academic partnerships can yield results, but nationwide issues—ranging from low starting wages to outdated classroom machinery—keep pushing young talent back into the college entrance pipeline.

Safety remains the most alarming bottleneck. As Gyeonggi Provincial Council member Kim Young-hoon highlighted, student interns face severe risks when dispatched to industrial sites without robust protections. The tragedy of workplace fatalities suffered by vocational trainees during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure underscores the danger of prioritizing employment figures over safety regulations. Without rigorous oversight and modernized facility investments, driving higher placement rates merely pushes teenagers into high-risk labor.

Fixing the Safety Net and Lifelong Training

Beyond high school education, vocational training for adults and job seekers suffers from administrative blind spots. While programs like KOREATECH’s STEP platform and corporate initiatives from Hanwha Ocean demonstrate how practical education can enable upward mobility, inadequate living stipends severely undermine these efforts. When trainees are forced to drop out because allowances do not cover basic food and rent, public job training fails its core mission.

To deliver real progress under President Lee Jae-myung’s job creation agenda, South Korea must modernize vocational school equipment, guarantee strict safety standards for internships, and adjust training stipends for inflation. Adopting comprehensive monitoring mechanisms—similar to recent TVET tracking initiatives seen in developing nations like Nigeria—will ensure that public investments translate into safe, sustainable careers rather than short-term statistical gains.
