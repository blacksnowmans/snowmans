AI Memory Boom Rewrites Chip Dynamics, but Investors Demand More

October 30, 2026

South Korea’s semiconductor titans are riding an unprecedented AI-driven supercycle, yet markets are demanding immediate shareholder payouts over record-breaking balance sheets.

  • Samsung breaks records, stock slides: Samsung Electronics reported a monumental quarterly operating profit of 107.4 trillion won (about $78 billion)—led almost entirely by its semiconductor division (106 trillion won)—yet its shares dropped over 4% after consumer device losses and delayed shareholder return plans disappointed investors.
  • SK Hynix sold out through 2029: SK Hynix posted an astonishing 80% operating margin and announced that its High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) and DRAM inventory is effectively sold out through 2029, securing long-term contracts with global tech giants.
  • Memory suppliers gain leverage over Apple: Soaring enterprise SSD demand from AI data centers has triggered severe NAND flash shortages, eroding Apple’s historic pricing power and forcing device makers to accept painful component cost hikes.
  • Supply chain shifts: Domestic equipment and materials suppliers like Wonik IPS and Korea Zinc are expanding their global footprint, even as subcontracted labor unions demand a fair share of the industry's massive profits.

The Paradox of Record Profits and Shareholder Restlessness

The contrast between operational performance and investor sentiment has rarely been sharper. Samsung's semiconductor division effectively carried the entire conglomerate, masking an 800 billion won (about $580 million) loss in its mobile and consumer appliance unit. While Samsung announced a 30 trillion won cash dividend, foreign and institutional investors dumped shares because detailed plans for share buybacks were postponed until January. Furthermore, Samsung's plan to ramp up capital expenditures to 84 trillion won by 2026 signaled to Wall Street and Yeouido that management remains focused on aggressive hardware spending rather than immediate capital returns.

SK Hynix, on the other hand, saw its stock rise after outlining a 100 trillion won (around $75 billion) shareholder return package alongside historic margins. By locking in over half of its long-term output with Big Tech through multi-year agreements, the company is defying the traditional three-to-four-year boom-and-bust cycle that has long defined the memory market. With next-generation technologies like 4F² capacitorless memory in the pipeline, SK Hynix is aggressively insulating itself against low-cost DRAM competition from Chinese rivals like CXMT.

"RAMageddon" and the Realities of the AI Supply Chain

The explosive demand for AI servers is sparking an industry-wide "chipflation." With Big Tech players like Meta committing nearly $100 billion to AI infrastructure, fabrication lines are prioritized entirely for high-margin enterprise chips. This has triggered severe supply squeezes in consumer-grade NAND flash and PC memory, pushing retail prices of high-performance DDR5 modules up dramatically. Apple, renowned for squeezing component suppliers to preserve gross margins exceeding 80% on storage upgrades, now finds itself on the receiving end of unilateral price hike notices.

Meanwhile, Korea’s semiconductor equipment and materials ecosystem is expanding abroad. Wonik IPS has delivered nearly 800 units of its flagship GEMINI deposition tools, and Korea Zinc is negotiating high-purity semiconductor sulfuric acid supplies with Micron, Intel, and TSMC. Yet this feast at the top exposes deep internal fractures: labor unions across six major subcontractors are now organizing joint protests, demanding that windfall profits trickle down beyond prime contractors. As the Lee Jae-myung administration commits over 1 trillion won in state-backed growth funds to power and solar infrastructure, Korea's challenge is no longer just manufacturing supremacy—it is managing equity across its entire industrial base.
