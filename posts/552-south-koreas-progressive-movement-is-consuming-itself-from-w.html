South Korea's Progressive Movement is Consuming Itself from Within

October 01, 2026

South Korea's ruling progressive camp holds a comfortable parliamentary majority and a strong economy, yet its approval ratings are sliding and its political momentum is stalling due to toxic infighting driven by partisan YouTube influencers and factional purity tests.

  • What happened: President Lee Jae-myung's administration and the Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party, are seeing their approval ratings flatline or drop despite solid economic indicators like a tech sector boom.
  • The root cause: Rather than governing, the progressive camp is consumed by internal purges, labeling allies with dissenting legal views as enemies and traitors.
  • Who is affected: The administration is losing its legislative momentum, public trust is eroding, and civil servants are growing paralyzed as reform agendas stall amid political chaos.

The Toxicity of Factional YouTube Politics

The core of the current crisis lies in an intolerant political culture amplified by partisan YouTube channels and social media. When a progressive policy institute director publicly offered standard legal interpretations regarding a controversial public appointment, he was immediately targeted by hardcore supporters of the ruling party. Instead of engaging in policy debate, the progressive camp's loudest voices treated any minor divergence in legal opinion as an unforgivable betrayal, branding dissenters with derogatory nicknames and treating them as internal enemies.

This atmosphere of ideological purity has replaced healthy political debate with emotional hatred and demands for blind loyalty. The Democratic Party's national convention in August institutionalized this fracture, transforming a party unity event into a mechanism to sideline reform-minded veterans who helped bring the current government to power. Consequently, the party is alienating moderates and trapping itself in an echo chamber.

Stalled Momentum and the Loss of Public Trust

When political energy is diverted toward internal witch-hunts, the government loses its ability to govern. The sliding approval ratings for both the president and the ruling party are clear signals of a deepening trust deficit. Without public confidence, key policy initiatives drift, bureaucratic inertia sets in, and the grand reforms promised by the progressive movement risk fizzling out into empty gestures.

Blaming external critics or the media misses the point. The structural flaw destroying the progressive camp's momentum is self-inflicted division. If South Korea's progressives cannot abandon the politics of absolute conformity and restore a big-tent approach that tolerates internal debate, they will continue to squander the legislative power and economic advantages handed to them by the voters.
