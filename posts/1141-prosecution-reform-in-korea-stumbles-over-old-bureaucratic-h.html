Prosecution Reform in Korea Stumbles Over Old Bureaucratic Habits

October 14, 2026

South Korea’s drive to dismantle the prosecutor’s monopoly on justice is facing a crisis of credibility as the Lee Jae-myung administration promotes a compliant insider to lead the newly minted Serious Crime Investigation Office while silencing a veteran whistleblower.

  • The nominee: Kim Ji-yong, nominated as the inaugural chief of the Serious Crime Investigation Office (SCIO), faced intense questioning at his parliamentary confirmation hearing over his history of rubber-stamping controversial prosecutions.
  • The whistleblower targeted: Minister of the Interior and Safety Yoon Ho-jung ordered an internal audit into Im Eun-jeong, currently head of the SCIO's Gwangju regional branch, accusing her of "political activity using public office" after she criticized institutional cover-ups.
  • The controversy: Lawmakers blasted Kim for admitting he "only opposed in my mind" questionable past indictments—including targeted prosecutions in the 2021 Kim Hak-ui travel-ban case—while formally signing off on them.
  • The broader stakes: The split of investigative and prosecutorial powers, meant to end decades of prosecutorial overreach, risks changing only agency names while preserving institutional self-protection.

The Rubber Stamp vs. The Whistleblower

The contrast at the center of the SCIO rollout could not be sharper. Im Eun-jeong spent years exposing sexual misconduct cover-ups and procedural abuses inside the prosecution. In 2018, she sought an inquiry into supervisors who neglected their duties over a 2015 sexual misconduct scandal. That petition was summarily dismissed by the Supreme Prosecutors’ Office inspection department—headed at the time by none other than nominee Kim Ji-yong. Now, Im faces disciplinary scrutiny for recounting those facts, while Kim stands on the verge of heading the nation’s premier independent investigative body.

Kim’s defense during his hearing laid bare the careerist mindset reformers pledged to eradicate. When pressed on why he approved the selective indictment of working-level officials in the 2021 travel ban against disgraced former Vice Justice Minister Kim Hak-ui—while shielding powerful higher-ups like senior prosecutor Yoon Dae-jin—Kim claimed he thought the acts were not criminal, yet admitted he signed the approvals anyway. As Democratic Party of Korea lawmaker Yoon Kun-young pointed out, claiming to "oppose an order only in your mind" is not a defense; it is the textbook definition of bureaucratic abdication.

Old Wine in New Institutional Bottles

The entire rationale for establishing the Serious Crime Investigation Office and stripping the state prosecution service of direct investigative authority was to end selective, politicized justice. That system routinely shielded its own elite while deploying criminal indictments against political targets and field-level bureaucrats. If the new agency’s inaugural leadership is drawn from figures who facilitated those very practices, the restructuring will amount to little more than cosmetic rebranding.

Worse still, targeting internal whistleblowers like Im sends a chilling signal throughout the civil service. Defining candid testimony about past institutional corruption as illicit "political behavior" shows that the impulse to enforce organizational silence remains alive and well. For true judicial reform to take root under the current administration, the government must abandon the habit of favoring pliant yes-men over those who actually put their careers on the line for institutional integrity.
