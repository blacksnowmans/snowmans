South Korea’s Judiciary Faces Crisis as High-Profile Inmate Nears Release

November 11, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is bracing for a volatile showdown as the Supreme Court has scheduled a hearing for former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo just one day before his detention expires on November 20. Han, who was sentenced to 15 years in prison for insurrection in the first trial, faces the possibility of release, triggering a fierce confrontation between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP).

  • The Trigger: The Supreme Court set the hearing for November 19, sparking fears that procedural delays could lead to the release of a major convict on insurrection charges.
  • Rising Tensions: The ruling DPK has warned of "an all-out war" should Han be released and has floated the idea of impeaching Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.
  • Opposition Strategy: The PPP, the main conservative opposition party, is staging outdoor protests, labeling the government's proposed "Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel Act" as a "prison-break law" designed to protect current President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Stakes: With the nation’s legal system at a crossroads, the judiciary is accused of being either inefficient or politically compromised, leaving the public deeply skeptical of judicial independence.

A Judiciary Under Fire

The decision by the Supreme Court to wait until the final hour to hold a hearing for a figure convicted of such a grave crime as insurrection has fueled public outrage. Critics argue that the judiciary is hiding behind "procedural limits" to justify what looks like a de facto release of a high-ranking political figure. For many, this is a significant retreat from judicial justice, suggesting that a former high-ranking official is receiving preferential treatment compared to ordinary citizens.

The response from the ruling DPK has been equally aggressive. By threatening the impeachment of the Chief Justice, the party is effectively dragging the judiciary into the political arena. This move signals a dangerous trend where legislative power is used to intimidate judicial processes whenever rulings or timelines fail to align with the government's political objectives.

The "Prison-Break" Narrative and Legislative Gridlock

On the other side of the aisle, the PPP has shifted its focus to the streets. By holding an emergency assembly near the presidential office, the opposition is framing the ruling party's legislative agenda—specifically the special counsel bill—as a tool for "prison-breaking" to shield President Lee Jae-myung from ongoing legal scrutiny. The use of such inflammatory rhetoric, matched by the ruling party’s accusations of "second insurrection conspiracies," indicates that the room for parliamentary compromise has all but vanished.

Ultimately, the current standoff reflects a deeper, structural failure. When legal procedures regarding insurrection and special counsel investigations become indistinguishable from partisan warfare, the rule of law itself becomes the primary casualty. Whether the Supreme Court acts to maintain the integrity of its sentence or chooses a path of least resistance on November 19, the decision will likely cement the ongoing crisis of confidence in South Korea’s legal institutions.
