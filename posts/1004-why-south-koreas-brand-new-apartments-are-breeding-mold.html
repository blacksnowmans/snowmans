Why South Korea's Brand-New Apartments are Breeding Mold

October 12, 2026

South Korea’s modern apartment buildings are marvels of energy efficiency, featuring thick insulation and airtight double-glazed windows—yet every winter, their walls are plagued by black mold and condensation.

  • The top defect: According to South Korean government data, condensation (17.8%) is now the leading complaint in new apartment defect assessments, occurring twice as often as water leaks (8.5%).
  • The root cause: Modern buildings are sealed so tightly to save energy that daily household activities like cooking, showering, and drying laundry release about 10 liters (over 2.5 gallons) of trapped moisture daily with nowhere to go.
  • The ventilation trap: While South Korea has mandated heat-recovery ventilation systems in large apartment complexes since 2006, studies show that up to 83% of residents turn them off or ignore them due to noise, filter costs, or unfamiliarity.
  • Rising disputes: Apartment defect dispute filings have surged dramatically, with 2026 figures highlighting a breaking point for residents stuck between soaring heating bills from opening windows and rotting wallpaper from keeping them shut.

For decades, the standard for a great home in South Korea has been an impenetrable fortress that keeps the elements completely out and heating bills firmly down. But as builders and regulators tightened window frames and bulked up insulation to meet energy-saving standards, they inadvertently created an airtight lunchbox effect. A home is an inanimate structure, but the humans living inside it act as continuous moisture generators. When you lock down every seam of a building, you trap not just the heat, but the very humidity of daily life.

The blame game between residents and construction companies misses the forest for the trees. Builders often brush off complaints with a single line telling residents to ventilate more frequently, while dismissing warranty claims under the letter of the law. Yet expecting families to choose between freezing temperatures with open windows, skyrocketing heating bills, or destructive mold is a structural design failure. Moving forward, South Korea's housing market must transition away from manual, easy-to-ignore habits toward automated, sensor-driven ventilation systems that let modern buildings breathe on their own.
