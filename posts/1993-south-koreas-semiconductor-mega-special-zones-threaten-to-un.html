South Korea’s Semiconductor "Mega-Special Zones" Threaten to Unravel Labor Protections

November 05, 2026

The proposed "Mega Special Zone Act" aims to boost South Korea’s semiconductor competitiveness by stripping away fundamental labor protections, effectively legalizing overwork and precarious employment in the nation's most vital industry.

  • The legislation, championed by the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party), introduces "White-Collar Exemptions" and broad exceptions to the 52-hour workweek within designated industrial zones.
  • At Amkor Technology Korea—the world’s second-largest semiconductor packaging firm—veteran workers with 29 years of service report basic monthly wages of just 2.09 million KRW (approximately $1,500).
  • The bill facilitates the "full package" of labor deregulation, including extending the use of temporary contracts and loosening rules on dispatched (outsourced) labor.
  • Labor advocates warn that a drive for 95% equipment utilization is creating a "safety vacuum," leading to reports of increased cancer cases and sudden deaths among cleanroom workers.

The Invisible Workers of the Chip War

While global headlines focus on the "chip war" between giants like Samsung and SK Hynix, the reality for those in the "back-end" of the industry—the packaging and assembly known as OSAT (Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test)—is increasingly grim. At Amkor Technology Korea, the promise of a semiconductor "bonanza" has not reached the factory floor. Despite the company’s global status, 29-year veterans find their basic pay hovering near the minimum wage. This wage stagnation is coupled with a staggering increase in labor intensity; where one worker once managed six machines, automation has ironically pushed that number to 40 machines per person.

This "automated" efficiency comes at a physical cost. Workers describe a environment where 95% machine uptime is the only metric that matters, often at the expense of safety. Safety training is frequently reported as a mere formality—"photo ops" where workers sign attendance sheets while their 40 machines continue to run unattended. In the sealed environments of semiconductor cleanrooms, the lack of transparency regarding Material Safety Data Sheets (MSDS), often hidden behind the veil of "trade secrets," has left workers vulnerable to toxic exposure with little legal recourse.

Political Alignment and Labor’s Betrayal

The political landscape provides a bitter irony for organized labor. The Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party are now the primary drivers of the Mega Special Zone Act, a move critics see as a pivot toward corporate interests under the guise of regional development. During the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (currently imprisoned), the threat of a "69-hour workweek" sparked national outrage. Now, labor leaders argue that the current government is implementing a similarly regressive agenda by carving out "special zones" where labor laws simply do not apply.

The Democratic Party’s focus on securing large-scale investments—such as the $2.2 billion (3 trillion KRW) expansion planned by Amkor—seems to have come at the cost of its traditional labor base. With union membership at Amkor sitting below 1% due to what workers describe as systematic promotion discrimination against union members, there is little internal friction to stop the deregulation. If the Mega Special Zone Act passes in its current form, it will signify a shift where "national security" in the tech sector becomes a permanent justification for the erosion of the 40-hour workweek and the expansion of a disposable workforce.
