K-Pop Heavyweight JYP Faces Backlash Over State-Backed Project Trademark Grab

October 08, 2026

  • What happened: Park Jin-young, a veteran K-pop producer and head of the state-run Popular Culture Exchange Commission, faced intense questioning at a parliamentary audit over allegations of preferential treatment and trademark monopolization tied to a government-backed project called "Phenomenon."
  • Who is affected: South Korea's Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (MCST), taxpayers funding the project (with a budget scaling from about $2.7 million to $11 million), and the "Big Four" K-pop agencies (JYP, SM, YG, and HYBE) that secured early involvement and trademark rights.
  • What it changes: The controversy exposes deep friction between fast-moving private entertainment giants and public administrative safeguards, sparking fierce debate over whether state resources are being used to cement a corporate cartel rather than support the broader cultural ecosystem.

A high-stakes clash at a recent parliamentary audit has put the spotlight on the intersection of state funding and South Korea's powerhouse entertainment industry. Lawmaker Bae Hyun-jin of the conservative People Power Party zeroed in on Park—who leads the Presidential Popular Culture Exchange Commission—over a massive government-backed initiative aimed at creating a Korean equivalent of the Coachella music festival. Critics argue the project bypassed fair procurement processes, allowing private agencies to snap up domestic and international trademark rights while leaving government ministries sidelined.

At the heart of the scandal is a questionable timeline. Barely a month after Park was appointed to lead the commission in late 2024, JYP Entertainment rushed to file domestic trademark rights for the Phenomenon project on the very day the committee officially launched. Subsequent overseas filings and an initial contract draft heavily favored JYP as the primary rights holder, prompting legal experts and lawmakers to question whether public infrastructure was leveraged for private gain. While officials defended the involvement of K-pop's biggest players as a necessary step to leverage global star power, critics point out that an industry already generating massive organic revenue shouldn't require state-sponsored monopolies.
