South Korean Stocks Stage Relief Rally as Battery Sector Surges and Chipmakers Slump

October 19, 2026

South Korean benchmark indexes rebounded sharply on October 19 after an early-morning panic, driven by double-digit gains in battery and biotech heavyweights even as semiconductor giants faced their steepest weekly declines since the 2008 global financial crisis.

  • Market rebound: The benchmark Kospi rose 1.76% and the tech-heavy Kosdaq gained 1.78%, closing at day highs after foreign bargain hunters absorbed heavy morning retail sell-offs caused by broker margin calls.
  • Sector divergence: Secondary battery makers soared—Samsung SDI jumped 11.7% and LG Energy Solution gained over 7% on speculation that Washington may restrict Chinese energy storage systems (ESS)—while chip titans Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix dropped 17.8% and 24.3% over the week.
  • Governance backlash: SK Hynix faced intense domestic anger over plans to list its U.S. NAND subsidiary, Solidigm, on the Nasdaq, prompting lawmaker questioning during a parliamentary audit over the perennial Korean practice of "split-off listings" that dilute existing minority shareholders.
  • Policy response: The current government under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party are advancing amendments to the Commercial Code to expand board directors' fiduciary duties to all shareholders, aiming to cure the persistent "Korea Discount."

A Polarized Rally and the Shadow of Split Listings

The session showcased extreme polarization in Seoul. While the broader market bounced back thanks to foreign inflows and shifting global supply chain narratives around AI data center energy storage, the semiconductor sector remained frozen. The brutal weekly plunge in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix reflects not only shifting global tech sentiment, but also deep domestic investor fatigue with structural corporate governance risks.

SK Hynix's push to list Solidigm on the Nasdaq became a political flashpoint during the National Assembly's audit. Lawmaker Park Sun-hoon of the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition, pressed Financial Supervisory Service Governor Lee Chang-jin on why the chipmaker detailed dilution risks to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission while leaving domestic retail investors in the dark. For decades, Korean conglomerates have enriched controlling families by listing lucrative subsidiaries separately—a double-listing habit that strips value from parent-company shareholders.

This discontent is accelerating legislative momentum. An online public petition demanding stricter curbs on split listings has neared 20,000 signatures, heading for formal committee review. Unlike during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term, when research budgets were slashed and corporate reform lagged, the current Lee administration is pushing to hold boards legally accountable to general shareholders. Until such reforms take root, South Korea's low valuations will continue to offer sharp tactical rebounds, but persistent structural traps for ordinary investors.
