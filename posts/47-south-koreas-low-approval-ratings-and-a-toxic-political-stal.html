South Korea's Low Approval Ratings and a Toxic Political Stalemate

September 01, 2026

South Korean President Yoon Suk Yeol's approval rating has sunk to 36.2%, but the country's main opposition party is suffering from even higher unfavorability numbers as voters reject both sides.

  • The 30-Something Revolt: Voters in their 30s have largely abandoned the administration, with just 27.3% approval amid severe personal financial stress and stock market losses.
  • Anti-Government Sentiment Rules: Roughly half of voters (49.9%) now view the upcoming parliamentary elections as a referendum to punish the current government.
  • A Plague on Both Houses: In a bizarre twist, the main opposition Democratic Party faces a higher unfavorability rating (44.6%) than the ruling conservative People Power Party (40.1%).
  • Self-Serving Maneuvers: Both major parties are accused of treating legislative issues—such as the contentious debate over abolishing the crime of breach of trust—primarily as tools to manage their leaders' ongoing legal risks rather than addressing the economy.

The Death of the Traditional Base and the Rise of the "Least Worst" Vote

South Korean politics has entered a grim phase where elections are no longer won by inspiring voters, but by convincing them you are simply the lesser of two evils. Traditional loyalties are fracturing fast. Generations and regions that once offered reliable safety nets for politicians—such as voters in their 40s and 50s—are showing no hesitation in withdrawing their support when economic realities bite.

The real danger for the political establishment is that this apathy has morphed into active hostility. When nearly half the electorate approaches an election primarily looking to punish those in power, policy debates take a backseat to pure political survival. Yet the opposition is sleepwalking through this crisis, assuming public anger at the government automatically translates into a mandate for them. Instead, voters see a political class entirely consumed by factional infighting and legal defense strategies while everyday household debt and inflation mount unchecked.

Playing Politics While the Economy Bleeds

The recent wrangling over whether to abolish the legal penalty for breach of trust perfectly illustrates why the public holds both major parties in such low regard. What should be a sober discussion of corporate law and economic reform has instead degenerated into frantic legislative math, driven by fears over how specific legal precedents might impact pending corruption trials for major political figures.

When parties weaponize the legislative process to shield their leaders, they accelerate their own political decay. The drop in moderate support—marked by a notable 5.8 percentage point exodus—signals that the center is fed up. Until South Korea's political heavyweights stop treating the National Assembly as a shield for party bosses and start addressing the cost-of-living crisis, the public's bitter mood will only deepen, setting the stage for a volatile political reckoning.
