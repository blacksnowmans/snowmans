The Leadership Test: President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party’s Path to Unity

October 13, 2026

As approval ratings fluctuate, the Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party face a critical test in balancing their reformist drive with the need for inclusive, flexible communication with the public.

  • The Dangers of Conflict: Political analysts warn that the administration must avoid the "trap of winning" against the public, as trying to force persuasion on a resistant populace leads to extreme fatigue and loss of trust.
  • A Warning from History: The failure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently imprisoned—serves as a stark reminder that unilateral, "uncommunicative" governance leads to political ruin.
  • Internal Party Dynamics: Kim Min-seok, the chairman of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's ruling liberal party), is being urged to view internal dissent not as a threat to his leadership, but as a healthy sign of democratic diversity.
  • The Strategy of Frontal Breakthrough: Drawing inspiration from the late President Roh Moo-hyun, the current leadership is encouraged to face challenges directly and transparently rather than resorting to political maneuvers or social media isolation.

The Trap of Unilateral Governance

The core challenge for President Lee Jae-myung is maintaining the "frontal breakthrough" spirit that defines his political identity without appearing deaf to public concerns. When a leader treats every policy dispute like a winner-take-all match, the public begins to feel ignored rather than represented. This is particularly sensitive in the current political climate, where the memory of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration remains fresh. Mr. Yoon’s refusal to pivot in the face of public opposition eventually led to his downfall; for the current government, the lesson is that true political power comes from the ability to humbly accept public judgment and adjust course when necessary.

Furthermore, the psychological isolation of leadership—often described as a sense of "loneliness" or "unfairness" at the top—can be a dangerous barrier to effective governance. When leaders retreat into defensive postures or rely on a narrow circle of supporters, they lose the ability to read the "texture" of public sentiment. Analysts suggest that the administration should focus less on the daily fluctuations of approval ratings and more on whether their policies are creating tangible improvements in the lives of ordinary citizens. In a democracy, power is delegated by the people, and any attempt to "fight" that source of power is inherently self-defeating.

Democratic Unity and the Kim Min-seok Mandate

Within the Democratic Party, the focus has shifted to the leadership style of Chairman Kim Min-seok. As the head of a massive ruling party, Kim faces the difficult task of forging unity among various factions. The current critique is that a "strong" leadership shouldn't be defined by enforced silence or the suppression of different voices. Labeling internal criticism as "leadership damage" risks creating an echo chamber of "yes-men," a phenomenon that historically precedes the decline of major political organizations. Instead, a mature ruling party must treat internal diversity as a resource for spotting policy flaws before they reach the public.

Ultimately, the success of the Lee administration and the Democratic Party depends on their ability to move beyond the "zero-sum" mentality of the past. Reform initiatives—such as those targeting the prosecution or the media—require not just political will, but a high degree of procedural legitimacy and public consensus. By embracing the complexity of public opinion and fostering a more inclusive internal culture, the DPK can avoid the pitfalls of its predecessors. The goal is not just to maintain a majority, but to build a lasting foundation of trust that can withstand the inevitable storms of the political cycle.
