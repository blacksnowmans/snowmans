The Rise of Physical AI: South Korea’s Next Industrial Frontier

November 06, 2026

The global tech landscape is shifting from generative models that create text to "Physical AI"—the integration of intelligence into robotic bodies that operate in the real world. For South Korea, mastering this technology is no longer an option but a prerequisite for economic survival.

  • The "ChatGPT Moment" for Robotics: Industry leaders, including the founder of Chinese firm Unitree, suggest that robots are transitioning from pre-programmed machines to intelligent entities capable of learning and interacting with physical environments.
  • Industrial Deployment: State-run power utility Korea Plant Service & Engineering (KPS) is moving beyond pilot programs, deploying AI robots to handle heavy machinery and safety tasks in high-risk power plant environments.
  • Global Competition: The race to secure "world models"—AI architectures that allow robots to understand physical laws—is intensifying, with China’s recent move to block SoftBank’s acquisition of ABB’s robotics arm highlighting growing geopolitical friction over industrial control.
  • Infrastructure and Regulation: The South Korean government, led by President Lee Jae-myung, is focusing on integrating these technologies into regional industrial hubs to foster growth outside the Seoul metropolitan area, while the Personal Information Protection Commission is drafting new guidelines to address the privacy risks of AI agents in domestic and workplace settings.

From Lab Curiosities to Field Reality

The era of "Physical AI" represents a paradigm shift where the labor market's structure is being fundamentally reordered. While ChatGPT transformed knowledge work, Physical AI is set to redefine physical labor. As seen at the recent 2026 Robot World exhibition, South Korean firms are moving past foreign-dependent hardware to develop autonomous, software-integrated solutions. By utilizing virtual simulators—such as Makeitall's "Ziko Ground"—companies are lowering the barrier to entry, allowing developers to stress-test robots in digital twins before deploying them into real-world factories and logistics centers.

However, this transition brings significant social challenges. As skills become digitized and embedded into robotic systems, the workforce faces a period of inevitable displacement. President Lee Jae-myung’s administration has emphasized human-centric technology, prioritizing labor safety over mere efficiency. The use of robots in hazardous sectors, like power plant maintenance, serves as a model for "kind technology"—innovation that protects human life rather than simply replacing it. Unlike the previous administration of the imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, which often sought to weaken industrial safety laws, the current government is pushing for AI-driven environments that make accidents physically impossible.

Geopolitics and the "World Model" Race

The struggle for dominance in this field is increasingly defined by resource nationalism. China’s interference in the ABB acquisition deal underscores that robots are no longer viewed as consumer goods, but as national strategic assets. For South Korea, the path forward requires a two-track strategy: deepening its self-reliance in core components like speed reducers and servo motors, while aggressively securing global leadership in the "world model" software that serves as the robot's brain.

The push by the Jeonbuk region to become a national hub for robotics, championed by Governor Lee Won-taek, is a strategic move to decentralize the nation's tech ecosystem. By pairing local agricultural and commercial vehicle industries with AI, the region aims to create a sustainable growth model. Ultimately, the winners of the Physical AI era will be those who can balance cutting-edge capability with public trust, ensuring that as these machines enter our homes and factories, they serve as partners rather than silent observers.
