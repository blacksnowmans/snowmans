South Korea's Ruling Democratic Party Realigns with President Lee Jae-myung Amid Legislative Stalemate

November 13, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party has moved to shut down rumors of friction with President Lee Jae-myung's administration, presenting a united front on economic policy while bracing for a showdown with conservative opponents in the National Assembly.

  • President Lee Jae-myung hosted Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok and floor leader Han Byung-do for a three-hour dinner at the presidential office, explicitly signaling policy alignment between the party and the administration.
  • The party officially quashed internal calls from Seoul-area lawmakers to ease the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (종부세), reaffirming its core principle against cutting taxes for high-value property owners.
  • After South Korea's main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, boycotted a scheduled plenary session on November 12, the Democrats pledged to force a vote on key livelihood bills on November 19.
  • Party leaders pushed ahead on broader reforms, including draft legislation to curb "self-reappointment" by commercial bank holding company CEOs and renewed efforts behind an anti-discrimination equality act.

Policy Discipline Trumps Electoral Anxiety Over Real Estate

The three-hour dinner at the presidential office was designed to kill external speculation that the executive branch and ruling lawmakers were diverging on pocketbook issues. Democratic lawmakers representing affluent Seoul districts had floated easing real estate tax burdens to avert voter backlash. However, party leader Kim Min-seok drew a firm line, insisting that policy consistency matters more than short-term electoral maneuvering.

By siding with President Lee's broader agenda, party leadership chose ideological coherence—specifically resisting tax breaks for the wealthy—over piecemeal relief for middle-class homeowners feeling squeezed in the capital. Instead of property tax rollbacks, the ruling coalition is steering its legislative energy toward targeted relief: expanding youth employment and housing budgets by 53.5%, and overhauling corporate governance at major financial groups to prevent executive self-entrenchment by giving independent directors exclusive board nominating power.

Clashes in Parliament and Mounting Pressure on the Courts

The truce inside the ruling camp immediately shifted friction back toward the legislature. After the People Power Party blocked the November 12 plenary session through procedural delays, floor leader Han Byung-do lashed out, accusing conservatives of paralyzing the budget review and waiting on orders from the imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol. The Democrats have committed to holding the November 19 session to advance pending measures, including recognition for pro-democracy activists (the Democratic Merit Act) and Representative Park Ju-min's equality bill.

Simultaneously, the ruling party is turning its guns on the judiciary. Alarmed by procedural delays and the prospect of release for conservative former prime minister Han Duck-soo, Democratic leadership sharply criticized Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. While top leaders have stopped short of endorsing grassroots demands to impeach Chief Justice Cho—a rift highlighted when Representative Park Hee-seung drew fury from progressive activists for calling judge impeachment "something underdeveloped countries do"—the party is openly considering a formal parliamentary inquiry into court delays, signaling an aggressive push against institutional resistance across all fronts.
