90-Meter World Record and a Decade of Growth: The Current State of Korean Freediving

November 14, 2026

Ukrainian diver Kateryna Sadurska has pushed the limits of human endurance with a 90-meter world record in the no-fins category, as the South Korean freediving scene celebrates its tenth anniversary and expands its domestic infrastructure.

  • Kateryna Sadurska reached a depth of 90 meters (approx. 295 feet) in the Constant Weight No Fins (CNF) discipline, holding her breath for 3 minutes and 33 seconds.
  • The Korea Cup, the nation’s first competitive freediving event, celebrated its 10th anniversary, marking a decade of rapid growth for the sport in East Asia.
  • Barrel, a major South Korean sports brand, launched its "Genius Swell" short fins with a 20% pre-order discount until November 15 to capture the growing beginner market.
  • The Yulpo Marine Complex Center in Boseong, a southern coastal county, is now 60% complete and will provide much-needed deep-water training facilities outside the Seoul area.
  • A rescue operation in Taean, South Chungcheong Province, saved a solo night diver drifting in heavy swells, sparking renewed calls for strict adherence to the "buddy system."

The Maturity of the Korean Market

Freediving in South Korea has transitioned from a niche hobby to a mainstream lifestyle sport. The 10th anniversary of the Korea Cup highlights a robust community that now attracts corporate sponsorship and significant media attention. While high-end infrastructure like Deep Station in Yongin and K26 in Gapyeong has historically centered around the Seoul metropolitan area, the upcoming Yulpo facility in Boseong suggests a strategic move to decentralize the sport. This geographic expansion is essential for sustaining the current "freediving craze" by making professional-grade training accessible to the southern provinces.

The market is also seeing a shift in equipment accessibility. Barrel, South Korea's leading name in water sports apparel, is moving beyond swimwear into technical hardware like short fins. By offering an open-heel design that allows for more ankle flexibility, they are targeting pool-based training—a massive segment of the Korean market where enthusiasts practice "static" and "dynamic" disciplines in urban centers before heading to the ocean. This democratization of gear is a clear signal that the industry expects the diver population to continue its upward trajectory.

Tech Innovation vs. Safety Realities

The intersection of Korea’s technology prowess and the diving market is best exemplified by WatchDive, a solution from the Korean startup Diveroid. Scheduled for a Kickstarter launch in December, the product aims to turn standard smartwatches into professional dive computers. This "price disruption" strategy could lower the barrier to entry for many "light" users who find dedicated dive computers prohibitively expensive. However, as the source material notes, the success of such tech will depend entirely on software stability in high-pressure environments where equipment failure can be fatal.

Despite the technological and athletic milestones, the recent rescue in Taean (a coastal region west of Seoul) illustrates the "safety gap" that often accompanies rapid growth. A diver in his 40s was swept away while diving alone at night during heavy swells—violating two of the most basic rules of the sport. While Sadurska’s world record proves what the human body can achieve with elite discipline, the Taean incident serves as a stark reminder that as freediving becomes more popular, the community must ensure that safety education keeps pace with equipment sales and infrastructure development.

"Safety is not a matter of skill, but a matter of following the principles."
