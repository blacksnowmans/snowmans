Korea Pushes $75M Marine Park as Global Diving Headlines Highlight Safety and Reef Restoration

November 15, 2026

South Korea has officially approved a 100.7 billion won (about $75 million) national marine ecological park on the Homigot Peninsula, highlighting a broader shift toward ocean conservation and marine tourism.
  • Major Park Approval: South Korea's Ministry of Economy and Finance passed the preliminary feasibility study for the Homigot Peninsula National Marine Ecological Park in Pohang, funding eelgrass restoration, sea turtle protection, and deep-sea eco-exhibits.
  • Record Artificial Reef: The historic 300-meter ocean liner SS United States was successfully sunk off the coast of Florida, resting upright on the seabed to create the world's largest artificial reef.
  • Warming Waters in Jeju: National Institute of Fisheries Science researchers confirmed five tropical and subtropical fish species off Jeju Island for the first time, underscoring rapid ecological shifts alongside fatal diving accidents that prompted renewed health warnings.
  • Global Tech & Safety: Saudi Arabia launched a Red Sea coral restoration initiative utilizing Samsung-backed monitoring modules, while dive accidents in Egypt and Cyprus triggered international calls for equipment maintenance and conservative dive profiles.

Korea’s Marine Investment Meets Changing Ocean Ecosystems

The approval of Pohang's 100.7 billion won (about $75 million) ecological park marks a major commitment by South Korean authorities to strengthen marine conservation along the East Coast. Designed to restore coastal habitats and protect species such as sea turtles, the project reflects how local governments are blending environmental preservation with eco-tourism. This comes as South Korea's waters undergo noticeable shifts: state marine researchers recently spotted five previously unrecorded tropical fish species off Jeju Island, providing clear evidence of rising sea temperatures.

However, environmental shifts and increased coastal activity bring operational challenges for local divers and municipalities. Alongside regional economic initiatives—such as a new two-day dive package in Tongyeong launched by the Korea Fishing Ports and Harbors Association—domestic safety concerns have heightened following fatal diving incidents in Jeju and dozens of unauthorized river diving reports along Seoul's Han River bridges.

Global Trends: High-Tech Conservation and Strict Safety Discipline

Globally, the diving community is balancing ambitious conservation technology with sobering safety lessons. Saudi Arabia's King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST) recently opened a massive Red Sea coral restoration facility using underwater monitoring modules co-developed with tech giant Samsung Electronics. Meanwhile, marine scientists mapped a massive, untouched coral colony in the remote Pacific, proving that natural ecosystem resilience remains viable when marine habitats are protected.

Yet, recent international incidents—including a liveaboard boat fire off Hurghada, Egypt, and a fatal deep-wreck dive on Cyprus’s Zenobia—highlight why pre-dive checks and conservative limits remain non-negotiable. Online dive communities on Reddit have actively debated these risks, warning against relying on consumer smartwatches like the Apple Watch Ultra as primary dive computers instead of dedicated equipment. As water temperatures drop in the Northern Hemisphere, both Korean and international divers are being urged to prioritize equipment checks and physical preparation over novelty gear.
