Why South Korea's Ruling Party Is Bleeding Support

October 09, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's liberal ruling Democratic Party has seen its approval ratings slide to a low of 35%, driven by voter fatigue over internal factionalism and lingering frustration with the prosecution service.
  • What it changes: President Lee Jae-myung's administration faces mounting pressure to deliver tangible economic gains and push through sweeping prosecutorial reforms before public disillusionment damages its core base.
  • Who is affected: Grassroots party members who powered the current administration into office are increasingly alienated by leadership silence and infighting, threatening the party's cohesion ahead of future elections.

South Korea's progressive landscape is facing an unexpected crisis of confidence. Despite securing the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung, the ruling Democratic Party is grappling with a steep drop in public support. A recent episode of the political commentary show hosted by Hong Sa-hun brought these tensions to the surface, highlighting a wave of quiet despair among loyal supporters who feel abandoned by a party leadership seemingly disconnected from reality.

At the root of the voter backlash is a deep-seated frustration with the nation's prosecutorial system. Critics on the broadcast pointed out that despite past vows to rein in prosecutorial overreach—a legacy famously abused during the tenure of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol—the institutional DNA of the prosecution remains largely unchanged. Prosecutors continue to wield investigative powers to protect their own corporate interests, operating outside robust democratic control. High-profile missteps, such as leadership shuffling and controversial personnel recommendations surrounding figures like nominee Kim Ji-yong, have convinced the party's base that meaningful judicial reform is stalling due to institutional self-preservation and political half-measures.

Internal Infighting and the Perils of Leadership Silence

Compounding the policy friction is a leadership vacuum regarding plummeting approval numbers. According to senior party figures, discussions on the party's 35% approval floor have been conspicuously absent from official meetings. Instead of addressing the alienation of traditional voters—many of whom have endured decades of political heartbreak—some party voices have resorted to enforcing strict internal conformity, branding dissenting opinions as treasonous or factionalist. This environment of mutual suspicion and aggressive fandom politics has alienated the very grassroots activists who carried the party to victory.

President Lee has taken to social media to warn against radical purists who weaponize ideological purity for self-interest while sabotaging governance. Yet, this reliance on direct digital communication also underscores a broader systemic weakness: a hostile, conservative-leaning mainstream media that glosses over the administration's economic achievements while hyper-focusing on manufactured internal scandals. To secure long-term stability, the ruling bloc must move past internal purges, rely on member-driven democracy, and aggressively communicate its legislative and economic wins to a weary public before the cracks in the progressive coalition become too wide to bridge.
