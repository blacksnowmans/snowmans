South Korea to Launch New Childcare Allowance for Freelancers and Solo Entrepreneurs

October 10, 2026

The South Korean government, led by President Lee Jae-myung, is moving to bridge a major gap in the nation’s social safety net by introducing a new "childcare allowance" specifically designed for individuals currently excluded from the traditional employment insurance system.

  • What happened: The government is creating a new subsidy program for self-employed individuals, freelancers, and contract workers who do not qualify for standard parental leave benefits.
  • Why it matters: Existing parental leave policies are tied to employment insurance, leaving those without steady corporate affiliation—or those who cannot afford to halt business operations—entirely unprotected during early parenthood.
  • What’s next: The government is pushing to bypass standard preliminary feasibility studies to launch the program by June of next year, signaling a sense of urgency to combat the country’s record-low birth rate.

Expanding the Safety Net Beyond Traditional Employment

For many workers, taking leave to raise a child is a standard right. For solo entrepreneurs and freelancers, however, it is a luxury that often leads to bankruptcy. Because the current system requires 180 days of employment insurance coverage, those who do not fit the traditional "salaryman" mold have been effectively left to fend for themselves.

The new allowance marks a shift in policy philosophy: moving away from a system centered solely on payroll employees to one that supports "all working parents." By delinking benefits from the strict definition of "leave" and focusing on income loss due to childcare, the administration aims to provide a more flexible form of support. Whether this will be enough to cover the actual costs of shutting down a one-person business remains a point of debate, but it represents a significant move toward inclusive social welfare.

Policy Improvements and Agricultural Support

Beyond childcare, the government is utilizing "gender impact assessments"—a tool that reviews laws and policies for potential biases—to refine public service equality. Last year alone, this process resulted in over 4,400 concrete improvements, including new protections for court-appointed attorneys facing pregnancy-related career penalties. This proactive approach marks a departure from the divisive political environment that characterized the tenure of the previous administration under the now-imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol.

The government is also strengthening support for the agricultural sector. The administration has set a target to purchase 400,000 tons of public reserve rice, with a significant increase in interim payments to farmers—raising the price per 40kg bag by about $14 (20,000 KRW). Additionally, a new pilot program will offer price premiums for farmers who produce high-quality, eco-friendly rice, shifting the focus from mere quantity to long-term agricultural competitiveness.

The Bottom Line: Under President Lee Jae-myung, the government is signaling a shift toward a more responsive social model. While the success of these programs will ultimately depend on their real-world implementation and funding levels, the administration’s focus on closing institutional loopholes for freelancers and farmers suggests a move toward a more comprehensive and equitable social contract.
