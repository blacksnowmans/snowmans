Why South Koreans are Avoiding the "Three-Dong" Social Circles

September 11, 2026

In South Korea, a new, cynical term has emerged among married couples: the "Three-Dong" (3동) clubs. This shorthand refers to hiking groups (Dung-san), hobby clubs (Dong-ho-hoe), and alumni associations (Dong-chang-hoe)—social circles increasingly viewed by the public not as innocent pastimes, but as the primary breeding grounds for infidelity.

  • The "Three-Dong" Threat: Online discourse and legal statistics suggest these three types of social gatherings are frequently cited as the point of origin for extramarital affairs.
  • Physical Proximity: High-intensity or social hobbies like camping, hiking, tennis, and dancing are flagged for creating environments where boundaries are easily blurred.
  • The Danger of Nostalgia: Alumni groups are criticized for using shared past experiences to bypass the "getting to know you" phase, accelerating emotional intimacy.
  • A Call for Isolation: The prevailing advice in this narrative is that "cool" partners who allow total freedom are often the first to suffer the consequences of infidelity.

The Anatomy of Modern Infidelity

While the debate over whether men and women can maintain platonic friendships is global, the South Korean perspective currently leans heavily toward skepticism. The "Three-Dong" phenomenon is being treated as a social "truth" rather than a joke. Legal professionals and divorce experts report that a significant portion of marital breakdowns can be traced back to these seemingly harmless weekend activities. The danger, experts suggest, is not necessarily a premeditated search for an affair, but the vulnerability created by loneliness, alcohol, and the casual nature of these "hobbyist" settings.

Even individual lessons—like one-on-one tennis or personal training—are viewed with suspicion. The close physical contact and the inevitable "after-party" dinners are often the catalysts that transform a professional relationship into something personal. In a hyper-connected society where individuals may feel isolated from their partners, these clubs offer a temporary sense of belonging, but often at the cost of the marital unit.

Past Memories as a Catalyst

Alumni associations, or Dong-chang-hoe, pose a unique risk because they utilize a "cheat code" for intimacy: shared history. Unlike a blind date, where partners must work to build a connection, alumni meetings provide an immediate emotional shortcut. Participants are often bored with their current domestic lives and find a dangerous, seductive allure in seeing a familiar face from a different time in their lives.

This reality has led to a hardened approach to managing marital boundaries. The prevailing sentiment is that trusting a partner is not enough; one must actively discourage environments where temptation can flourish. While some might dismiss this as excessive jealousy or "germaphobic" paranoia, many South Koreans now argue that protecting a marriage requires an aggressive, preemptive defense. In this view, being "cool" about your spouse’s late-night hiking trips or old-school reunions isn't a sign of maturity—it’s an invitation for disaster.
