Jensen Huang Steps Down From Nvidia, Threatening South Korea’s Semiconductor Supply Chain

November 13, 2026

Nvidia Chief Executive Jensen Huang has abruptly resigned following an admission of an extramarital affair and mounting political pressure over alleged Chinese intelligence ties, triggering immediate instability across global markets and South Korean tech suppliers.

  • What happened: Huang stepped down after 33 years at the helm, leaving Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress to take over as interim manager while Nvidia’s board searches for a permanent successor.
  • What drove the exit: The personal misconduct confession coincided with media allegations of technical leaks to China, prompting U.S. lawmakers to accelerate three major legislative bills targeting AI chip exports to Beijing.
  • Who is affected: South Korean chipmakers Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—which rely heavily on Nvidia for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) orders—face direct revenue risks, alongside thousands of South Korean retail investors holding Nvidia shares.
  • The broader market shift: The abrupt transition threatens to disrupt product rollouts, including Nvidia's next-generation Blackwell architecture, while accelerating interest in open-source AI software frameworks.

A Geopolitical and Leadership Crisis at Nvidia

Jensen Huang was more than just Nvidia's founder; his technical vision and charismatic authority effectively served as the central roadmap for the entire artificial intelligence boom. Replacing him with CFO Colette Kress signals a shift toward risk mitigation and financial stewardship, but a financial specialist cannot easily replace a visionary engineer. Kress faces the daunting task of steadying an anxious workforce, preserving partner alliances, and keeping critical product timelines like the Blackwell architecture on track without Huang’s hands-on direction. Competitors like AMD and Intel, along with cloud giants developing custom silicon, are positioned to exploit this sudden vacuum.

The geopolitical ramifications are equally severe. With Huang’s exit, Washington’s bipartisan push for stricter semiconductor export controls will likely intensify. American lawmakers are moving swiftly to tighten security oversight, elevating national security above corporate market share. For Chinese tech firms, this fallout accelerates a desperate race to hoard inventory before access is cut entirely, while pushing developers toward open-source software alternatives that bypass Nvidia’s proprietary CUDA ecosystem.

The Direct Shock to South Korea's Chip Sector

For South Korea, the upheaval at Nvidia directly hits the nation's economic crown jewels: memory chip titans Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. Both firms have tied their recent earnings momentum to supplying high-value HBM memory for Nvidia's AI accelerators. SK Hynix, which maintains an exceptionally high exposure to Nvidia's order books, is particularly vulnerable if leadership uncertainty delays Nvidia’s production cycles or dampens overall AI infrastructure spending. Furthermore, heightened U.S. scrutiny over Chinese supply chains puts extra operational pressure on Samsung and SK Hynix's fabrication plants located inside China.

South Korean retail stock traders—who have heavily bought into overseas tech stocks in recent years—are facing acute volatility. Nvidia has long been a favorite holding among Korean retail investors who viewed the company as an unbeatable proxy for AI growth. With the central icon of that thesis gone and governance risks taking center stage, Korean investors are being forced to re-evaluate their exposure, marking a painful transition from unchecked market optimism to cautious risk management.
