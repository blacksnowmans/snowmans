The End of the Production Truck: How IP-Based PTZ Cameras are Scaling Live Events

November 17, 2026

The era of massive broadcast crews and heavy cabling is ending as PTZ cameras and IP protocols like NDI and SRT allow small teams to execute complex live productions over a single LAN cable.

  • Automation over Manpower: Advanced controllers use motion presets to automate smooth pans and tilts, allowing a single operator to perform tasks that previously required a full team of camera operators.
  • Single-Cable Logistics: The combination of Power over Ethernet (PoE) and NDI (Network Device Interface) protocols bundles video, audio, control, and power into one ethernet cable, drastically reducing setup and strike times.
  • Geographic Independence: The SRT (Secure Reliable Transport) protocol facilitates remote production, enabling a director in one city to switch a live feed from cameras located hundreds of miles away via the public internet.
  • Network Infrastructure Risks: While efficient, IP-based production requires professional-grade managed switches and network expertise to avoid latency issues and "blackouts" that consumer hardware cannot handle.

From Heavy Gear to Agile Networks

In the professional video scene, the shift toward PTZ (Pan-Tilt-Zoom) systems is a direct response to rising labor costs and the physical toll of traditional broadcasting gear. By using modern controllers like the RP150-N—which features a built-in 5-inch screen—a single operator can now monitor and control multiple units without the fatigue of staring at a distant multi-viewer wall. This setup eliminates the need for separate preview monitors and intercom stations at every camera position.

The real efficiency gain comes from "Motion Presets." In settings like large church services or corporate conferences, certain shots—like a slow pan across a choir or a stage—are repetitive. Previously, these required a dedicated operator to ensure a smooth, manual move. Now, these trajectories can be recorded and triggered with a single button. This automation allows the technical director to focus on the overall flow of the broadcast rather than the mechanical movement of individual lenses.

The Rise of Remote Switching

The most disruptive change in the industry is the decoupling of the production team from the physical event location. Using SRT, a protocol designed for stable video delivery over unpredictable networks, production companies can now send only the cameras and a skeleton setup crew to a venue in Seoul, while the director, switcher, and graphics team remain in a studio in Busan. This "Remote Integration" (REMI) model eliminates travel expenses, lodging, and the risk of damaging expensive switching gear during transport.

"The ability to send five camera feeds from a venue to a studio halfway across the country for real-time switching was once the exclusive domain of multi-million dollar satellite trucks. Today, it happens over a standard internet connection."

However, professionals must remain wary of "IMAG" (Image Magnification) latency. While a 100-millisecond delay is invisible to a YouTube viewer, it creates a jarring "lip-sync" error for a live audience watching the speaker on a giant LED screen behind the podium. For high-stakes live events, a hybrid approach remains the gold standard: using direct 12G-SDI cables for on-site screens to ensure zero latency, while utilizing the IP network for recording, streaming, and remote production. As cameras become network endpoints, the role of the broadcast engineer is rapidly evolving into that of a network administrator.
