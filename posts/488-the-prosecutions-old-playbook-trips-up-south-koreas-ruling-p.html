The Prosecution's Old Playbook Trips Up South Korea's Ruling Party

September 09, 2026

  • What happened: Conservative politician Han Dong-hun leaked selective prosecutor files to target South Korea's ruling Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Seung-won, triggering a furious political defense on pro-government media.
  • What it changes: The scandal has exposed deep contradictions in President Lee Jae-myung's administration, which critics say is preserving rather than dismantling the powerful prosecution service it once vowed to reform.
  • Who is affected: Lawmaker Kim Seung-won faces intense scrutiny over past legal and ethical controversies ahead of his ministerial confirmation hearing, while the ruling party loses moral high ground on judicial reform.

A Reformist Government That Clings to Prosecutor Power

South Korea's progressive administration came to power promising to dismantle the formidable, politicized apparatus of the state prosecution service. Yet, the current reality looks entirely different. Instead of cutting the prosecution down to size, the ruling camp has tried to seize control of the levers of prosecutorial power for itself. The predictable result is a system where internal leaks are weaponized by opposition figures like Han Dong-hun—the former conservative justice minister—while the ruling party scrambles to protect scandal-tarred insiders.

The appointment of Kim Ji-yong, a former high-ranking prosecutor with deep ties to the old establishment, as head of the newly created Investigation Office has only deepened the disillusionment. Critics from civil society groups and minor progressive parties argue this amounts to little more than the old prosecution service returning under a fresh coat of paint. Rather than an independent anti-corruption body, the new office risks becoming an instrument of partisan politics.

Ethics, Scandals, and the Cost of Blind Partisanship

Public sympathy for lawmaker Kim Seung-won is remarkably thin, even among supporters of the ruling bloc. Kim carries heavy political baggage, including a past drunk-driving conviction that was omitted from election literature and unaddressed allegations regarding preferential treatment in business ventures. When conservative media outlets and politicians push out heavily edited snippets of prosecution records to attack him, the ruling party's reflexive circling of the wagons looks less like standing up for justice and more like blind tribalism.

This dynamic leaves many progressive voters deeply alienated. When leaders abandon rigorous moral standards to protect compromised allies, they hand their political opponents an easy weapon. The upcoming confirmation hearings threaten to turn into a messy, hollow spectacle where neither side cares about the truth, and the grand promise of systemic reform is sacrificed on the altar of short-term political survival.
