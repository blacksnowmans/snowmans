When Politicians Hang Up on Live TV and Culture Wars Erupt: A Snapshot of Korea's Media Fatigue

September 30, 2026

South Korea’s media landscape is growing increasingly exhausted by a toxic mix of political arrogance and culture-war flashpoints, from politicians blowing off live interviews to bitter fights over historical films.

  • The Incident: Song Young-gil, a prominent former political party leader, abruptly hung up during a live broadcast, casually telling the studio he was busy eating dinner with the president.
  • The Culture War: The film Assassins has reignited fierce conservative backlash and censorship demands over its depiction of historical figures, specifically touching on conspiracy theories surrounding former authoritarian leader Park Chung-hee.
  • The Problem: Both events highlight a pervasive disdain for public accountability, where politicians treat broadcasting as a disposable PR tool and ideologues treat art as a battlefield.
  • Who is Affected: Media production crews are left scrambling to apologize and clean up after entitled figures, while the general public is fed an endless diet of cynical, polarizing spectacles.

Power, Arrogance, and the Death of Live Broadcasting

When a public figure cuts off a live, national interview mid-sentence because dinner with the president calls, it is more than just an embarrassing gaffe—it is a glaring symptom of institutional entitlement. South Korean politicians often treat media appearances not as a solemn duty to inform the electorate, but as convenient megaphones to be picked up and discarded at will. This casual dismissal shifts the burden entirely onto studio producers and production staff, who must absorb the blowback and smooth over the chaos.

This same contempt for public boundaries bleeds directly into the cultural sphere. Whenever a film like Assassins dares to touch raw nerves in South Korea's complex modern history—particularly regarding the Park Chung-hee era—opposing political factions rush to slap labels of "distortion" or "propaganda" on it before the credits even roll. Rather than treating art as a space for open debate, critics demand outright bans and ideological purity tests. It is a suffocating cycle that reduces nuanced historical reflection into knee-jerk partisan warfare.

Trading Substance for Sensationalism

At their core, these two distinct controversies share the exact same DNA: a deep-seated elitism that assumes the public can be easily manipulated or ignored. Politicians believe their core base will forgive any behavioral lapse, while culture-war zealots believe audiences lack the intelligence to evaluate art on its own terms. In a media ecosystem driven by viral YouTube clips and polarized thumbnails, thoughtful deliberation loses out to whoever can generate the most outrage.

South Korea’s democracy relies on a healthy, functional tension between politics, media, and artistic expression. When political power attempts to bully the culture and treat public platforms like private chat rooms, the public is left with a hollowed-out public square. Moving past this fatigue requires rejecting petty partisan theater and demanding basic respect for both the media and the public intelligence.
