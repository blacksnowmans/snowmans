Why South Korea’s Liberals Win by Energizing Their Base, Not Courting the Center

October 24, 2026

South Korea’s liberal bloc is locked in a sharp internal debate over electoral strategy, with prominent progressive figures arguing that retaining power requires mobilizing non-voters and confronting an entrenched judiciary, rather than diluting core principles to court conservative moderates.

  • The strategic rift: Commentators on the political broadcast Hong Sa-hoon Show, including lawmaker Shin Jang-sik and former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook, rejected claims that progressives are a "structural minority," warning that shifting toward the political center alienates core supporters without winning over swing voters.
  • Targeting non-voters: Rather than fruitlessly trying to convert committed conservatives, analysts urge the ruling Democratic Party of Korea to focus on the roughly 40% of the electorate that abstains from voting by delivering tangible social safety nets and bold reforms.
  • Judicial backlash: Public fury among liberals has intensified following an appellate court's acquittal of conservative Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon (of the main opposition People Power Party) regarding 33 million won (about $24,000) in suspicious bank transfers linked to political polling.
  • Calls for impeachment: Reformist figures, including Cho Kuk of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, are escalating demands to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, accusing the courts of systematically protecting conservative contenders while discarding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol as a political scapegoat.

Mobilization Over Political Conversion

The debate highlights a critical strategic tension inside President Lee Jae-myung’s ruling coalition: whether to play cautious defense by moderating liberal platforms, or to push forward on sweeping reform. As political pollster Park Si-young pointed out, much of the public resistance liberals faced in recent years stemmed from targeted investigations and hostile media framing during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, not an inherent rejection of progressive ideals. Attempting to appease conservative commentators by sidelining loyal base voters threatens to gut the party's core engine.

Electoral history shows that durable liberal majorities are rarely forged by convincing conservative partisans to switch sides. Commentators argue that true expansion happens when a government provides tangible economic and social efficacy, motivating cynical, disaffected non-voters to show up on election day. Diluting platforms such as judicial reform or universal welfare to appear "moderate" leaves both dedicated party members and swing voters with little reason to stay engaged.

The Judicial Battleground

The appellate court's exoneration of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has brought this political rift into sharp relief. To the progressive base, the court's willingness to dismiss documented financial transfers as standard transactions highlights an ongoing institutional double standard. While Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol remains behind bars following his historic impeachment, critics argue that the judicial establishment under Chief Justice Cho is actively sheltering alternative conservative leaders to keep progressive governance in check.

For the ruling Democratic Party, attempting to sidestep these institutional clashes out of election jitters is a dangerous trap. Reluctance to fight only breeds voter fatigue; sustaining power will depend on confronting institutional imbalances head-on and delivering the clear policy results its supporters demand.
