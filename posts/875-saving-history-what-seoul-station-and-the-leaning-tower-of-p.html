Saving History: What Seoul Station and the Leaning Tower of Pisa Teach Us About Urban Renewal

October 09, 2026

How do you save a historic landmark when tearing it down is illegal, but keeping it as-is makes modern life impossible?

  • Seoul Station's historic red-brick predecessor: Originally built in 1925 as Gyeongseong Station during the Japanese colonial period, the grand Renaissance-style building survived war and rapid industrialization before the KTX bullet train made its cramped platforms obsolete.
  • The pivot to preservation: Designated as Historic Site No. 284, the old station could not be demolished. Instead, planners built a modern terminal nearby and transformed the 1925 building into Culture Station Seoul 284, a vibrant arts and exhibition space.
  • The parallel of Pisa: Similarly, the Leaning Tower of Pisa was saved from a catastrophic 5.5-degree tilt not by adding heavy lead weights, but by counter-intuitively extracting small amounts of soil from beneath its higher side to bring it back to a safe 4-degree angle.
  • The hidden cost of renewal: While transforming old buildings into cultural hubs breathes new life into cities, it frequently triggers gentrification, driving out long-standing residents and small business owners as rents skyrocket.

The Trap of Quick Fixes and Brute Force

Humanity loves a shortcut. When faced with structural flaws or social complexities, our first instinct is often to apply brute force—whether that means slapping 900 tons of lead onto the sinking foundation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa or pushing to bulldoze outdated train stations in favor of gleaming glass towers. Yet, as both architectural history and urban planning show, superficial Band-Aid fixes usually store up worse disasters for later.

The decision to convert Seoul's old colonial-era station into an arts platform rather than demolishing it was a masterclass in lateral thinking. It acknowledged that a city without its architectural memory is like an amnesia patient. Preserving these spaces is rarely efficient or cheap, and forcing modern needs into historic envelopes creates constant friction. But cities are not spreadsheets driven purely by capital efficiency; their identity relies on the layers of time they manage to hold onto.

Beyond Nostalgia: The Art of the Pivot

Whether dealing with a crumbling 800-year-old Italian bell tower or a century-old railway terminus, the underlying lesson is about framing problems correctly. The engineers who saved Pisa didn't try to violently shove the tower upright; they studied gravity, soil mechanics, and physics to let the structure gently right itself. Likewise, urban regenerators didn't cling to the impossible idea of running modern high-speed rail through a 1920s terminal—they changed the building's job description entirely.

In a world obsessed with maximum speed and disposable real estate, these interventions remind us that preservation is not about freezing a city in amber. It is about finding a functional, breathing compromise between past memories and contemporary demands. If a red-brick train station can learn to carry art instead of steam engines, our approach to modern urban challenges could use a similar dose of creative reinvention.
