Gyeongju's Global Boom: How Silla's Ancient Capital Became South Korea's Hottest Tourist Destination

September 11, 2026

  • Driven by upcoming high-level international summits and soaring rail passenger numbers, Gyeongju is experiencing an unprecedented surge in foreign visitors.
  • Global travel platforms and K-drama exposure have turned ancient sites like Bulguksa Temple and Daereungwon Tomb Complex into essential pilgrim stops for international travelers.
  • Strict urban design rules and night-time lighting are successfully transforming the city from a day-trip stop into an overnight destination.

Mention Gyeongju to anyone who grew up in South Korea, and they will likely think of mandatory elementary school field trips to look at old rock piles. But South Korea's ancient capital—home to the Silla Dynasty (57 BC–935 AD)—is undergoing a massive structural overhaul. While Seoul and Busan handle the bulk of inbound travel, Gyeongju is quietly emerging as a powerhouse of its own. Foreign railway users spiked 46.5% to nearly 1.7 million in the first quarter, proving that the city's push to attract international tourists is paying off.

The city's appeal lies in its unique geographic and visual setup. Located just over two hours from Seoul and 30 minutes from Busan via the KTX high-speed rail, Gyeongju offers seamless access similar to Kyoto's relation to Osaka. Walk into Gyeongju Station, and the platform is crowded with international families and couples pulling suitcases. Sites like Bulguksa Temple, featured extensively on global platforms like Klook and GetYourGuide, now draw crowds of diverse nationalities who view the historic structures less as dusty relics and more as a living architectural museum.

Tradition Meets Modernity: From Hwangridan-gil to Night Illumination

Much of Gyeongju's staying power comes from how it blends daily life with history. Hwangridan-gil, a trendy neighborhood where building height and traditional tile-roof architecture are strictly regulated, commands the longest average stay for visitors at 1 hour and 21 minutes. International tourists queue for cheese-filled coin bread and herbal street snacks, walking through a neighborhood that feels entirely removed from modern concrete blocks. Nearby, the Daereungwon Tomb Complex—massive grassy burial mounds of ancient royals—draws glowing Google reviews from travelers who playfully compare the green hills to the Teletubbies' home or a "Korean pyramid."

Nighttime is where Gyeongju truly seals its transition into a multi-day destination. Sites like Woljeongyeoh Bridge—reconstructed using both ancient and new stones to show its history—and Donggung and Wolji Pond offer stunning night views. Visitors sit on stepping stones listening to stream water and enjoying a level of nighttime safety that surprises many Western travelers. Local merchants note that travelers from the US, Europe, and across Asia are packing the streets after dark, driving a shift from quick daytime bus tours to immersive, overnight stays.

Yet this boom brings warnings. Rapid commercialization risks sparking overtourism, rising rents, and displacement for local residents—issues familiar to overcrowded tourist hotspots across the country. Furthermore, visitors frequently complain about a lack of multilingual signage and audio guides, highlighting that basic infrastructure must catch up with marketing goals. If Gyeongju wants to sustain its success without sacrificing its local identity, it must balance quantitative visitor growth with community-led governance. For now, the ancient capital proves that history does not have to stay trapped behind museum glass to captivate the world.
