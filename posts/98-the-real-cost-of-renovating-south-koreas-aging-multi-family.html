The Real Cost of Renovating South Korea’s Aging Multi-Family Buildings

September 27, 2026

Renovating a 20-year-old multi-family "daga-gu" building—a low-rise residential property common in South Korea designed for multiple households—is a financial minefield where initial quotes often fail to account for the harsh realities of aging infrastructure.

  • Total Investment: A comprehensive renovation of a 19-unit building totaled approximately $165,000 (224.7 million KRW).
  • Hidden Costs: Nearly $18,000 (24.35 million KRW) was spent solely on unforeseen issues like plumbing leaks, gas pipe replacements, and structural surprises.
  • Cost-Saving Strategy: By opting for "semi-self" contracting—managing individual specialized workers rather than hiring a single general construction firm—the owner successfully trimmed a $185,000 (250 million KRW) estimate.
  • The "Human" Factor: Success relied on active on-site management and building rapport with workers, proving that building renovations are as much about communication as they are about construction.

The Hidden Traps of Aging Infrastructure

The primary mistake new property owners make is focusing on aesthetics while ignoring the "invisible" health of the building. With structures over two decades old, water leaks and outdated safety systems are inevitable. Investing $900 (1.2 million KRW) in a pressure test for plumbing might seem like an unnecessary expense, but it is effectively an insurance policy against catastrophic post-renovation leaks that would require tearing up brand-new floors.

Similarly, gas line safety is non-negotiable. Allocating $8,800 (12 million KRW) to replace gas pipes for 18 units is a significant hit to the budget, but it is a necessary safety standard that prevents long-term liability. When dealing with demolition, owners should include a "real-cost settlement" clause in contracts to handle the inevitable structural surprises, such as steel reinforcement bars hidden in walls, which often cause construction quotes to balloon midway through a project.

Strategic Spending for Profitability

For an income-producing property, the goal is not to create a luxury showroom, but to provide a clean, functional living space. Opting for mid-range fixtures—like 22mm glass instead of high-end 24mm premium brands—saved thousands without sacrificing tenant satisfaction. However, cutting corners on the "first impression" is a mistake; spending $2,200 (3 million KRW) on professional door films and $1,500 (2 million KRW) on a new owner-unit entrance door significantly elevated the building's perceived value.

The most important takeaway for investors is that the renovation budget must include a substantial buffer for "finishing touches." Furnishing 18 rooms with basic appliances and furniture alone cost over $25,000 (roughly 34 million KRW). If you do not have a dedicated reserve fund for these final-stage costs, you will likely face a liquidity crisis just as the project nears completion.

Ultimately, a successful renovation is not about beating a quote; it is about managing the information asymmetry between the owner and the contractors. By staying present, managing the site daily, and accepting that a perfect plan is impossible, owners can transform a depreciating asset into a sustainable, income-generating property for the next 20 years.
