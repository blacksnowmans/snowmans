Why South Korea's Stock Market Is Lagging While Wall Street Soars

October 07, 2026

While U.S. markets hit record highs driven by the artificial intelligence boom, South Korea's stock market is suffering from a severe crisis of confidence, marked by foreign shareholdings in tech giant Samsung Electronics dropping to a 13-year low.

  • The U.S. Contrast: Nvidia trades at a reasonable forward price-to-earnings ratio in the mid-teens, proving that Wall Street's rally is backed by genuine productivity gains and massive data center revenues, not a dot-com-style bubble.
  • The Foreign Exflux: Foreign investors are bailing on Samsung Electronics—pushing foreign ownership down to about 46%—primarily due to perceived lower relative returns compared to U.S. tech and a strategy of selling into share buyback programs.
  • Underlying Fundamentals: Despite short-term supply-and-demand bottlenecks, Samsung's third-quarter operating profit projections remain robust at over 100 trillion won (about $74 billion), anchored by the unstoppable rise of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) chips.
  • Broader Market Strengths: Beyond semiconductors, South Korean export sectors like K-beauty cosmetics are capturing significant global market share (such as 26.5% in the U.S.), proving that targeted industrial strengths remain potent.

Sizing Up Samsung and the AI Semiconductor Ecosystem

The anxiety plaguing South Korean retail investor forums—asking why local stocks are languishing while global peers surge—ignores the structural shifts happening in semiconductors. As big tech companies like Google, Amazon, and Meta accelerate their own custom chip designs to reduce reliance on Nvidia, the hardware ecosystem is expanding. Major players like Broadcom and Marvell Technology are capturing immense demand, putting pressure on traditional suppliers to prove their moat.

For South Korea's crown jewel, Samsung Electronics, the challenge is defending its technological edge in HBM while weathering temporary losses in its foundry (contract manufacturing) division. While critics point to governance concerns and short-term earnings adjustments, analysts project strong long-term fundamentals. If Samsung expands shareholder returns as predicted, prospective dividend yields could hit 6% to 7%, providing a powerful psychological floor for investors anxious about foreign capital flight.

Navigating Volatility Under the Lee Jae-myung Administration

Market uncertainty in South Korea is compounded by macroeconomic headwinds and shifting political landscapes. Under the progressive administration of President Lee Jae-myung, policymakers are aiming to balance technological innovation with social equity—a transition that is forcing local conglomerates to mature their corporate governance and shareholder return policies.

In our view, investors checking their stock portfolios in a panic are usually reacting to over-leveraged positions rather than genuine economic reality. Just as K-beauty has proven its dominance abroad through specialized excellence, South Korea's semiconductor giants must evolve from mere components suppliers into irreplaceable, core partners in the global tech supply chain. Short-term foreign outflows will reverse once earnings cement the market's psychological defenses. For patient investors, the current dip is less of a structural collapse and more of a test of long-term conviction.
