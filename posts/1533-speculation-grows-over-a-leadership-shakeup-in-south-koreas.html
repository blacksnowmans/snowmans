Speculation grows over a leadership shakeup in South Korea’s ruling party

October 27, 2026

Rumors are swirling that the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's ruling party, is preparing to replace its interim leader, Kim Min-seok, following a high-profile meeting between President Lee Jae-myung and former National Assembly Speaker Woo Won-shik.

  • What happened: President Lee held an unscheduled meeting with Woo, which political observers interpret as a clear signal that the current DPK leadership has lost the President’s confidence.
  • The controversy: Leader Kim Min-seok is facing intense pressure over allegations of ties to the Shincheonji Church—a controversial religious group—and has been criticized for failing to deliver promised reforms.
  • The stakes: With party approval ratings stagnating, the DPK is under mounting pressure to abandon its current "management" style and pivot toward decisive political action to avoid further eroding the administration's momentum.

A leadership in crisis

The meeting with Woo Won-shik is being read by insiders as the beginning of the end for the Kim Min-seok era. While the current leadership was tasked with providing stability, it has instead become a source of internal division. President Lee appears to be signaling that the party needs a new direction to regain public trust and effectively advance his legislative agenda. Critics within the party argue that the leadership is hiding behind administrative rules rather than addressing the political crises at hand.

The core of the internal unrest stems from the unresolved allegations regarding Kim’s connections to the Shincheonji Church. DPK lawmakers have increasingly gone public with their frustrations, demanding that the party come clean with the facts. One senior lawmaker stated that the party can no longer simply sweep the issue under the rug, urging the leadership to release investigation results and hold those involved accountable. By refusing to do so, the leadership is being accused of prioritizing self-preservation over the party's health.

The cost of lost momentum

There is a growing sense of buyer's remorse among the party's base. Critics, including prominent political commentators, argue that the DPK squandered its early political capital by failing to pursue bold reforms—such as the impeachment of controversial judicial figures—out of fear of political backlash. Ironically, this cautious approach has only caused the party’s approval ratings to slide further, as voters perceive the hesitation as weakness or incompetence.

The administration’s communication style, once praised for its transparency, is now being viewed by the public as stubbornness. For the DPK, the message from the grassroots is clear: voters are tired of excuses and "show-boating" task forces that yield no tangible results. If the party fails to replace its current leadership or address the scandals plaguing its top officials, it risks losing the very mandate that brought President Lee to power. The question now is not whether a change will come, but how much more political capital the DPK is willing to burn before it happens.
