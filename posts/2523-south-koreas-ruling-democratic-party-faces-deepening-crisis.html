South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party Faces Deepening Crisis and Warning of a 'Lost Decade'

November 20, 2026

South Korea's governing Democratic Party is facing a severe political crisis as plummeting public support, an economic disconnect, and a lack of viable future leadership threaten to push the ruling bloc into a decade-long slump.

  • Deepening public alienation: More than half the public now expresses strong disapproval of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), driven by perceptions that the party has lost both its moral standing and governing competence.
  • Leadership vacuum: Analysts warn that the DPK lacks clear successor candidates for future presidential runs, contrasting sharply with a crowded field in the main opposition conservative People Power Party (PPP).
  • Economic disconnect: While global demand for memory chips from giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix inflates export metrics, ordinary citizens face high inflation, weak domestic spending, and stagnant household incomes.
  • Growing hostility to dissent: Discontent is compounding as the administration reacts defensively to criticism, relying on core loyalists and aggressive online pushback rather than broader dialogue.

An Illusory Economic Boom and Missing Succession

The core of the DPK’s troubles lies in a widening gap between official macroeconomic metrics and the daily reality of South Korean households. As economics professor Choi Bae-keun points out, recent spikes in semiconductor exports by tech leaders Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are a windfall from the U.S.-led artificial intelligence boom rather than the result of domestic policy innovation. With corporate profits failing to trickle down to small businesses and job seekers, the administration’s claims of economic success feel out of touch to an increasingly frustrated public.

Compounding this policy mismatch is a strategic vulnerability: the party's reliance on President Lee Jae-myung has left it without a visible bench of future leaders. While the opposition People Power Party is cultivating multiple potential contenders, the DPK risks repeating the mistakes of the mid-2000s, when internal division and a lack of fresh figures led to a crushing presidential defeat in 2007 and a prolonged period in the political wilderness.

Echoes of Past Self-Destruction

The current dynamics closely mirror the downfall of the Uri Party—a historic predecessor to the DPK—which won a massive parliamentary majority in 2004 only to collapse within four years due to internal squabbling and failure to deliver on bread-and-butter economic issues. Today, despite holding a dominant position in the National Assembly, the DPK is criticized for alienating its core base while trying to court conservative voters, leaving both sides dissatisfied.

Rather than addressing these structural warnings, the ruling party has increasingly closed ranks and suppressed internal dissent. If the administration continues to dismiss public frustration as mere political hostility, it risks turning a temporary mid-term slump into a decade of electoral losses across local, parliamentary, and presidential cycles.
