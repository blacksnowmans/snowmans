2026 Parliamentary Audit: A "Failing Grade" for Korean Oversight

October 28, 2026

The 2026 parliamentary audit has concluded with harsh criticism, as lawmakers and executive agencies clashed over critical public livelihood issues, from a collapsing pediatric emergency system to the mismanagement of the national pension fund.

  • Pediatric Crisis: A police raid on the Ajou University Hospital pediatric emergency room led to the mass resignation of seven professors, sparking rare bipartisan condemnation of "reckless" police power.
  • Abortion Pill Debate: The introduction of the abortion pill "Mifegyne" (Mifepristone) triggered intense partisan bickering, with the opposition accusing the ruling Democratic Party of pushing for approval under "presidential mandates" without adequate safety checks.
  • Pension Fund Controversy: The National Pension Service faced backlash over its refusal to disclose meeting minutes, with the agency chief’s dismissive attitude toward lawmakers causing a temporary suspension of proceedings.
  • Systemic Failure: The audit was widely labeled a failure, with some committees recording zero questions for agencies, leading to a "F-grade" performance review for the second consecutive year.

The Crisis in Pediatric Emergency Care

The most explosive moment of this year’s audit was the fallout from a police raid on Ajou University Hospital. While the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition) and the Democratic Party (the ruling liberal party) are usually locked in bitter rivalry, they stood united in criticizing the police. Lawmakers argued that the raid severely demoralized staff already working in a fragile system. Despite calls for an official apology, police representatives offered only vague, "regretful" responses, further angering the committee members.

The incident was not merely procedural; it had real-world consequences. Seven professors at the hospital—a key hub for pediatric emergency care in the southern Gyeonggi region—offered their resignations in protest. While the head of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), Jeong Eun-kyeong, promised to coordinate with police to prevent a recurrence, the damage to the medical workforce remains a major blow to pediatric care accessibility.

Stagnation and Political Theater

Beyond the medical crisis, the audit showcased a troubling trend of political maneuvering over substantive policy. The debate over the abortion pill Mifegyne devolved into a struggle for legislative dominance, with the conservative opposition framing the government’s push for expedited approval as an authoritarian "presidential mandate" by President Lee Jae-myung. This framing obscures the underlying health policy necessity of providing safe, legal alternatives to the black market.

Similarly, the National Pension Service (NPS)—which manages a massive fund exceeding $700 billion (1,000 trillion won)—suffered from a lack of transparency. The refusal to release meeting minutes and the high-handed attitude of the NPS leadership toward auditors highlights a growing disconnect between the agency and the public it serves. When lawmakers prioritize political point-scoring over the actual oversight of national assets, the audit ceases to be a tool for governance and becomes a performance for voters.

The "zero questions" debacle in the Agriculture, Food, Rural Affairs, Oceans and Fisheries Committee is the final indictment of this year’s session. With agencies standing by while lawmakers ignored their own oversight duties, the 2026 audit failed to address the very issues it was meant to resolve. Until the National Assembly shifts its focus from political theater to rigorous, expert-led policy scrutiny, the public will remain the primary victim of these institutional shortcomings.
