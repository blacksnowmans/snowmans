AI "Slop" vs. Original IP: The New Survival Logic for South Korean Content Creators

November 01, 2026

As global platforms like TikTok and YouTube aggressively tighten their monetization filters, the era of "passive income" via automated AI video production is ending for South Korean creators.

  • Algorithmic Gatekeeping: TikTok’s "Creator Rewards Program" is now systematically rejecting AI-generated content for "Lack of Originality," even when videos reach millions of views and meet the 10,000-follower threshold.
  • YouTube’s Rising Bar: Starting in 2027, YouTube will double the requirement for Shorts monetization from 10 million to 20 million views over 90 days, while increasing long-form requirements to 8,000 watch hours.
  • The "AI Slop" Purge: Platforms are intentionally raising barriers to flush out low-quality, automated content that drives up server costs and alienates high-value advertisers.
  • Legal Vulnerability: South Korean creators have faced unilateral account freezes and fund seizures by platforms, often requiring expensive legal intervention to recover earnings from automated moderation systems.

The Death of the "AI Copycat"

The dream of earning "10 million won a month" (about $7,200) while "lying down" is hitting a harsh reality. South Korean creators who once thrived by using AI to upscale and slightly modify viral foreign content are finding themselves blocked by cold, mechanical rejections. TikTok’s moderation doesn't care about the hours spent "polishing" a prompt or rendering high-quality textures; if the core idea is a derivative of an existing viral hit, the algorithm labels it as "recycled." This has led to a "hellish" cycle for many local creators who see their application dates for monetization pushed back indefinitely without explanation.

This shift is mirrored in the current regulatory climate under the Lee Jae-myung administration. The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and the Korea Copyright Commission have been tightening guidelines on generative AI, moving toward a framework where simple prompt-based outputs without significant human creative intervention are denied legal protection. As the government prepares to implement the "AI Basic Law" by 2026, the focus has shifted from mere technology adoption to protecting the integrity of the domestic "Original IP" (Intellectual Property) ecosystem.

Survival via World-Building

To survive, Korean creators are being forced to pivot from "copying" to "storytelling." The creator featured in the source material only achieved monetization after a four-month struggle, during which they stopped chasing raw dopamine-inducing clips and began building a cohesive narrative universe. By evolving a simple visual trope—like "spiky armor"—into a cinematic world involving deep-sea exploration and mechanical "spring-leg" characters, they created a brand that the algorithm finally recognized as original. This reflects a broader market trend: as the revenue per 1,000 views (RPM) for short-form content remains as low as 40 to 100 won (about $0.03 to $0.07), only those with a dedicated, brand-loyal audience can find long-term stability.

Ultimately, the "gentle monster" approach is becoming the standard. Just as the famous South Korean eyewear brand Gentle Monster succeeded by wrapping its products in avant-garde art and storytelling, AI creators must now wrap their technology in unique world-building. For the busy professional looking for a "side hustle," the message is clear: if your content looks like someone else’s, the platforms—and the increasingly strict regulatory environment under the current government—will treat you as a "counterfeit designer" rather than a creator, and you will not be paid.
