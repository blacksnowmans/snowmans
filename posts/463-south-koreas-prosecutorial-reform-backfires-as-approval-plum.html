South Korea’s Prosecutorial Reform Backfires as Approval Plummets

September 08, 2026

South Korea’s progressive government is facing a severe political backlash as critics accuse it of preserving old prosecutorial powers under the guise of reform, driving public approval ratings down to 37.4% against a negative evaluation of 59.5% in September 2026.

  • Faux Reform on Prosecution: The Ministry of Justice's new prosecutorial agency reorganization maintains a total staff of 8,412—including 2,292 prosecutors—despite pledges to abolish direct investigative powers.
  • Controversial New Units: The plan creates a "Judicial Control Division" to review police non-indictment cases and rebrands the forensic department into a planning office backed by a massive $43 million (about 57 billion KRW) forensics center budget.
  • Political Fallout: President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is losing critical support from young male voters over perceived hypocrisies in cabinet appointments and political cronyism.

Old Habits Die Hard Under New Management

When a reform-minded administration takes power, the expectation is a fundamental overhaul of corrupt structures. Yet, the newly proposed organization of South Korea's prosecutorial bodies looks suspiciously like a rebranding exercise. By keeping headcount virtually untouched and establishing new oversight divisions—such as the Judicial Control Division, which critics label an arrogant attempt to treat police departments as subordinates—the administration risks repeating the heavy-handed governance styles it once vowed to dismantle.

The refusal to fully relinquish prosecutorial leverage, compounded by multi-billion-won investments into forensic infrastructure, suggests that the institutional machinery of state power remains intact, just handed to a different set of hands. Observers argue that bypassing legislative votes through presidential decrees to push these changes mimics the authoritarian tactics of prior conservative administrations, alienating the public on matters of basic democratic procedure.

Cronyism and the Threat of Disillusioned Youth

Compounding these policy missteps is a severe crisis of political cronyism, often driven by opportunistic insiders trading on the president's name. Controversies surrounding key appointments and close aides have enraged younger demographics, particularly men in their twenties and thirties who view the system as increasingly rigged against fairness and merit. When soaring living costs and hyper-competitive career ladders leave youth struggling, elite maneuvering and political patronage sting all the more.

Ultimately, a government built on the promise of reform cannot afford to coast on the weakness of its political opposition. If the administration continues to ignore the warning signs in the polls and prioritizes the preservation of state power over democratic accountability, it risks squandering its mandate entirely.
