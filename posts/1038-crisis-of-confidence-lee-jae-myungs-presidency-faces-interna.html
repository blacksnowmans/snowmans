Crisis of Confidence: Lee Jae-myung's Presidency Faces Internal and External Pressures

October 12, 2026

  • Falling Approval: President Lee Jae-myung’s Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the ruling liberal party, saw its approval rating drop 7 percentage points to 35.2% over the last two weeks.
  • SNS Controversy: Critics argue the President’s use of social media has shifted from "direct communication" to inflammatory, vague attacks that deepen internal divisions.
  • Judicial Tension: Allegations that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is seeking to restart criminal trials against the President—potentially violating constitutional protections—have sparked debates over impeachment.
  • Leadership Vacuum: Critics within and outside the party suggest that the DPK leadership is failing to address public disillusionment, instead relying on internal echo chambers.

The Perils of "SNS Governance"

President Lee Jae-myung’s habit of using social media to target political rivals—often using cryptic metaphors like "the pampered elite"—has become a lightning rod for criticism. While his supporters view this as bypassing traditional media filters to reach the public directly, detractors see it as "emotional venting" that lacks the gravity required of a head of state. The core issue is a perceived lack of effective guidance from his staff. When a President uses social media to engage in personal political disputes, it suggests a breakdown in the advisory structure. Rather than acting as a buffer or a source of clear, policy-focused messaging, the President’s aides are accused of allowing him to operate in isolation. This perceived "shadow organization" influence and the absence of institutional restraint are fueling concerns that the administration is prioritizing petty factional squabbles over stable governance.

A Fractured Party and a Politicized Judiciary

The DPK’s recent struggles are exacerbated by internal divisions stemming from its last national convention. The labeling of dissenters as "anti-Lee" or "divisionists" has created a toxic environment, driving voters toward alternatives like the Rebuilding Korea Party or causing them to disengage from politics entirely. The leadership’s silence on these issues, coupled with their failure to offer a path for "renewal," has left many supporters feeling alienated. Simultaneously, the judiciary has become a flashpoint for constitutional crisis. Legal experts are fiercely debating Article 84 of the Constitution, which grants the President immunity from criminal prosecution while in office. Critics fear that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is maneuvering to resume trials against the President as a political power play to influence judicial appointments. If the judiciary is seen as violating the Constitution to undermine the President’s mandate, it risks a backlash that could trigger talk of judicial impeachment. For the DPK, the path forward is narrow. To regain its footing, the party must move beyond the current culture of internal purging and return to the basics of "livelihood politics." Without a clear, unified strategy that prioritizes the public interest over internal factional dominance, the President’s legislative and political agenda will remain trapped in a cycle of grievance and deepening isolation.
