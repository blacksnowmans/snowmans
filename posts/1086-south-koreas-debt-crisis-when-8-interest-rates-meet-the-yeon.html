South Korea’s Debt Crisis: When 8% Interest Rates Meet the "Yeong-kkeul" Generation

October 13, 2026

South Korean households are facing a brutal reality as interest rates on "minus accounts" (revolving credit lines) have surged to 8%, turning the country’s high-leverage property bubble into a systemic threat.

  • The Interest Rate Shock: Loans that carried 4% interest just a few years ago have doubled, pushing those who utilized maximum leverage to purchase homes—the so-called "yeong-kkeul" (literally "scraping together every penny") generation—to the brink of insolvency.
  • The Macroeconomic Trap: The Bank of Korea is caught between a rock and a hard place: raising rates to combat inflation and capital flight threatens to collapse household balance sheets, while lowering them could trigger currency devaluation.
  • The Real Estate Fallout: Property auctions are surging, with many properties linked to "jeonse" (a unique Korean deposit-based rental system) fraud cases, where declining market values have left tenants unable to recover their life savings.

The End of the "Scraping" Era

The term "yeong-kkeul" defined the reckless optimism of 2020-2021, when social pressure dictated that taking on massive debt for real estate was the only way to avoid poverty. Today, that debt has become a boomerang. With property prices stalling or declining in non-core areas, households are finding that their assets are no longer sufficient to cover their liabilities. This is not just a personal financial failure; it is a structural crisis in a country where approximately 70% of household assets are tied up in real estate.

The situation is compounded by external shocks. As the Middle East conflict pushes up global oil prices, South Korea—which imports over 94% of its energy—is facing persistent inflation. The economic pressure is so acute that households are beginning to cut back on private education spending, a "final line of defense" for Korean families, suggesting that the limit of household endurance has been reached.

Policy Challenges and the "Debt Wall"

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the government faces the daunting task of managing this fallout without triggering a broader collapse. The previous administration, led by the now-imprisoned Yoon Seok-youl, has been widely criticized for policies that encouraged this massive accumulation of household debt. The current reality is a stark reminder that when investment strategies are based on the assumption that asset prices will rise indefinitely, the result is not wealth creation—it is a debt trap.

The market is currently showing a dangerous bifurcation. While prime locations in Seoul may hold their value, the suburban and regional markets are rapidly deteriorating. As auction listings climb, the focus must shift from reckless growth to basic financial hygiene: cash flow management and debt reduction. For many, the dream of "one-shot" wealth has turned into a desperate fight for survival, proving that the most powerful economic defense is not leverage, but the ability to live within one's means.
