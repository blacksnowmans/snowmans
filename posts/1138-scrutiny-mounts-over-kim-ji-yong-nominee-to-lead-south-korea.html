Scrutiny Mounts Over Kim Ji-yong, Nominee to Lead South Korea’s New Major Crimes Agency

October 14, 2026

South Korea’s confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong, the inaugural nominee to lead the upcoming Major Crimes Investigation Bureau, has ignited fierce pushback over his past role in contentious prosecutions and his lack of reform credentials.

  • The confirmation battle: Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party grilled nominee Kim Ji-yong over whether a career prosecutor who complied with institutional overreach is fit to lead the new investigative agency.
  • The core controversy: Kim served as head of the criminal department at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office in 2021 when prosecutors aggressively indicted Lee Kwang-chul, a former presidential secretary, over an emergency travel ban on former vice justice minister Kim Hak-ui—a case that ended in a full acquittal by the Supreme Court after five years.
  • Institutional hypocrisy exposed: Hearing records revealed that top prosecution brass in 2019 had internally coordinated and approved the emergency travel ban to prevent Kim Hak-ui from fleeing abroad, only to later rebrand the action as illegal to prosecute working-level officials while shielding superiors.
  • What is at stake: The new Major Crimes Investigation Bureau—set to launch alongside an indictment-only office to complete the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers—risks a crisis of legitimacy before opening its doors if led by a passive bureaucratic insider.

A Test of Accountability for Prosecutorial Reform

The creation of the Major Crimes Investigation Bureau was promised as the definitive end to South Korea’s legacy of prosecutorial overreach, particularly following the political turmoil of the Yoon Suk-yeol era. Citizens demanded a clean break from an era where prosecutors monopolized both investigation and indictment to target political opponents and shield their own. Appointing a director who personified that quiet compliance undermines the very mandate of the agency.

During questioning by Democratic Party lawmakers, including Jung Chung-rae and Yoon Kun-young, Kim claimed he privately opposed the flawed 2021 indictment of Lee Kwang-chul. Yet he produced no written dissenting opinion, admitting only that he found the outcome regrettable and offering a personal apology. For victims subjected to half a decade of manufactured legal jeopardy, an undocumented, post-hoc expression of regret falls far short of administrative accountability.

Technocrats Cannot Lead Radical Structural Change

Kim’s defense relied on the classic stance of a bureaucratic technocrat: claiming he merely performed his duties while working within the chain of command. But the history of abusive prosecutions in South Korea was sustained not just by active conspirators, but by senior officials who remained silent. Handing the reins of a newly decoupled investigative apparatus to someone who never demonstrated institutional resistance sends the wrong signal at the wrong time.

If the new agency is to establish public trust, its leadership must represent an uncompromising departure from past prosecutorial habits. Installing a career insider who accommodated questionable indictments risks recreating the old culture under a new agency banner.
