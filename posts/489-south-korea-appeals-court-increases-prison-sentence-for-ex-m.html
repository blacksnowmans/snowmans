South Korea Appeals Court Increases Prison Sentence for Ex-Minister Over Martial Law Order

September 09, 2026

  • An appeals court in Seoul sentenced Lee Sang-min, South Korea's former Minister of the Interior and Safety, to nine years in prison for his role in last winter's unlawful martial law attempt.
  • The ruling overturns a lower court's seven-year sentence, which prosecutors had argued was far too lenient given the severity of the charges.
  • The court cited the former minister's defiant behavior, attempts to cover up the crime, and his order to cut off utilities to a critical news outlet as key reasons for the harsher penalty.
  • The decision marks another major legal milestone as South Korea's judiciary deals with the fallout from the abortive martial law decree issued by former President Yoon Suk Yeol.

When an appeals court tacks an extra two years onto a high-profile political prison sentence, it usually means the judiciary has lost patience with the defendant's excuses. That is precisely what happened when South Korea's high court reviewed the case of Lee Sang-min, the former head of the Ministry of the Interior and Safety—the agency handling domestic security and disaster management. The original seven-year sentence handed down by the lower court had drawn widespread criticism for being a slap on the wrist. By bumping the term up to nine years, the appeals panel sent an unmistakable signal: occupying the heights of state power is no shield when you help subvert the constitutional order.

The core of Lee's conviction rests on his compliance with illegal directives during the chaotic hours of the emergency decree. As one of only two cabinet members legally empowered to advise the president on martial law, Lee instead acted as an enforcer of unlawful orders, instructing the fire agency to cut power and water to an independent media organization. The court rightly dismissed the defense that no actual disruption occurred, pointing out that disaster officials dodged the order and parliament acted swiftly to block the decree—not because Lee had a change of heart. His refusal to show remorse throughout the trial, combined with efforts to obfuscate the truth, sealed his fate.

Democracies do not fall overnight; they erode when officials entrusted with upholding the law choose obedience to a rogue executive over their constitutional oaths. By dragging emergency-response workers into a political conspiracy and attempting to muzzle the press, Lee treated public infrastructure as a tool for political suppression. This ruling does not undo the shock of that winter night, but it reinforces a vital democratic baseline: no political title is grand enough to place anyone above the law.
