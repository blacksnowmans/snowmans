Selective Justice in South Korea: Amazon Royalties, Tragic Deaths, and the Blind Spots of the Law

October 12, 2026

South Korea’s legal and investigative systems are facing sharp criticism for an apparent double standard, treating political figures with leniency while failing ordinary citizens in cases ranging from questionable publishing profits to industrial fatalities.

  • The Book Royalties Controversy: Police dismissed allegations of commercial intent in an Amazon self-publishing case involving former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon, claiming the book was sold for just $0.99 with no profit margin. Lawmakers later pointed out that standard Amazon Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) options yield a 35% royalty—a detail easily found via basic online searches.
  • The Labor Tragedy: In February 2025, an elevator safety inspector named Kim Kyung-tae fell to his death from the 24th floor while on the job. Police ultimately cleared two supervisors of professional negligence resulting in death without thoroughly investigating.
  • Administrative Blunders: In the elevator fatality case, police mailed the formal non-prosecution notice to the deceased victim's home address rather than his grieving family, leaving relatives unaware of the decision until it surfaced during a parliamentary audit.
  • Wider Public Fallout: Critics argue these incidents highlight a persistent bias where authorities show intense scrutiny toward political opponents—such as past aggressive raids targeting figures associated with the liberal opposition—while shielding conservative figures and ignoring basic accountability for working-class deaths.

When Investigators Miss What Interns Find in Thirty Minutes

The controversy surrounding the Amazon textbook sales highlights a recurring complaint among critics of South Korea's police and prosecutors: when powerful political figures are involved, investigative rigor tends to vanish. During a parliamentary audit, opposition lawmaker Yang Bu-nam questioned the police's handling of the case. Investigators had shrugged off accusations of commercial gain by pointing to the nominal $0.99 price tag. Yet, a basic look at Amazon’s KDP guidelines reveals a standard 35% royalty structure. The fact that a lawmaker's junior aide uncovered this within half an hour while professional investigators missed it has fueled accusations of willful blindness.

This leniency stands in stark contrast to the aggressive tactics historically deployed against political rivals, such as the exhaustive, multi-site searches directed at the family of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Observers note that when the justice system applies strict rules to ordinary citizens while offering polite excuses for the powerful, public trust in the rule of law evaporates. The police's defense—claiming international cooperation hurdles and overlooking basic e-commerce arithmetic—suggests an institution more interested in protecting political allies than uncovering facts.

Administrative Indifference and the Cost of Worker Safety

Away from the political headlines, the systemic neglect of ordinary workers reveals an even darker side of South Korean bureaucracy. The death of Kim Kyung-tae, an elevator safety inspector who plunged 24 floors to his death, has become a symbol of administrative callousness. Not only did police clear the site supervisors without holding anyone accountable, but they also mailed the official decision papers to the dead man's address, as if expecting him to read them. The victim's family learned of the case's closure only by chance during a national audit.

This procedural absurdity points to a broader institutional failure. When law enforcement treats human lives with bureaucratic indifference—mishandling notifications and rushing to drop safety investigations—it inflicts secondary trauma on grieving families. South Korea's investigative agencies face mounting pressure to reform not just their oversight of industrial safety, but the fundamental empathy and competence of their administrative processes. Without an overhaul, the law risks remaining a shield for the strong and a sharp blade only for the weak.
