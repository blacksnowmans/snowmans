Why Global Inequality Isn't About Intelligence

September 21, 2026

The vast disparities in human development—why some societies conquered the globe while others struggled with basic survival—are not the result of inherent racial or cultural superiority, but rather a profound roll of the environmental dice.

  • Environmental Determinism: The primary driver of civilization was not genius, but access to wild plants and animals that could be domesticated.
  • The "Big Five" Factor: Of the 148 large wild herbivore species on Earth, only 14 could be domesticated; most were native to Eurasia, giving its inhabitants a massive head start.
  • Biological Warfare: Long-term cohabitation with livestock gifted Eurasians with resistance to diseases like smallpox and measles, which later decimated indigenous populations elsewhere.
  • The Kahamarca Turning Point: The 1532 massacre of 80,000 Inca soldiers by 168 Spaniards demonstrated how steel, horses, and written records—all products of environmental luck—shattered an empire in hours.

The Geography of Power

In his landmark study Guns, Germs, and Steel, biologist Jared Diamond argues that technological innovation requires "surplus food." Hunter-gatherers had no time to develop tools because they spent their entire lives searching for calories. Societies in the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East, however, stumbled upon easily farmable crops like wheat and barley, allowing for specialists—smiths, scribes, and soldiers—to emerge. This wasn't a choice; it was a consequence of the local flora.

The Americas and sub-Saharan Africa suffered from a lack of "domesticable" candidates. While Eurasians utilized horses for transport and oxen to pull plows, other regions lacked the animal "engines" necessary for industrial development. By the time Europeans arrived in the New World, they carried not just weapons, but invisible biological weapons. Smallpox and other pathogens, which had already culled the weak in Eurasia over centuries, effectively acted as a vanguard for conquest, wiping out up to 95% of indigenous populations.

Humility in History

Understanding these historical forces is essential for viewing modern inequality through a lens of empathy rather than superiority. The historical advantage of Eurasia was built on environmental conditions that existed 13,000 years ago, not on the inherent merit of its people.

As we navigate a modern world defined by institutional and technological gaps, remembering these origins is crucial. The history of humanity is not a record of the smartest winning, but a narrative of geographical distribution. Acknowledging this prevents us from falling into the trap of blaming the marginalized for their circumstances, serving as a reminder that our current position in the global order is as much about luck as it is about effort.
