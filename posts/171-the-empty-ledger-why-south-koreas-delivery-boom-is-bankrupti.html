The Empty Ledger: Why South Korea’s Delivery Boom is Bankrupting Small Businesses

September 05, 2026

For many small business owners in South Korea, the rise of delivery platforms has become a trap where high sales volume masks a reality of razor-thin profits or outright losses.

  • The Hidden Drain: Beyond nominal commission fees, costs like transaction fees, delivery subsidies, and mandatory discount coupons often consume over 30% of gross revenue.
  • Platform Dependence: South Korean shopkeepers feel "held hostage" by major apps like Baedal Minjok (the country’s leading food delivery app) and Coupang Eats, fearing that opting out would result in a catastrophic drop in daily orders.
  • The Profit Paradox: Many restaurants, particularly those with low per-order price points, find that higher sales volume only increases their operational labor while bleeding out their net margins.

The Illusion of Success

In the current South Korean landscape, an eatery without an app presence is invisible. However, owners are increasingly realizing that these platforms are not partners, but intermediaries that extract value at every stage. When a customer orders a 15,000 won (about $11) meal, the merchant doesn't just lose the cost of ingredients; they lose a significant percentage to the platform’s complex fee structure. For low-margin businesses like gukbap (pork soup) or bunsik (snack) shops, the math often ends up equating to less than the national minimum wage for the owner’s labor.

Furthermore, platforms often control the relationship with the customer. Because the app acts as the gatekeeper, the restaurant owner rarely gains access to customer data or the ability to build a direct, loyal relationship. This turns the merchant into a mere "subcontractor" for the platform, stripping away the autonomy that defines independent entrepreneurship.

Shifting the Strategy

The "share-growth" policies proposed by delivery giants—offering tiered commission rates—are often dismissed by merchants as superficial window dressing. While the headline rate may fluctuate, the total cost of operating on these platforms remains prohibitively high. The only viable path forward for many owners is to treat delivery apps not as their primary business model, but as one of many marketing channels.

Successful owners are now pivoting to incentivize direct sales. By encouraging customers to order via telephone for pickup, promoting their own websites, or offering in-store-only benefits, merchants are trying to reclaim their customer base. The goal is to move from 100% platform reliance to a balanced approach where the shop owner, not the app, controls the connection to the consumer. In a market where the platform takes a significant cut of every transaction, the only path to survival is to stop chasing gross revenue and start prioritizing the net profit that stays in the drawer.
