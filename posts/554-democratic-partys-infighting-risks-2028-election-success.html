Democratic Party’s Infighting Risks 2028 Election Success

October 01, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation's current ruling party, is facing intense internal conflict that critics warn could jeopardize its success in the 2028 legislative elections—a critical referendum on the administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

  • Internal Fractures: Deep-seated tensions between the "pro-Lee" (loyalists to President Lee) and "pro-Moon" (allies of former President Moon Jae-in) factions are alienating the party's core support base.
  • Strategic Blunders: Leadership decisions, such as refusing election coalitions in key districts like Pyeongtaek and failing to address financial scandals, have damaged the party's public image.
  • Rising Competition: The Rebuilding Korea Party (led by Cho Kuk) is gaining traction among disillusioned DPK members who feel the ruling party has lost its reformist edge.

The Crisis of Governance

The 2028 legislative elections are widely viewed as a pivotal moment for the Lee Jae-myung administration. However, rather than consolidating power, the DPK leadership is accused of engaging in "self-harming" behavior. Observers point to recent controversies, including a suspicious gathering of 100 party members advocating for the withdrawal of specific criminal charges—a move that many see as lacking transparency—and the party’s silence regarding the "leveraged ETF" scandal, which saw many young and middle-class investors lose significant capital.

The party’s refusal to form electoral coalitions in districts like Pyeongtaek is being cited as a primary example of exclusionary politics. By rejecting unity with other progressive forces, the DPK suffered significant electoral losses. Critics argue that this narrow-minded approach, coupled with the propagation of baseless rumors regarding "secret deals" between party leaders, is stripping the DPK of its ability to expand toward moderate voters.

The Role of the Rebuilding Korea Party

Amidst the DPK’s turmoil, the Rebuilding Korea Party is positioning itself as a "catfish"—an industry term for a competitor that keeps the main players active and alert. While some DPK loyalists label this as betrayal, many rank-and-file members are increasingly looking toward the Rebuilding Korea Party to provide the vocal, aggressive oversight that they feel the ruling party has abandoned in its attempt to play it safe.

The reality is that if the DPK continues to alienate its supporters and ignore internal criticism, it faces a total collapse in 2028. True reform requires the party to move beyond blind loyalty and address its structural issues. As the opposition grows more organized, the DPK’s failure to act as a cohesive, self-reflective ruling party risks not only the 2028 elections but the long-term viability of the Lee Jae-myung administration itself.
