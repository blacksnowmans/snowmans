Supreme Court Faces Backlash Over Sudden Sentencing in Han Duck-soo Case

November 17, 2026

South Korea’s Supreme Court is facing intense scrutiny following a sudden decision to deliver a final verdict in the case involving Han Duck-soo just one day before his legal detention period is set to expire.

  • Sudden Schedule Shift: The court abruptly changed the case status from "continuing deliberations" to a final sentencing date of November 19, sparking concerns over procedural irregularities.
  • Political Friction: Lawmakers from the Democratic Party (South Korea’s ruling party) have challenged the judiciary, suggesting the timeline was manipulated to preempt public resistance or political fallout.
  • Judicial Defense: Roh Kyung-pil, Minister of the Court Administration Office (the agency responsible for the judiciary's administrative affairs), maintains that the date was set only after the written judgment was finalized.
  • Ethics Allegations: Allegations of a private meeting between Chief Justice Cho Hee-de and the defendant have surfaced, with critics noting the court has failed to provide a definitive denial.

Procedural Inconsistency and Public Trust

The controversy centers on the jarring transition in the court’s calendar. Until recently, the Supreme Court had indicated that it would continue hearings to deliberate the legal complexities of the Han Duck-soo case. The sudden pivot to a final verdict—scheduled for the very eve of Han’s release from detention—has led observers to question whether the conclusion was reached prematurely. In a heated session of the Legislation and Judiciary Committee (the parliamentary body that oversees the legal system), Representative Park Kyun-taek of the ruling Democratic Party argued that the sequence of events defies common legal experience. He suggested that such a rapid turnaround implies the verdict may have been determined before the final deliberations were even concluded.

The court’s administrative leadership has struggled to offer a narrative that satisfies both the opposition and legal pundits. While Minister Roh Kyung-pil argued that the court was simply working against the clock of a looming detention deadline, the lack of transparency regarding the internal "consensus-building" process has fueled suspicion. For a judiciary already operating in a highly polarized environment under the Lee Jae-myung administration, any appearance of "rushing" a high-profile verdict to manage the defendant's physical custody status risks damaging the institution's perceived impartiality.

Allegations of Private Influence

Adding to the tension are reports from influential media outlets, including Kim Ou-joon’s News Factory, regarding a potential private meeting between Chief Justice Cho Hee-de and Han Duck-soo. During parliamentary questioning, the judiciary's inability to "confidently deny" such a meeting took place has become a focal point for critics. In the context of South Korean judicial ethics, even the suggestion of an ex parte communication between the head of the Supreme Court and a defendant in a pending case is enough to trigger a crisis of confidence.

Ultimately, the November 19 verdict will be judged not only on its legal merits but on the perceived fairness of the path taken to get there. If the Supreme Court fails to address the "procedural void" between its deliberation phase and the final sentencing, the ruling may be viewed as a political calculation rather than a judicial necessity. With the ruling party watching closely, the court's next moves will determine whether it can maintain its authority as an independent arbiter or if it will be seen as bending to the pressures of the clock and the current political climate.
