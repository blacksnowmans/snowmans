South Korea’s Fiscal War Begins as President Lee Unveils Record $615 Billion Budget

November 02, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung will address the National Assembly to present a record 821 trillion won (about $615 billion) budget for next year, igniting a fierce clash with opposition conservatives over fiscal spending, judicial oversight, and political reform.

  • What happened: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is pushing a massive budget featuring a 162 trillion won ($120 billion) "Future Response Fund" for artificial intelligence and green tech, while the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) vows to cut 30 trillion won ($22.5 billion).
  • What it changes: The fiscal battle is expanding into a multi-front conflict, pulling in parliamentary probes against Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and special prosecutor bills regarding alleged judicial misconduct.
  • Who is affected: Local administrations facing severe revenue shortfalls, tech sectors awaiting state investments, and conservative opposition leaders struggling with internal factional rivalries.

A Battle Between Future Growth and Fiscal Restraint

President Lee’s proposed budget represents an aggressive push to reboot South Korea’s economy amidst high inflation and sluggish growth. By allocating massive capital toward strategic industries like AI, quantum technology, and carbon neutrality, the administration argues that state-led spending is essential to secure South Korea's global competitiveness. To support fiscal soundness elsewhere, the government is loosening administrative rules to speed up the sale of non-core assets held by state-owned enterprises. However, the PPP, led by party chief Jang Dong-hyuk, has labeled the budget an unfunded "blank check" that burdens future generations with debt, pointing to public sector debt that has surpassed 1,000 trillion won ($750 billion).

Judicial Warfare and Opposition Fractures

Beyond the balance sheet, the legislative session is escalating into an all-out institutional feud. The DPK is pushing forward with a parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae alongside a special counsel bill addressing alleged target-indictments by prosecutors. Relations between parliament and the bench have deteriorated further after a sitting judge publicly compared legislative actions to Orwell's "Animal Farm," drawing sharp condemnation from Cho Kuk, head of the minority Rebuilding Korea Party.

Meanwhile, the conservative opposition is struggling with deep internal division. PPP chief Jang Dong-hyuk has drawn a strict line against letting former leader Han Dong-hoon rejoin the party, favoring a solo leadership strategy despite recent encouragement from former leader Park Geun-hye to unify conservative factions. At the same time, lingering claims from conservative lawmakers regarding early-voting discrepancies risk entrenching the party in procedural grievances rather than positioning it as a credible check on President Lee's fiscal ambitions.
