South Korea Moves to Fine Giant Platforms 1% of Revenue for Obstructing Probes

October 26, 2026

South Korea’s antitrust regulator is proposing a massive fine of up to 1% of total annual revenue for major platforms like Coupang that refuse or obstruct on-site investigations.

  • Joo Byung-ki, chairperson of the Fair Trade Commission (FTC), announced the plan to replace ineffective flat-rate fines with revenue-based penalties to ensure "heavyweight" firms comply with government probes.
  • The move targets a long-standing practice where tech giants delay data submission or block investigators, treating current small fixed fines—often only a few hundred thousand dollars—as a minor cost of doing business.
  • Shinsegae Group, one of South Korea's largest retail conglomerates, launched its "2024 SSG Day," using integrated online-offline promotions and exclusive iPhone deals to spark year-end consumption.
  • Local producers are finding success in niche markets, with the Jeongseon Blueberry Cooperative selling out its entire stock through live commerce broadcasts supported by local government digital growth programs.
  • Global trends indicate a rising crisis for small vendors as platform fees and marketing costs reach up to 40% of total sales, reinforcing the FTC’s argument for stricter oversight of market-dominant players.

Cracking Down on the "Heavyweights"

The Lee Jae-myung administration is signaling a definitive end to the era of corporate leniency. By proposing a fine linked to total revenue rather than a fixed amount, the FTC is arming itself with a "heavyweight" weapon to match the scale of companies like Coupang, the nation's leading e-commerce platform. For a company generating tens of billions of dollars in annual sales, a 1% fine could reach hundreds of millions of dollars (several thousand billion won), making it a genuine deterrent against obstructing justice.

This policy shift marks a sharp departure from the "self-regulation" approach favored during Yoon Suk-yeol 씨’s tenure. Critics of that period argue that the lack of oversight allowed dominant platforms to squeeze small businesses through unfair pricing and the promotion of private-label brands. The current government’s stance is that market innovation must not come at the cost of legal accountability or the survival of small-scale sellers who are often left with no bargaining power against "big tech" gatekeepers.

The Squeeze on Small Sellers

While the retail landscape is buzzing with high-profile events like Shinsegae’s SSG Day, the underlying reality for many vendors is grim. Reports of platform fees and commissions reaching 40% of revenue highlight a predatory ecosystem where sellers can move high volumes of goods but still fail to turn a profit. This "tilted playing field" is exactly what the FTC aims to level by ensuring that dominant platforms cannot hide their internal data or algorithmic preferences from regulators.

However, there is a glimmer of hope in the "local commerce" model. Success stories from regional cooperatives, such as the blueberry farmers in Jeongseon, show that when local governments provide the infrastructure for live streaming and direct-to-consumer sales, small producers can bypass expensive intermediaries. The challenge for the government now is to ensure that these small-scale successes aren't eventually swallowed by the same monopolistic practices they are currently trying to regulate.

The Bottom Line

The FTC's move is more than just a regulatory update; it is a declaration of sovereignty over digital markets. If the government succeeds in implementing revenue-based fines, it will fundamentally change the risk calculation for global tech giants operating in Korea. Innovation must be paired with responsibility, or the very ecosystem that platforms built will collapse under the weight of their own rent-seeking behavior.
