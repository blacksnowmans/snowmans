Why Samsung Electronics’ Record Quarter Sent Its Stock Tumbling

October 11, 2026

  • Record profit, falling stock: Samsung Electronics posted a record 107.4 trillion won (about $78 billion) in third-quarter operating profit—the first time a global tech giant crossed the 100 trillion won mark—yet its shares fell on the announcement.
  • Wall Street vs. wild expectations: The sell-off was driven by lofty market expectations that outpaced the record numbers, alongside external pressures like U.S. Treasury yields and an awkward trading calendar crunch.
  • Cooling semiconductor cycle: Analysts warn of slowing growth into 2026, driven by a sharp deceleration in standard DRAM price hikes from 60% down to 10–15%.
  • Capital shifts to KOSDAQ: Frustrated retail investors are abandoning heavyweights like Samsung in favor of South Korea’s junior stock market, the KOSDAQ, sparking a rotation into cosmetics, security stocks, and smaller semiconductor supply-chain plays.

South Korea's stock market is serving up a harsh dose of reality. When Samsung Electronics—the undisputed anchor of the country's stock exchange—reports the highest quarterly operating profit ever recorded by a global tech company, basic logic says the stock should rally. Instead, shares slid on the day of the announcement, exposing the brutal mechanics of modern capital markets where missing unspoken, bloated expectations is treated as a sin.

The blame goes beyond unmet hype. A potent mix of rising U.S. Treasury yields, mechanical ETF rebalancing that collided with local holiday closures, and looming headwinds for general DRAM chips have left retail investors reeling. With profit margins on standard memory chips cooling from a blistering 60% down to a projected 10–15%, the market is waking up to the reality that the current semiconductor cycle may be peaking.

Smart money is already voting with its feet. As heavyweight tech giants stagnate, capital is rotating rapidly into South Korea’s secondary market, the KOSDAQ. While major blue chips drag, smaller-cap sectors—including cosmetics, telecommunications, security stocks, and lesser-known semiconductor parts-and-equipment exchange-traded funds (ETFs), which have quietly doubled since late July—are seeing a surge. The overarching lesson for investors is simple: stop waiting for a broken stock to save you, and follow the cash where it is actually moving.
