The AI Crisis in South Korea's Video Industry: Real Threat or Economic Scapegoat?

September 10, 2026

South Korea’s professional video and motion graphics industry is facing a severe downturn, with practitioners reporting that the combination of a prolonged economic slump and the rapid adoption of generative AI has led to shrinking job opportunities and falling project fees.

  • Economic double-whammy: A decline in corporate marketing budgets has led to fewer freelance projects, while clients increasingly demand that AI be used to cut costs.
  • Falling rates: Professionals report that project fees have dropped by 20% to 30% (about $150–$225 per average small-scale project), as clients argue that AI makes the work "easier and faster."
  • The "AI Trap": While AI excels at initial brainstorming and storyboarding, it struggles with the hyper-specific, iterative edits required for final commercial delivery, often leading to wasted time and increased frustration.
  • Need for new regulations: Industry leaders are calling for urgent legal frameworks—such as licensing models and protection against unauthorized data scraping—to prevent the collapse of the creative ecosystem.

The Perfect Storm: Recession Meets Automation

The current malaise in the Korean video market isn't driven solely by technology; it is a structural crisis. Studios report that while content demand seems high, the willingness of clients to pay for high-quality, human-led production has plummeted. Companies are aggressively slashing marketing budgets, and generative AI is being used as a justification to force down prices. As noted by Kim Young-min, director of the production studio Superberry Moore, the hiring market has dried up as firms opt for cheap automation over human labor.

The "AI-induced" price depression is creating a vicious cycle. Clients often demand that producers use AI to cut production time in half, yet they continue to demand the same level of granular, human-tailored control. This creates a "predictability hell" where designers spend more time fighting the limitations of AI tools than they would have spent creating the work from scratch.

Survival of the "Director," Not the "Tool-User"

Industry veterans argue that the era of the "technician-designer" is ending. Survival now depends on high-level directing and conceptual planning—skills AI cannot replicate. As AI lowers the barrier to entry for basic image generation, the premium is shifting toward those who can design a cohesive vision and solve complex client problems that go beyond mere visual output.

Furthermore, there is a growing consensus that without strong foundational design skills, AI users are essentially "building castles in the sand." The ability to discern quality and understand composition remains a human trait. As the industry grapples with the legal battle over AI training data—a fight currently stalled in the Korean National Assembly—designers are realizing that the most effective way to survive is to stop fearing the tool and start mastering it as a staff member, not a replacement.
