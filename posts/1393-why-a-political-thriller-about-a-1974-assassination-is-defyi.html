Why a Political Thriller About a 1974 Assassination Is Defying Conservative Boycotts in South Korea

October 22, 2026

South Korean investigative thriller The Assassins has crossed 3 million admissions in its fifth week, defying organized conservative boycotts and security threats to become one of 2026's biggest box office hits by tapping into the public's enduring hunger for press freedom and unresolved modern history.

  • The Milestone: The film surpassed 3 million tickets after drawing 1.4 million moviegoers over the four-day Chuseok holiday, ranking fourth overall at the domestic box office this year despite coordinated review bombing and calls for screening bans from conservative circles, including the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition.
  • The Story: Centered on the August 15, 1974 assassination of First Lady Yuk Young-soo—wife of military dictator Park Chung-hee—the film dramatizes reporters investigating gaping inconsistencies in the authoritarian regime's official single-gunman narrative.
  • The Disruption: Escalating backlash and physical threats against the cast and director forced organizers to cancel outdoor appearances and audience discussions at the prestigious Busan International Film Festival, yet ticket sales continued to surge.
  • The Resonance: The film chronicles the roots of South Korea's historic October 24, 1974 Declaration for Free Press Practice, contrasting the fierce resistance of 1970s journalists with contemporary concerns over partisan and commercialized media.

A Box Office Hit Born from Historical Scars

Directed around an ensemble cast including Yoo Hae-jin, Park Hae-il, and Lee Min-ho, The Assassins builds its narrative on investigative reporting originally unearthed in television documentaries. In 1974, Park Chung-hee's authoritarian government pinned Yuk's killing on Mun Se-gwang, an ethnic Korean from Japan linked to a pro-Pyongyang organization. Yet glaring procedural anomalies—missing shell casings, conflicting gunfire reports, and suspicious passport routes—went largely unreported at the time because the regime held mainstream newsrooms in a stranglehold.

The film captures the suffocating dread of the Yushin dictatorship, an era marked by the kangaroo-court trials of student dissidents and the rushed execution of eight defendants in the People's Revolutionary Party incident within 18 hours of their verdict. By highlighting the journalists who refused to surrender their conscience—staging newsroom sit-ins and demanding intelligence agents vacate their offices—the film taps into a deep reservoir of public empathy for reporters who faced torture, dismissal, and poverty for doing their jobs.

An Uncomfortable Mirror for Modern Media

The public's enthusiastic reception is also an implicit verdict on the state of Korean journalism today. Decades of sacrifice established independent newsrooms, labor unions, and newsroom autonomy agreements following the 1987 democratic transition. Yet many viewers feel that contemporary reporters have squandered that legacy, morphing into a self-serving cartel obsessed with portal clicks and access journalism. Prominent news anchors seamlessly transition into presidential spokespersons or ruling party legislative candidates, blurring the line between reporting and political ambition.

While mainstream media weathered open intimidation and regulatory capture during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term in office, the broader press environment remains heavily polarized into hostile ideological camps under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung. The viral momentum behind The Assassins reflects a public exhausted by algorithmic outrage and partisan echo chambers, seeking a reminder of what the press looked like when it spoke truth to power on behalf of the vulnerable rather than serving political factions.
