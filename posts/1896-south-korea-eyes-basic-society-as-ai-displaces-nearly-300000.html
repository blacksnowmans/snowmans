South Korea Eyes "Basic Society" as AI Displaces Nearly 300,000 Youth Jobs

November 03, 2026

South Korea is facing a severe employment crisis as nearly 300,000 youth jobs have vanished over the last three years, with 94% of those losses occurring in sectors highly vulnerable to artificial intelligence.

  • The Data: According to the Bank of Korea (the central bank), 285,000 jobs for those aged 15–29 disappeared recently, signaling that AI is replacing white-collar roles like programming and accounting faster than expected.
  • The Policy Shift: The administration of President Lee Jae-myung, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, is pivoting toward a "Basic Society" model to decouple basic survival from traditional labor.
  • Healthcare Innovation: A new AI-driven medical system is slated for completion within three years, aiming to provide specialist-level diagnostics to rural areas to fix the country’s "Seoul-centric" healthcare gap.
  • The Funding Model: Policymakers are proposing a public equity investment strategy—similar to the U.S. CHIPS Act—where the state takes stakes in tech firms to redistribute profits as universal basic income.

The "Basic Society" vs. the AI Employment Cliff

The impact of AI on the Korean labor market has shifted from a theoretical threat to a statistical reality. Data from the Bank of Korea suggests that the "levee is about to break," particularly for the youth. Unlike previous industrial shifts that replaced manual labor, the current AI revolution is hollowing out the middle-class professional tier. Experts like Park Tae-woong of the Nokseo Forum, a prominent tech policy group, warn that Korea ranks first in "anxiety" regarding AI job displacement because its economy is so heavily reliant on the very office-based roles AI performs best.

In response, the Lee Jae-myung administration is framing the "Basic Society" not as a welfare project, but as a systemic necessity. The logic is simple: if AI enables production without human labor, the state must find a new way to distribute that wealth. Rep. Cha Ji-ho of the Democratic Party is currently drafting a roadmap to integrate AI into public services, ensuring that the efficiency gains of technology are not monopolized by private capital but are used to guarantee "basic rights," such as high-quality healthcare and a floor for personal income.

Funding Redistribution Through Public Equity

The most radical shift in this proposal is how the government intends to pay for it. Rather than relying solely on traditional taxes, the administration is looking at a public equity model. By taking ownership stakes in innovative tech companies—much like the U.S. government did with Intel via the CHIPS Act—the Korean government aims to create a sovereign wealth fund of sorts. The proposed breakdown would see 50% of returns reinvested in future tech, 25% distributed as a universal dividend, and 25% targeted at youth support.

Critics often raise concerns about fiscal health, but the administration points to Korea's current debt-to-GDP ratio of approximately 46%, which remains low compared to other OECD nations. Furthermore, corporate tax revenues are projected to surge, with an expected increase of about $45.8 billion (63.2 trillion KRW) next year and nearly $95.7 billion (132 trillion KRW) the following year. This fiscal window is being viewed as a "golden time" to establish the infrastructure for a post-labor economy before the projected wave of mass unemployment hits in the next two to three years.

A Test Case in AI Healthcare

The first major pillar of this transition is an AI-based primary care system. By providing AI diagnostic tools to local clinics in rural areas, the government hopes to provide the same level of care found in major Seoul hospitals. This would allow a general practitioner in a remote village to diagnose complex conditions with the accuracy of a specialist. If successful, this "AI Medical System" could be exported to doctor-scarce regions like Africa or Pakistan, turning a domestic social safety net into a global technology brand.
