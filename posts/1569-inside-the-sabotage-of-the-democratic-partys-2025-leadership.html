Inside the Sabotage of the Democratic Party’s 2025 Leadership Race

October 28, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea saw its July 2025 leadership contest heavily disrupted by orchestrated external sabotage, as outside agitators infiltrated party ranks to manufacture a bitter feud between two key progressive allies.

  • The manufactured rivalry: Online smear campaigns aggressively demonized senior lawmaker Jung Chung-rae—who led parliamentary reform during the impeachment crisis of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—labeling him a traitorous "watermelon" (a derogatory Korean political slur for a conservative masquerading as a progressive) in an attempt to spark open war with his rival contender, Park Chan-dae.
  • Infiltration exposed: On July 30, 2025, Park Chan-dae held a press conference revealing that supporters of the now-impeached and imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol had organized via online platforms like Naver Band to register as fake party members and sow internal chaos.
  • Religious sect and polling allegations: Party figures, including Kim Min-seok, also raised alarms over suspected covert entry by the controversial Shincheonji religious movement and subsequent schemes by political YouTubers to manipulate internal convention surveys by faking demographics.
  • The fallout: The party initiated formal legal complaints under the Political Parties Act and formed internal task forces to audit membership rosters and shield primary voting from bad-faith digital disruption.

Manufacturing Discord Among Progressive Allies

The July 2025 contest was never meant to be an ideological civil war. Both Jung Chung-rae and Park Chan-dae were stalwart backers of President Lee Jae-myung's administration. However, aggressive online campaigns sought to pit them against each other, deploying crude framing such as the so-called "Mun-Jo-Teol-Rae-Yoo" blacklist to divide progressive factions. The smear campaign escalated until Park publicly intervened on July 21, warning supporters that attacking Jung harmed the entire party: "If Jung Chung-rae is a watermelon, then I, his comrade and friend, am a watermelon too."

Phantom Members and Systemic Vulnerabilities

The controversy highlighted a glaring vulnerability in South Korea's digital party democracy: low barriers to online party membership. By mobilizing covert cells—such as the pro-Yoon "Cheonjiin" online network—hostile external actors successfully weaponized primaries to paralyze internal debates with mutual paranoia. Instead of evaluating policy platforms, genuine members spent the election cycle vetting whether their peers were authentic progressives or covert saboteurs.

While the Democratic Party pushed back with legal filings and an overhaul of its voter authentication systems by mid-2026, the episode revealed the fragility of open party apparatuses in the digital era. When factional antagonism can be manufactured on demand by external political enemies, party reform depends less on attacking rivals and more on building airtight verification systems that keep phantom members from hijacking the vote.
