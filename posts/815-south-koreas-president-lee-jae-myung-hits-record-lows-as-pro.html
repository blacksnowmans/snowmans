South Korea’s President Lee Jae-myung Hits Record Lows as Progressive Allies Turn on Stalled Reforms

September 19, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung held a high-stakes press conference following a plunge in his approval rating to 33%, drawing fierce criticism from progressive commentators and his own core supporters.
  • What it changes: The backlash exposes deep fractures within South Korea's progressive camp, particularly over compromised prosecutorial reforms and a string of controversial political appointments.
  • Who is affected: President Lee's administration faces a narrowing window to recover public trust before upcoming political milestones, with key aides and ruling party figures caught in internal crossfire.

South Korean political commentators are openly questioning whether President Lee Jae-myung has abandoned his core reform promises after a widely criticized press conference laid bare a growing rift between the administration and its most loyal supporters. During a special live broadcast on the popular political YouTube channel Sisatapa TV, guest panelists picked apart the president's remarks, describing the event not as a display of accountability, but as a reluctant admission of political failure.

The core of the backlash centers on watered-down prosecutorial reforms and repeated staffing scandals. For years, progressive voters marched in the streets demanding the complete separation of investigative and indicting powers to strip South Korea's prosecution service of its unchecked authority. Yet, the administration's newly proposed blueprint—splitting investigative and indicting prosecutors within the same institutional framework—was slammed by critics as a bureaucratic shuffle that preserves old power cartels rather than dismantling them. Compounding the frustration, President Lee's choice to push forward with controversial appointments, such as the head of the newly established Major Crime Investigation Agency, has alienated the very base that brought him to power.

A Fractured Administration and the Limits of Apology

The press conference also highlighted unprecedented internal friction within the executive office. In a dramatic moment, Kang Yu-jung, a presidential spokesperson, stepped forward to voice sharp criticisms of the administration's handling of public dissent—a move panelists described as a de facto public resignation. Rather than addressing the substantive warnings, the administration has increasingly relied on "both-sidesism," equating the grievances of battered supporters with political opposition.

Commentators drew grim parallels to the final struggles of past conservative administrations, noting that low approval ratings paired with defensive rhetoric rarely end well. As South Korea approaches a major holiday political inflection point, the warning from the progressive grassroots is clear: cosmetic apologies and half-measures will no longer suffice. Without a sweeping overhaul of presidential staff and a return to genuine reform principles, President Lee risks squandering his remaining political momentum and plunging the ruling Democratic Party into deeper disarray.
