Democratic Party Concedes Cult Conspiracy Allegations Were Groundless as Korean Progressive Bloc Recalibrates

November 03, 2026

The leadership of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea has officially backed down from conspiracy allegations linking internal political rivals to the Shincheonji religious sect, admitting after an internal investigation that no evidence of a secret alliance exists.

  • What happened: A party spokesperson confirmed following a private lawmakers' meeting that an internal audit found zero evidence supporting allegations of a "three-party secret alliance" between rival factions and the controversial Shincheonji fringe religious group during the party's recent leadership convention.
  • Internal backlash: Senior party figure Jung Cheong-rae publicly rejected the proxy apology delivered behind closed doors on behalf of lawmaker Kim Min-seok, demanding a direct and public accounting to rank-and-file party members.
  • Opposition maneuvering: Shin Jang-sik, leader of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, explicitly rejected merger calls with the Democratic Party, unveiling a targeted strategy to contest 49 highly competitive capital-region districts in the next general election.
  • Policy developments: Meanwhile, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration revealed it is reviewing potential restrictions on youth social media access, sparking debates over constitutional rights and real-world efficacy.

Political Smears Backfire on Ruling Party Leadership

The collapse of the Shincheonji conspiracy narrative represents a serious self-inflicted wound for key factions within the Democratic Party of Korea. During the recent leadership race, accusations of covert collusion with the fringe group were weaponized to attack rival candidates and split the party base. With the party's own two-month task force investigation confirming the narrative was fabricated, calls for formal accountability have intensified. Senior lawmakers argue that offering a brief proxy apology behind closed doors undermines internal party democracy, insisting that those who spread divisive rumors must take direct responsibility before the public.

Rebuilding Korea Party Rejects Merger, Eyes Swing Seats

Beyond the ruling party’s internal strife, South Korea's broader progressive camp is seeing a strategic realignment. Rebuilding Korea Party leader Shin Jang-sik marked his first 100 days by ruling out an arbitrary merger with the Democratic Party. Pointing to 49 swing districts in the greater Seoul area where the margin between progressives and the main opposition conservative People Power Party was under 10 percentage points, Shin pledged to run distinct progressive candidates against uncompetitive or conservative-leaning Democratic nominees rather than offering automatic endorsement.

Concurrently, President Lee Jae-myung's administration is navigating a complex domestic debate after proposing potential limits on youth social media usage. Citing internal polls showing nearly 80% public support for intervention, President Lee highlighted concerns over algorithmic addiction, cyberbullying, and deepfake crimes among minors. However, the proposal faces resistance regarding youth communication rights, with critics pointing to global examples like Australia—where platform fines of up to 49.5 million Australian dollars (about $32 million USD) have simply driven teenagers toward virtual private networks (VPNs) and unmonitored online spaces.
