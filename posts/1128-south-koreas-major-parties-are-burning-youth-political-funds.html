South Korea's Major Parties are Burning Youth Political Funds on Internal Payrolls and Dinners

October 14, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean political parties are legally required to spend at least 5% of their state operating subsidies on youth political development — amounting to roughly $7 million (about 9 billion won) over three quarters for major parties like the Democratic Party and the People Power Party.
  • Who is affected: Young aspiring politicians and taxpayers, as data shows the conservative People Power Party spent 95.7% of its youth development budget on internal staff payrolls, while the liberal Democratic Party left nearly 70% of its allocation unaccounted for.
  • What it changes: Critics argue that state funding meant to foster a new generation of leaders is instead vanishing into bureaucratic overhead and casual dining expenses, exposing a hollow political system that treats young politicians as mere window dressing.

South Korea’s political landscape is plastered with banners calling for "young blood" in parliament, but a hard look at how state funds and candidate pipelines actually operate reveals a grim reality. For outsiders watching Seoul's political arena, the entry barriers for youth are formidable. Without deep-pocketed patrons or solid party connections, young candidates face a massive fortress with zero manuals on how to run a successful campaign. Existing youth-recruitment programs lean heavily on scripted debate shows that reward mechanical speech-making over genuine policy depth and empathy, leaving participants struggling on the periphery of Yeouido, Seoul's political heart.

Compounding this exclusion is the opaque handling of taxpayer money. Under South Korea's Political Funds Act, major parties receive hefty operating subsidies, with a strict 5% mandate dedicated entirely to cultivating young political talent. Yet, instead of funding genuine mentorship or policy training, these funds are routinely mismanaged. The People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, absorbed nearly all of its youth budget into internal payrolls, while the ruling Democratic Party—the party of President Lee Jae-myung—provided no transparent accounting for the vast majority of its remaining youth funds. Local youth politicians report that what little money remains often vanishes into casual dining and drinking sessions.

With Yoon Seok-yeol stripped of the presidency and imprisoned following his impeachment, South Korea is navigating a broader political reset under the Lee Jae-myung administration. Yet, until watchdogs and voters demand strict institutional penalties—such as slashing subsidies for parties that sideline youth or misuse funds—talk of empowering the next generation will remain little more than an empty slogan paid for by public taxes.
