Why South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Ratings Are Free-Falling

October 30, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings have stalled while his party’s numbers have crashed into the 30s, driven not by opposition attacks but by a self-inflicted breakdown of his own liberal coalition.

  • What happened: Approval ratings for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP) dropped into the mid-30s—falling behind the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP)—following the June 24 resignation of DP chairman Jung Chung-rae.
  • What it changes: The departure of key reformist leaders and heavy-handed presidential intervention in candidate nominations have alienated core progressive voters, particularly in Seoul and the traditional liberal stronghold of the Honam region.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung and the DP face mounting political paralysis as administration loyalists alienate base voters by prioritizing personal loyalty over party unity and reform.

The Collapse of Party Defense and Scapegoating

The inflection point for the current political crisis occurred in mid-June, culminating on June 24 when DP chairman Jung Chung-rae resigned to run in the party’s upcoming national convention. Backed by 46% of party members, Jung functioned as the administration’s key defender and a symbol of aggressive reform. His departure exposed severe internal rifts. Friction intensified when President Lee publicly criticized party leadership after his preferred candidate lost the Seoul mayoral primary, triggering widespread resentment among base voters who felt the president was deflecting responsibility for his own political miscalculations.

Rather than addressing these structural errors, presidential loyalists have scapegoated external critics. When prominent liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min observed that President Lee’s declining polling numbers mirror the dangerous trajectory seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency before his impeachment, loyalists bizarrely blamed Rhyu’s commentary for the drop. Rhyu scoffed at the claim, pointing out that if a single pundit could derail a presidency, the administration’s political foundation must be precarious indeed.

Historical Lessons and the Risk of Camp Dissolution

Progressive voters backed Lee Jae-myung to carry out systemic reforms, not to build a personal fandom. When an administration treats the ruling party as a personal organ and marginalizes broader progressive factions, base voters withdraw their support. This dynamic echoes historical betrayals in South Korean politics, such as the 1990 conservative three-party merger under Kim Young-sam, where political reshuffling behind closed doors triggered massive public backlash.

The DP’s current decline is a direct consequence of alienating the broader liberal coalition that brought President Lee to power. Unless the administration stops blaming outside figures and restores internal democracy within the ruling party, it risks permanent loss of public trust and total policy paralysis.
