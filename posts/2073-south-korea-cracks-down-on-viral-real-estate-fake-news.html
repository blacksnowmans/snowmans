South Korea Cracks Down on Viral Real Estate Fake News

November 07, 2026

South Korea's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport has vowed a strict police investigation into a viral social media rumor claiming the government would ban mortgages on homes worth over 2.5 billion won (about $1.8 million) and designate new heavily regulated housing zones.

  • What happened: A fabricated November real estate policy document spread across online communities and messengers, detailing extreme loan bans and new housing restrictions in key Gyeonggi Province areas like Suwon, Bucheon, and Anyang.
  • What it changes: The Ministry officially dismissed the documents as entirely false, confirming that no such extreme lending curbs or sudden regional designations are under review by President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.
  • Who is affected: Homebuyers, prospective first-time owners, and residents in targeted districts panicked and flooded financial institutions with inquiries before the government stepped in to refute the claims.

For South Korea's prospective homebuyers, housing policy rumors are never just idle gossip; they strike directly at household financial planning and long-term security. The fact that fabricated statistics—such as a zero-mortgage rule for properties over 2.5 billion won—gained traction so quickly highlights deep-seated public anxiety. Many citizens remain psychologically scarred by the unpredictable policy shifts and repeated confusion of the past under the impeached Yoon Seok-yeol administration. When trust in housing stability feels fragile, malicious actors find fertile ground to manipulate market sentiment for speculative gain.

By bypassing standard denial and immediately requesting a police investigation, the current administration is sending a sharp warning against market-distorting misinformation. Real estate scams and anonymous tip sheets ("jirashi") that weaponize information asymmetry to terrify ordinary citizens into panic-buying or selling off properties are treated as serious economic crimes. While South Korea's housing market faces genuine challenges from high interest rates and tight household debt controls, this decisive pushback aims to establish a transparent information ecosystem where policy is driven by data, not panic-inducing digital whispers.
