South Korea's New Polling Data Signals Deep Political Shifts

October 06, 2026

Fresh polling data reveals a divided public over President Lee Jae-myung's administration and a shifting race among opposition contenders, exposing deep cracks in the country's political landscape.

    What happened: A recent Realmeter survey shows President Lee's approval ratings hovering between 38% and 47% depending on the polling method, while the left-of-centre Democratic Party pulls ahead of the conservative People Power Party in overall support. What it changes: The race to succeed Lee is heating up, with prominent opposition figures like Kim Min-seok, Cho Kuk, and Jeong Cheong-rae jostling for position and energizing progressive voters. Who is affected: The ruling and opposition camps are forced to rethink their strategies as a volatile, fragmented electorate proves increasingly impatient with partisan gridlock and economic pressures.

A Divided Electorate and Shifting Presidential Contenders

The numbers behind President Lee's approval ratings tell a story of a fractured public. Depending on whether pollsters use automated phone responses or live interviews, negative assessments of Lee's performance swing from 52% to over 60%. This gap highlights a volatile middle class and an electorate heavily siloed by opposing political frames. The numbers suggest that Lee's administration faces a tough test in bridging deep demographic and regional divides.

Meanwhile, the race for the next presidential run is reshaping the progressive opposition. Lawmaker Kim Min-seok currently leads the pack, but figures like former lawmaker Jeong Cheong-rae and Rebuilding Korea Party leader Cho Kuk are gaining serious traction. Jeong's rise, in particular, points to a progressive base hungry for aggressive leadership and sharp opposition rhetoric over traditional consensus-building.

The Opposition's Advantage and the Cost of Polarization

While the Democratic Party enjoys a widening lead over the conservative People Power Party—which continues to struggle with internal cohesion—this political dominance comes with heavy caveats. The widening gap between the parties risks driving further political polarization at a time when voters are primarily worried about inflation, low growth, and a lack of future economic vision.

Ultimately, these polls are less a report card on individual politicians than a warning sign from an exhausted public. If Korean political parties continue to rely on partisan warfare rather than serious policy competition, they risk squandering the public trust entirely. The data makes one thing clear: voters are looking past mere popularity contests and demanding tangible solutions to the country's structural crises.
