Why South Korea's Young Men Are Giving Up on Marriage

September 11, 2026

Marriage in South Korea is no longer seen as a milestone, but as a high-risk financial trap that an increasing number of young adults are choosing to bypass entirely.

  • The Housing Barrier: Buying a modest 24-pyeong (about 850 sq ft) apartment in Seoul requires $450,000 to $530,000 (6억 to 7억 won)—meaning an entry-level worker needs at least $110,000 to $150,000 in cash just to secure a down payment.
  • Delayed Careers: Driven by intense competition and higher education, the average starting age for a major corporate job has climbed past 30.
  • The Financial Squeeze: A stark wage gap between conglomerates and small-to-medium enterprises leaves many young workers feeling they cannot afford even a solo life, let alone a family.
  • The Divorce Risk: Changing legal standards for asset division and soaring divorce rates have turned marriage into what many young men view as a zero-sum legal gamble.

The Math That Killed Romance

For older generations in South Korea, starting a marriage in a tiny rented room and working your way up was an accepted path. Today, that route is dead. With entry-level salaries hovering around $25,000 a year, a young professional would have to save every single penny for years simply to afford the deposit on an average Seoul apartment. When the cost of living, extreme educational expenses for children (such as private English kindergartens costing upwards of $1,400 a month), and heavy household debt enter the equation, marriage ceases to be a blessing and becomes a survival test.

Societal expectations have also shifted dramatically. Men are still expected to shoulder primary financial responsibility, yet they face intense pressure to handle equal shares of domestic labor and childcare while subsisting on meager personal allowances—often around $230 a month. Caught between the grinding demands of work and home, many men describe themselves as little more than "money-making machines." Meanwhile, the fear of losing half their hard-earned assets in a potential divorce adds a final, paralyzing layer of dread.

A Rational Response to a Broken System

Labeling South Korea's youth as selfish or uncommitted misses the point entirely. The surge in single-household living and voluntary singlehood ("bihon") is not a cultural whim; it is a calculated retreat from an unworkable economic model. Until the state and society address the root causes—sky-high housing costs, punishing work cultures, and a lack of social safety nets for struggling families—scolding young people for refusing to marry will change nothing.
