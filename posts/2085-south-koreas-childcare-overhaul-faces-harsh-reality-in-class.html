South Korea’s Childcare Overhaul Faces Harsh Reality in Classrooms and Small Workplaces

November 07, 2026

South Korea’s drive to modernize early childhood education is colliding with deep structural inequities, from persistent funding gaps between daycare centers and kindergartens to the routine firing of workers at small firms who attempt to take parental leave.

  • Preschool integration hits hurdles: Local lawmakers are raising alarms over the government's integration of kindergartens and daycare centers (a policy known as yubo tonghap), warning that administrative mergers mean little without closing subsidy gaps—currently around 110,000 won versus 70,000 won (about $80 vs. $50) per child—and addressing severe rural teacher shortages.
  • Small-business penalty: Reports reveal that only one in three workers at businesses with fewer than five employees can freely use maternity leave, with many still facing dismissal if they apply for parental leave.
  • Safety and welfare concerns: A recent child abuse scandal in the coastal city of Gangneung prompted emergency audits across 93 centers, while daycare associations in Gwangju took to the streets to protest regional disparities in children's meal and snack subsidies.
  • Budget strain: Local councils, including Gyeonggi Province, are pushing to restore slashed family care allowances, arguing that direct home-care subsidies are crucial for parents stuck on waiting lists for infant care.

The Small-Firm Blind Spot Undermining Family Policy

South Korea’s demographic crisis cannot be solved with cash transfers alone when basic labor protections still evaporate at smaller workplaces. While large corporations offer structured parental leave, the law offers little practical protection to employees in businesses with fewer than five workers. These workers make up a massive slice of the domestic labor market, yet taking statutory leave remains an invitation to be fired. Unless the Ministry of Employment and Labor enforces strict penalties and provides direct wage subsidies to small businesses, government pledges to boost the birth rate will remain empty promises for the country's most vulnerable working parents.

Integration on Paper, Neglect on the Ground

The push by President Lee Jae-myung’s administration to unify education and childcare tracks addresses a decades-old divide between education-oriented kindergartens and welfare-oriented daycare centers. However, simply merging administrative oversight will not resolve the stark reality inside the classrooms. Daycare centers in regional towns like Sancheong County are already facing crippling staffing shortages because wages and working conditions lag far behind rising classroom pressures. When facilities in different regions receive unequal budgets even for basic meals and snacks, the system fails its core promise of equal care for every child.

Paper reforms that shuffle bureaucratic reporting lines will not prevent classroom abuse or stabilize the workforce. Lasting progress requires equalizing per-child funding, closing the digital infrastructure gap, and shielding working mothers from workplace retaliation. Without these basic structural fixes, the burden of South Korea's broken care system will continue to fall squarely on exhausted teachers and isolated parents.
