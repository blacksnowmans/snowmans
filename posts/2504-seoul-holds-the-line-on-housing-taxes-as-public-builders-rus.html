Seoul Holds the Line on Housing Taxes as Public Builders Rush Small Sites

November 19, 2026

South Korea’s Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) refused industry demands on November 18 to scrap a windfall tax on apartment redevelopment, on the same day the state-run Korea Land and Housing Corporation (LH) broke ground on a fast-track urban housing project in southern Seoul.

  • Fast-track infill over mega-projects: LH began construction on a small-scale street-housing project in Seocho-gu’s Yangjae-dong, launching a roadmap to build 11,000 urban Seoul homes by 2030 on three-to-five-year timelines instead of the decade-plus typical of major redevelopments.
  • Tax relief off the table: MOLIT maintained that the reconstruction excess profit tax—which exempts gains up to 80 million won (about $58,000) per owner—has limited impact on supply, rejecting developers' claims that construction costs nearing 10 million won ($7,200) per 3.3 square meters have stalled projects.
  • Suburban infrastructure revolt: While Prime Minister Han Seong-sook urged 24 Gyeonggi Province mayors to speed up permits for 104,000 planned homes, Gimpo Mayor Lee Gi-hyeong pushed back against a 37,000-unit plan in Gochon, demanding central funding and special economic zone status to attract businesses rather than another transit-choked commuter town.
  • Cash-only urban market: Tighter lending rules and soaring prices now require 700 million to 800 million won ($500,000 to $580,000) in cash upfront for new Seoul apartments, sidelining middle-class buyers while wealthy Gangnam owners gift properties to children and bid on leftover units.
  • Closing gated loopholes: Authorities moved to legally bar newly redeveloped complexes from locking public walkways that builders had promised in exchange for higher building density.

The Supply Trap Between Cost and Cash

The Lee Jae-myung government is betting on a two-track workaround—tiny infill apartments inside Seoul and large tracts in surrounding Gyeonggi Province—because traditional private redevelopment has slowed. Yet both tracks face structural friction. Small LH complexes of one or two buildings lack the shared amenities that attract Korean families, while suburban mega-projects like Gimpo Gochon threaten to overwhelm already hazardous commuter lines like the Gimpo Goldline unless central ministries fund rail and road upgrades upfront.

Meanwhile, the gap between Seoul’s prime districts and the rest of the country is widening into a market fracture. Major builders such as Daewoo E&C are competing aggressively for marquee Seoul neighborhoods like Yeouido, Mokdong, and Yongsan, even as regional redevelopment bids in cities like Busan draw only a single contractor—forcing cities like Gwangju to audit 20 stalled sites just to secure housing for future semiconductor workers.

For ordinary households hoping to buy a home, the policy deadlock leaves little relief in sight. With MOLIT unwilling to ease redevelopment levies and banks tightening household credit under South Korea's Stress DSR Phase 2 lending caps, new Seoul apartments are shifting out of reach for salaried workers and turning into an exclusive cash market for high-net-worth families.
