South Korea’s New Investigative Agency Faces "Prosecutorial Capture" Allegations

September 10, 2026

South Korea’s upcoming launch of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA)—a new body designed to strip the prosecution of its monopoly on investigations—is facing intense backlash as critics argue it is being hollowed out from within by the very establishment it is meant to reform.

  • The Setup: The nine-member committee responsible for recommending the agency’s first chief includes three former prosecutors, with no representation from the police force.
  • The Conflict: Lawmakers, notably Park Eun-jung, have slammed the committee for being stacked with figures loyal to the current administration and those involved in past controversial investigations.
  • The Financial Gap: While the MCIA is set to take over 100,000 cases from the prosecution, its budget increase is a meager 300 million won (about $225,000), which critics warn is insufficient to support actual operations.
  • The Core Fear: Opponents worry the MCIA will become "Prosecution Season 2," maintaining the status quo under a new name rather than achieving a genuine separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers.

The Problem of "Rotating Doors"

The core of the controversy lies in the staffing process. The committee tasked with selecting the MCIA’s leadership is dominated by figures linked to the current power structure, including former aides to the prosecutor general. Furthermore, the vetting process for these candidates is handled by the presidential office's civil service discipline secretariat, which is also largely staffed by current or former prosecutors.

This creates a self-perpetuating loop where the prosecution effectively selects its own overseer. Lawmakers argue that if the MCIA is to be independent, it must be led by someone outside the traditional prosecutorial "caste." Instead, the administration appears to be positioning "pro-Yoon" (loyalists to President Yoon Suk Yeol) prosecutors into the agency’s top ranks, raising serious concerns about the impartiality of future high-stakes investigations.

Budgetary Strangulation and Structural Decay

Beyond personnel, the practical reality of the agency is raising alarm bells. With over 100,000 cases moving from the prosecution to the MCIA, the allocated budget is effectively a cut when adjusted for inflation. Critics argue that without adequate infrastructure, data analysis tools, and personnel funding, the agency is set up to fail. This "administrative theater"—creating an agency while starving it of the resources needed to function—suggests that the government may be prioritizing optics over meaningful judicial reform.

Furthermore, recent allegations regarding unethical lobbying and attempts to influence judicial outcomes—specifically reports of prosecutors requesting the removal of specific judges—have cast a shadow over the entire legal system. If the new agency is born into a culture where such "judicial manipulation" is suspected, its mission to restore public trust in the law remains an uphill battle. Genuine reform requires more than just new titles; it requires a fundamental break from the entrenched habits of a system that has long prioritized its own power over procedural justice.
