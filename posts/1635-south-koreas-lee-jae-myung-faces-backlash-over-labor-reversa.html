South Korea’s Lee Jae-myung Faces Backlash Over Labor Reversals and Combative Messaging

October 28, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung is confronting sharp criticism from his progressive base as pragmatic pushes to bolster strategic tech industries clash directly with his core campaign promises to expand labor protections.

  • The policy shift: The administration is moving to grant special exemptions from the statutory 52-hour weekly work cap for research and development staff in key industries like semiconductors, contradicting Lee's signature pledge to introduce a 4.5-day workweek.
  • The economic rationale: Officials argue that overtime flexibility is vital for strategic initiatives—such as the Honam regional semiconductor mega-project—to outpace intensifying global competition from China.
  • The rhetoric controversy: During a live-streamed cabinet meeting, Lee sparked public outrage by asserting that migrant workers send virtually all their earnings abroad rather than spending domestically—a claim refuted by data showing roughly 43% of these workers spend their wages locally.
  • The press dispute: The presidential office picked a high-profile fight with The Hankyoreh, a prominent center-left daily, publicly demanding a retraction over headline nuances regarding his migrant labor remarks.

The Dilemma: Strategic Chip Rivalry vs. Labor Rights

Lee's push to waive the 52-hour cap for elite tech engineers is driven by urgency. Facing pressure from the conservative opposition, the People Power Party, to produce tangible economic outcomes, the administration insists speed and regulatory flexibility are necessary to secure South Korea’s tech supremacy. However, pushing this change through legislation without deep public consensus has blindsided labor unions. Labor advocates warn that carving out exemptions for select researchers inevitably bleeds into production lines and subcontracting networks—a recurring pattern observed during Yoon Suk-yeol's term in office that eroded core labor standards.

Unfiltered Governance and Communication Stumbles

The administration’s communication tactics have compounded its policy troubles. Live-streamed cabinet sessions, initially intended to project transparency, have instead broadcast ill-prepared remarks and apparent buck-passing, such as Lee distancing himself from controversial farmland audits by shifting focus onto Agriculture Minister Song Mi-ryung. Lee's inaccurate assertion regarding migrant worker remittances drew accusations of scapegoating a vulnerable demographic to appeal to rural constituents, undermining his platform of social inclusion.

Rather than quietly clarifying the misstatement, Lee took to social media to wage a semantic battle, arguing bitterly over whether he meant migrant labor was "not helpful" or "not very helpful." Relentlessly policing press semantics and labeling domestic critics with polarizing tags may rally hardcore online followers, but it burns valuable political capital. To drive structural overhauls and regional industrial investments, the Lee administration must pivot from litigious online squabbles back to steady policy coordination and coalition-building.
