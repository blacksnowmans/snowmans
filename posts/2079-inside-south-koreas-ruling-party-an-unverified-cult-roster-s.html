Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party, an Unverified Cult Roster Sparks Factional Warfare and Privacy Alarms

November 07, 2026

A shadowy, unverified roster purporting to link members of the controversial Shincheonji religious sect to the ruling Democratic Party of Korea has ignited a bitter internal clash, raising serious concerns over unlawful data leaks from state investigative agencies.

  • Dubious intelligence: Democratic Party lawmaker Choi Min-hee reportedly reviewed an unverified roster tracking alleged Shincheonji members from 2015 to 2021, though the dossier cited a minuscule tally—just 10 to 15 members in Seoul’s Mapo district—undermining claims of a massive infiltration.
  • Violations of privacy law: Cross-referencing sensitive religious affiliations with political party rolls without consent constitutes a severe violation of South Korea's Personal Information Protection Act, fueling suspicions that the files were illicitly leaked by law enforcement or prosecutors who gathered them during past COVID-19 church crackdowns.
  • Factional crossfire: The faction surrounding President Lee Jae-myung has begun to fracture over the fallout, with lawmakers like Kim Min-seok under fire from grassroots supporters for calling for police inquiries that critics dismiss as stalling tactics.
  • Weaponized suspicion: Hardliners have used the unverified claims to target senior figures such as Representative Jung Chung-rae, turning an unsubstantiated dossier into an internal purge weapon ahead of party elections.

Political Warfare Built on Thin Evidence

The scandal exposes how easily unverified intelligence can paralyze party politics. Shincheonji, widely viewed by mainstream Korean society as a fringe cult, has long been a political lightning rod. Yet framing an organized party takeover around a list showing barely a dozen members in a major Seoul district stretches credulity. The fact that most Democratic Party lawmakers refused to touch the dossier—recognizing that handling improperly sourced material is both politically toxic and legally perilous—highlights the reckless nature of airing it publicly.

More alarming than the partisan mudslinging is the source of the data itself. Private citizens and individual lawmaker offices cannot legally compile nationwide lists of religious adherents and cross-reference them with proprietary political party registries. If state investigative bodies or task forces leaked confidential pandemic-era records to influence internal party dynamics, it echoes the politicized intelligence abuses seen during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, which current ruling party figures once vigorously denounced.

A Self-Inflicted Crisis for the Ruling Bloc

For President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, this manufactured crisis is an unwelcome distraction that threatens internal cohesion. Instead of consolidating control to advance the government's legislative agenda, allied lawmakers are consumed by mutual distrust. Representative Choi Min-hee, who championed reviewing the records, faces mounting credibility issues of her own—including intense scrutiny over questionable medical theories she endorsed in the past and ongoing criticism over receiving wedding gift money from state audit targets while chairing the parliamentary science and technology committee.

By resorting to opaque dossiers and whispered allegations, ruling party actors are indulging in a dangerous brand of internal policing. Deflecting accountability by referring the issue to the police will only prolong the controversy. Unless party leadership demands full transparency on where this leaked roster originated and halts the use of unvetted dossiers to settle political scores, the Democratic Party risks undermining its own moral standing and procedural legitimacy.
