President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Rating Sinks Amid Market Turmoil and Internal Strife

November 09, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dropped to 35.9% as a domestic stock market crash and unresolved internal party scandals weigh heavily on the ruling Democratic Party.

  • Market Panic: South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI and KOSDAQ indexes both plunged below the 5,000 level, triggering "circuit breakers" (emergency trading halts) and fueling public anxiety over the administration's economic management.
  • Opposition Momentum: Former Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has emerged as the clear frontrunner for the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, despite ongoing police investigations into his signature "Hangang Bus" project.
  • Judicial Distrust: Public trust in investigative agencies, including the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO), has collapsed to the 20% range, reflecting widespread frustration with the slow pace of post-impeachment reforms.
  • Ruling Party Friction: The Democratic Party (DP) is struggling with a scandal involving alleged ties to the Shincheonji religious group and a stalled merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP), a minor progressive party led by Cho Kuk.

Administrative Scandals and the Conservative Resurgence

The political landscape is shifting in favor of the opposition as the "Hangang Bus" controversy intensifies. Former Mayor Oh Se-hoon is accused of misrepresenting a decision by the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), the state’s top audit agency. While the BAI officially told the city to conduct its own internal audit, Oh reportedly claimed the agency had cleared him. With the statute of limitations for "spreading false information" under election law set to expire in less than 30 days, police are under immense pressure to conclude their investigation. The optics worsened recently after PPP members of the National Assembly’s Security and Public Administration Committee—who oversee the police budget—held a private dinner at Oh’s residence, sparking allegations of a "power cartel" attempting to influence the probe.

In contrast to the ruling camp's fragmentation, the conservative opposition is consolidating around Oh. His primary rival, Han Dong-hoon, has seen his support dwindle due to aggressive rhetoric that fails to appeal to moderate voters. For the DP, the lack of a clear "successor" to President Lee is becoming a liability. While figures like Cho Kuk and DP Chairman Kim Min-seok are active, they remain locked in a "minor league" struggle for influence, failing to project the same stability that the conservative "one-top" system currently offers to its base.

Market Panic and the Progressive Identity Crisis

President Lee’s dip in support—falling from nearly 40% to 35.9% in a single week—is primarily driven by "bread-and-butter" issues. Political scandals can be weathered, but the collapse of the stock market and rising living costs are direct hits to the administration's perceived competence. This economic anxiety is compounded by a sense of "reform fatigue." Years after the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, voters feel that the judicial system remains untouched. Trust in the courts sits at just 41%, and the public is increasingly critical of what they see as "lukewarm" punishments for those involved in the previous administration's constitutional violations.

The path forward for the progressive camp depends on a "unified front," yet the merger between the DP and the RKP remains in limbo. The RKP fears its candidates will be sidelined by the DP’s massive primary system, while the DP’s leadership is hesitant to share power. If the ruling party continues to prioritize internal "math" over a clear national vision, they risk losing the middle-of-the-road voters who are currently recoiling from the market crash and the perceived arrogance of the ruling elite. The window for President Lee to regain momentum is closing as the public’s patience with "reform delays" reaches a breaking point.
