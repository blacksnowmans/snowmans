Seoul’s Green Spaces Under Threat: The Cost of Urban Development

September 30, 2026

South Korea’s rapid urban development is reaching a critical inflection point as the government prioritizes immediate housing supply and budget gains over the preservation of vital "urban lungs."

  • The "Sunset Law" Crisis: A 2020 regulation meant to finalize land use has led to the loss of 15.08 square kilometers of public green space nationwide, valued at over $2.1 billion (3 trillion KRW) in land prices alone.
  • Future Debt: By converting designated park land into commercial or residential zones to solve short-term fiscal deficits, local governments are effectively "eating the seeds"—destroying essential ecological infrastructure that future generations will need to survive.
  • The JYP Case: A recent project involving a new headquarters for JYP Entertainment (a major South Korean music label) was jeopardized after administrative pressure to maximize commercial density destroyed the surrounding green space, ruining the project's original, eco-friendly vision.

The "Essential Infrastructure" Fallacy

For too long, urban planners in Seoul have treated parks as aesthetic luxuries rather than critical infrastructure. History tells a different story. In 19th-century Britain, the lack of urban planning and green spaces led to catastrophic mortality rates, turning industrial cities into death traps. Today, the "Sunset Law"—which forces the de-designation of private land slated for parks if not purchased by the government within a certain timeframe—is being used as an excuse for reckless development. When the Seoul Metropolitan Government or local agencies like SH Corporation (the Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation) convert park land into commercial real estate to generate quick revenue, they are opting for a short-term cash injection at the expense of long-term climate resilience.

Lessons from Yongsan and Beyond

The development of Yongsan Park, located on a massive 90-acre site formerly occupied by foreign military bases, represents a generational crossroads. There is constant political pressure to carve out sections of this land for residential housing. However, as experts argue, a city is not just a collection of buildings; it requires a "respiratory system." Small neighborhood parks act as capillaries, but large-scale green zones like Yongsan are the lungs of the city.

The current administrative tendency to prioritize high-density commercial zoning—as seen in the failed integration of the JYP headquarters with local public space—demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of modern urban value. A city that sacrifices its natural environment to attract businesses will eventually drive away the creative talent that requires a high quality of life to flourish. True urban progress does not come from top-down, government-led concrete expansion, but from creating environments where nature and private investment can coexist. Choosing to "sell the furniture to pay for dinner" is a path that offers temporary black-ink ledgers at the cost of our children's future health.
