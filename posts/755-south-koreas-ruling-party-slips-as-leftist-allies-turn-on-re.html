South Korea's Ruling Party Slips as Leftist Allies Turn on Reforming Ambitions

September 14, 2026

  • South Korean President Lee Jae-myung's administration faces a sharp drop in public approval, hitting a post-inauguration low with 38% positive and 51% negative ratings in a recent Gallup poll.
  • The Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive ally, has launched a blistering critique against Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok, warning that shifting right and appointing conservative figures will destroy the progressive coalition.
  • Critics within the progressive camp are also lambasting the government over watered-down prosecutorial reforms and controversial plans regarding foreign troop deployments.
  • The friction exposes deep cracks in South Korea's liberal political bloc, threatening the administration's legislative agenda and driving voters toward silent disillusionment.

South Korea's progressive landscape is fracturing from within. Barely settled into power, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal political force—are watching their momentum evaporate. Instead of basking in a traditional post-election honeymoon, the government is grappling with a steep slide in opinion polls driven by controversial personnel choices, perceived policy flip-flops, and a growing disconnect with its base.

The situation turned combative when the Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller progressive faction that helped unseat the previous conservative government, openly declared war on the Democratic leadership. Party leaders slammed Kim Min-seok for chasing conservative endorsements through "arithmetic addition" while alienating the loyal allies who brought the progressives to power. Rather than executing structural reforms, critics argue the administration is settling for cosmetic changes that leave entrenched bureaucratic and prosecutorial privileges intact.

Prosecutorial Reform and Foreign Entanglements Threaten Public Trust

Beyond domestic personnel feuds, the administration is catching heat for backing down on core reform promises. Long-demanded overhauls of South Korea's powerful investigative agencies have stalled into bureaucratic loopholes, leaving prosecutors with preserved staffing limits and administrative workarounds rather than the systemic dismantling voters expected. Critics point out that treating prosecutors like privileged judiciary members instead of administrative civil servants undermines the foundational separation of powers.

Compounding these domestic missteps are dangerous signals from the foreign policy front. Rumors and internal deliberations regarding potential troop deployments to contested regions, such as the Strait of Hormuz, have sparked fierce pushback. Observers argue that kowtowing to external pressures for foreign conflicts risks repeating past diplomatic subservience at the expense of South Korean lives and national sovereignty.

For an administration swept into office on a wave of popular reform, the message from the public and allied parties is unmistakable. Cosmetic compromises and fear-driven diplomacy will not rebuild fractured trust. If the ruling bloc continues to ignore its foundational base in favor of internal factionalism and half-measures, it risks squandering its political mandate entirely.
