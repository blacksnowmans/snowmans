South Korea Tackles an $8 Billion Sleep Debt as Digital Health Steps In

October 20, 2026

South Korean workers are among the most sleep-deprived in the world, generating an estimated 11 trillion won (about $8.2 billion) in annual economic losses and turning chronic exhaustion from a badge of diligence into a recognized public health crisis.

  • A persistent deficit: South Korean office workers average roughly six hours of sleep per night—an hour or two less than recommended by the World Health Organization—with seven in ten reporting chronic sleep shortages.
  • A sharp policy reversal: Following contentious attempts to expand working hours during Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, the current government under President Lee Jae-myung has shifted policy priorities toward labor reduction and protecting workers' fundamental right to rest.
  • Clinical precision: Medical specialists at Seoul National University Hospital emphasize that true insomnia is distinct from simple sleep deprivation; it is diagnosed when a person cannot sleep despite having adequate time and opportunity, persisting at least three times a week for more than three months.
  • Software over sedatives: In response to widespread reliance on alcohol and sleeping pills, South Korea is pioneering digital cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-I) through tools like "Somzz," the nation’s first approved digital therapeutic app, which improved patient sleep efficiency to 78.3% in clinical trials.

Why Seoul Is Rethinking Exhaustion as a Virtue

For decades, South Korea’s rapid economic expansion championed long hours, viewing sleep almost as dead capital. That ethos reached a breaking point during Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, when proposed labor policies sought to dramatically raise work-hour ceilings. The resulting public backlash highlighted how widespread chronic fatigue had become. Under President Lee Jae-myung's administration, the political conversation has pivoted toward treating rest not as a corporate concession, but as essential infrastructure for public health and economic productivity.

The biological stakes underscore this policy shift. Sleep medicine specialists, including Professor Lee Yu-jin at Seoul National University Hospital, point out that sleep deprivation directly degrades the prefrontal cortex, spikes cortisol, and leaves the immune system severely compromised. Chronic sleep deficits also inflict measurable damage on national productivity, demonstrating that cutting sleep to boost output is an economic fallacy.

From Sedatives to Behavioral Technology

Many Koreans still treat poor sleep with hazardous self-medication, particularly alcohol. While a nightcap may induce drowsiness, clinicians warn it creates "fake sleep" by disrupting REM cycles, spiking the heart rate, and exacerbating dangerous sleep apnea. Similarly, prescription sedatives like zolpidem have long served as an overused stopgap rather than a cure for behavioral sleep disorders.

The clinical solution is shifting toward scalable, non-pharmacological interventions. Instead of chemically forcing unconsciousness, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia focuses on stimulus control: establishing fixed wake-up times, banning clocks from view, and removing screens from the bedroom. By packaging these protocols into approved digital prescription platforms like Somzz, South Korea is attempting to systematically undo its culture of burnout through clinical software.
