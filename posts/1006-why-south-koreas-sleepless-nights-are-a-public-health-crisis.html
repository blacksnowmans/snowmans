Why South Korea's Sleepless Nights Are a Public Health Crisis

October 12, 2026

South Koreans are sleeping less than almost anyone else in the developed world, with chronic anxiety and a hyper-competitive culture fueling a surge in sleep disorders and mental health strain.

    Average sleep time: South Koreans average 6 hours and 50 minutes of sleep per night, well below the OECD average of 8 hours and 22 minutes. The 2 AM wake-up: Psychiatrists note a widespread phenomenon where adults wake up abruptly between 2 AM and 3 AM, gripped by subconscious dread about their careers and futures. Youth mental health: The suicide rate among South Korean teenagers has nearly doubled over the past decade, driven by intense academic pressure. Cultural shifts: Younger generations are increasingly turning to activities like running to disconnect from rigid societal expectations and find individual pacing.

The Anatomy of Exhaustion in a 24/7 Society

Data from local sleep reports highlights how deeply distorted South Korea's rest patterns have become. While 30-somethings log the most sleep, younger generations get the least, and average sleep times dip even lower during the mid-week crunch. The weekend often serves as a desperate attempt to recover from sleep debt rather than a time for genuine leisure, reflecting a lifestyle built on self-exploitation.

Psychiatrists trace this back to a post-pandemic reality where traditional markers of middle-class stability—securing a top university spot, a job at a major conglomerate, or an apartment in Seoul—feel increasingly out of reach. This has bred a form of "survival anxiety" where the brain refuses to switch off, even at night. The cultural pressure to conform to a single definition of success leaves little room for ordinary, unoptimized lives.

High-Functioning Depression and the Cost of Perfection

One of the most alarming trends discussed by mental health professionals is "high-functioning depression," where individuals maintain outward social markers of success—keeping up with work, sleep, and meals—while their inner emotional reserves are entirely depleted. Because South Korean society heavily values endurance and toughness, many push through until physical and mental burnout forces a collapse.

Experts argue that the path forward requires dismantling the rigid "ranking culture" that equates average living with personal failure. As younger Koreans begin to embrace slower, non-competitive pursuits like jogging and redefining personal worth outside of corporate checklists, the conversation around mental health is slowly shifting toward empathy, rest, and the courage to live a normal life.
