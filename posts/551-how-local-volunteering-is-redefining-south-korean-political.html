How Local Volunteering is Redefining South Korean Political Outreach

October 01, 2026

  • Cho Kuk, head of the Innovation Policy Research Institute, has shifted his political engagement from studio broadcasts to grassroots volunteering in Pyeongtaek, South Korea.
    •
  • Cho and other commentators launched a new weekly internet talk show called "Dangsan-dong Cafe," using the platform to discuss the gap between media politics and everyday citizen struggles.
  • In Pyeongtaek—a region known for conservative voter leanings—Cho has engaged in routine community service, including soup kitchen shifts at a senior welfare center and animal shelter work.
  • Initial resistance from local seniors, who openly criticized past voting choices, eventually gave way to warm exchanges after shared meals and direct conversations.

South Korean politics has long suffered from a disconnect between high-profile studio debates in Seoul and the realities of daily life in local neighborhoods. When politicians rely solely on whirlwind campaign stops, interaction with the public is often limited to brief handshakes and photo ops. However, recent grassroots efforts in regions like Pyeongtaek demonstrate a different approach—one where sustained, hands-on community service slowly breaks down political hostility.

The contrast between polished TV commentary and actual fieldwork is stark. While political figures easily lose touch behind desks and strategy meetings, showing up in work clothes to serve meals or clean alleyways forces a return to basic human connection. Even in politically conservative areas, shared meals and eye contact can disarm skepticism faster than any polished policy speech.

From Studio Debates to Soup Kitchens

The launch of the internet talk show "Dangsan-dong Cafe" brought together prominent political commentators, including Cho Kuk, journalist Kim Dae-ho, Kim Seung-hyun, and media company CEO Park Si-young. While the program focuses on current political analysis, its participants have emphasized that politics ultimately belongs in the field, not just in front of studio cameras.

For Cho, this philosophy has translated into a weekly routine in Pyeongtaek involving soup kitchen duty, stray animal care, and neighborhood cleanups. An encounter at the Pyeongtaek Paengseong Senior Welfare Center highlighted this dynamic. Initially met with blunt remarks from elderly residents—some of whom frankly stated they had not voted for him—Cho and the attendees bridged the gap over shared meals. By the end of the visit, the initial political friction had softened into casual conversation and mutual respect.
