The Hidden Crisis: Why Korea’s Local Governments are Bankrupt

September 10, 2026

South Korea’s system of "local autonomy" is facing a structural crisis where regional governments are left with the costs of development and public services, while the central government monopolizes the tax revenue generated by those same regions.

  • The Semiconductor Paradox: Even when massive semiconductor plants (like SK Hynix in Cheongju) are built in a region, the local government receives only about 5% of the resulting tax revenue.
  • The Cost of "Nationalization": When firefighting services were converted to national status to improve standards, the financial burden—roughly $800 million (1.1 trillion KRW) in labor costs alone—was shifted entirely onto local budgets like Gyeonggi Province.
  • Fiscal Strain: Rapid urban development in regions like Gyeonggi creates an immediate, skyrocketing demand for infrastructure and emergency services that local budgets cannot sustain without central intervention.

The Illusion of Local Wealth

In South Korea, it is a common belief that hosting a major industrial complex, such as a semiconductor plant, will turn a province into a fiscal powerhouse. However, the reality is far from this expectation. Because the vast majority of corporate and income taxes are classified as national taxes, local governments like Gyeonggi are left with only a sliver—often as little as 5%—of the total tax generated by these facilities.

This creates a lopsided dynamic: the local government and its residents endure the environmental impacts, traffic congestion, and infrastructure strain caused by the massive industrial activity, while the central government reaps the tax harvest. Local leaders are essentially reduced to begging the central government for handouts to fund basic public works, turning the concept of "local autonomy" into a hollow formality.

"National" Services, Local Bills

The situation is further exacerbated by unfunded mandates from Seoul. A prime example is the conversion of firefighters to national civil servants. While the policy was intended to provide better, standardized protection for citizens, it was implemented without a commensurate shift in funding. For Gyeonggi Province, this change resulted in an annual burden of roughly $800 million in labor costs, plus hundreds of millions more in equipment and vehicle expenses.

When you add the constant need for new fire stations and emergency services in rapidly expanding "New Towns," the math simply stops working. Local governments are expected to manage public safety—a core government duty—while their financial "leash" is held tightly by the central authorities in Seoul.

This is not just a budget issue; it is a fundamental flaw in the Korean administrative structure. By assigning the responsibility for services to local governments without granting them the authority to control their own revenue, the central government is effectively stifling the growth and stability of the regions. Unless there is a massive overhaul of the national-to-local tax distribution ratio, "local autonomy" will continue to be a political slogan rather than a fiscal reality.
