Packing for the Camino de Santiago: A Pragmatic Gear Guide

September 07, 2026

Preparing for an 800-kilometer trek along the Camino de Santiago is a masterclass in downsizing, where the golden rule of pack weight—keeping it under 10% of your body weight—matters far more than brand-name prestige.

    Pack weight is survival: A heavy bag guarantees knee and shoulder strain over long daily distances. A budget-friendly 40-liter pack (like Decathlon’s Quechua, around $75) with a built-in rain cover often outperforms expensive luxury brands because it cuts unnecessary base weight. Leave the laptop behind: Smartphones and durable external storage (like a 1TB portable SSD) handle backups and connectivity without sacrificing precious rest time at pilgrim hostels (*albergues*). Keep clothes minimal and functional: Three sets of synthetic, quick-drying clothing are plenty; avoid cotton entirely to prevent chafing and heavy, sweat-soaked layers. Protect your body: Essential add-ons like trekking poles (with rubber tips for trail manners), cheap knee braces, and earplugs are non-negotiable for surviving 30 days of physical endurance.

The Fallacy of Over-Packing

Anxiety-driven packing is the quickest route to Camino burnout. Many first-timers load up with heavy gear, extra outfits, and bulky electronics "just in case," only to ditch them along the trail or mail them home within the first week. Expensive waterproof shells and designer logos cannot counteract the physical toll of carrying excess bulk across shifting Spanish weather.

Instead of chasing perfection through high-end retail, treat gear as disposable utility. Smart customization—like strapping a water bottle to your shoulder strap for easy access or keeping a dedicated tech pouch for cables—prevents daily annoyances from compounding into serious injuries.

Embracing the Minimalism of the Trail

Ultimately, the Camino de Santiago strips away the urge to accumulate. The gear list is merely a tool to get you moving, but the true weight being left behind is psychological. As daily routines distill down to walking, eating, and sleeping, the obsession with perfect preparation gives way to the simple reality of the road. You do not need the best equipment to finish; you just need enough to keep going.
