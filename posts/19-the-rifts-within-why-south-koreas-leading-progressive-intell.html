The Rifts Within: Why South Korea’s Leading Progressive Intellectual Refused to Meet the President

September 24, 2026

The recent decision by prominent author and political commentator Rhyu Si-min to decline a meeting with President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal opposition—has exposed a deepening crisis of confidence within the progressive camp.

  • What happened: Rhyu, a long-time influential voice on the left, publicly rejected overtures from President Lee’s administration, framing the relationship between power and criticism as one that requires healthy distance.
  • The catalyst: Rhyu reported sensing a collapse in core support for the president as early as September last year, noting that loyalists were beginning to question whether the leader was still delivering on his promises.
  • The political impact: With the president’s approval ratings struggling—notably seeing a double-digit drop among key demographics—the administration is attempting to frame Rhyu as an obstructionist, while Rhyu maintains that he is a critic, not a political operative.

The Anatomy of a Falling Out

Rhyu’s refusal is not a mere personal slight; it is a rejection of the "insider" dynamic that often compromises political criticism. He argues that since December, he has been providing constructive critiques of the president's staff—including ministers and secretaries—only to find the administration incapable of receiving feedback. According to Rhyu, the president consistently misinterpreted calls for reform as personal attacks, a classic hallmark of leadership that has lost touch with its base.

The administration’s focus on the "failed meeting" serves as a convenient distraction. By painting Rhyu as a stubborn intellectual who refuses to engage, the presidential office attempts to shift the blame for its internal failings onto the lack of communication from its critics. For Rhyu, engaging in a private, unofficial dialogue with the president would only undermine his own independence as a public intellectual.

Beyond Blind Allegiance

The core of this conflict lies in the distinction between political support and blind worship. Rhyu has emphasized that his support for the president was contingent on "political efficacy"—the ability to deliver tangible results. When that efficacy is blocked by an administration that isolates itself from reality, the intellectual's responsibility shifts from boosterism to warning.

Recent data from Realmeter—a prominent polling agency—shows that the president has seen a sharp decline in support among his base in the Jeolla region and voters in their 50s. As approval numbers flirt with the 30% range, the risk of a "lame duck" period accelerates. Rhyu’s public warnings were intended to be a defensive barrier against this decline, but by ignoring these signals and attacking the messenger, the administration is accelerating its own isolation.

Ultimately, Rhyu’s stance is a reminder that in a healthy democracy, the role of a critic is not to be a friend to power. By keeping his distance, Rhyu is arguably doing more for the progressive cause than any sycophant could, holding the administration accountable to the voters who feel increasingly disillusioned by the current state of governance.
