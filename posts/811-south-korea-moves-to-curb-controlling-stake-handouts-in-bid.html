South Korea Moves to Curb Controlling Stake Handouts in Bid to Tackle 'Korea Discount'

September 18, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's National Assembly Policy Committee has advanced a mandatory tender offer bill, a key step toward dismantling the long-standing market practice where controlling shareholders pocket massive premiums during M&A deals while leaving retail investors behind.
  • The catch: Rather than requiring a full 100% buyout of remaining shares as seen in Europe, the compromised bill settles on a "50% plus 1 share" rule, protecting only a portion of minority shareholders.
  • Who is affected: Local retail investors remain partially exposed, while small-to-mid-sized private equity funds and corporate acquirers face complex financing hurdles as the capital market attempts to balance fairness with deal-making activity.

Tackling the M&A Loophole That Built the 'Korea Discount'

South Korea’s stock market has long suffered from the "Korea Discount"—a structural phenomenon where listed companies trade below their actual value. While recent revisions to commercial law and fiduciary duty rules have attempted to clean up the market, critics argue that the core driver of this discount remains untouched: the unchecked practice of controlling shareholders monopolizing control premiums during company sales.

Historically, an acquirer could buy a controlling stake (often around 40%) and gain total management rights, leaving minority shareholders with depreciated shares and zero share of the premium. Experts and reform advocates argue that this practice amounts to a legalized form of asset stripping. The newly advanced mandatory tender offer rule aims to force buyers to extend the same purchase terms to a portion of minority holdings, marking a cultural shift toward shareholder equality in Asia's fourth-largest economy.

A Compromised Rule and the Road Ahead

While moving past the committee hurdle is a milestone, the "50%+1 share" compromise has drawn sharp criticism from market watchdogs who wanted a complete 100% buyout requirement. Financial industry insiders worried that a total buyout rule would freeze the M&A market by imposing trillions of won in acquisition costs, potentially crushing smaller private equity players. Yet, reformers counter that diluting the rule risks leaving everyday investors sidelined yet again.

True market reform requires more than half-measures. If South Korea genuinely wants to cast off the Korea Discount and establish a "Korea Premium," policymakers must ensure that future enforcement prioritizes transparency and equal treatment over corporate comfort. Until minority shareholders are fully protected from being treated as bystanders in ownership handovers, the push for market fairness remains an unfinished project.
