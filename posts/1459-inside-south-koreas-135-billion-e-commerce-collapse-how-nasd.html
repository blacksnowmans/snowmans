Inside South Korea’s $1.35 Billion E-Commerce Collapse: How Nasdaq Ambitions Wiped Out 60,000 Merchants

October 26, 2026

South Korea's retail market suffered a catastrophic $1.35 billion (1.8 trillion won) payment freeze after e-commerce operator Qoo10 misallocated seller settlement funds to finance aggressive corporate acquisitions, leaving over 60,000 small merchants facing financial ruin.

  • What happened: Ku Young-bae, CEO of Singapore-based platform Qoo10, used merchant payouts—including $170 million (230 billion won) to acquire global shopping platform Wish—to inflate transaction volumes for a US Nasdaq listing of his logistics arm, Qxpress.
  • The implosion: Platforms Tmon and WeMakePrice froze seller payouts in July 2024. Tmon completed court restructuring in August 2025 with an insulting 0.8% debt recovery rate for creditors, while sister platforms WeMakePrice and Interpark Commerce were declared bankrupt.
  • Regulatory overhaul: Korea enacted strict new legislation cutting platform settlement deadlines from 60-plus days to 20 days and mandating that e-commerce platforms ring-fence at least 50% of vendor funds with third-party financial institutions.
  • Market shift: Devastated vendors and consumers migrated en masse to financially solid giants like Coupang and Naver, consolidating the market around a few dominant players.

Regulatory Loopholes and Corporate Moral Hazard

The tragedy exposed fatal gaps in South Korea’s financial and retail regulations. Unlike traditional financial institutions, general e-commerce platforms were under no legal obligation to keep merchant sales proceeds in separate escrow accounts. Exploiting standard 60-day payout windows, Qoo10 treated vendor revenue as an interest-free line of credit to fund cash-burning M&A deals. When cash ran dry following the Wish acquisition, Tmon resorted to selling pre-paid gift cards at absurd 10% discounts to raise immediate cash to pay off earlier debts, turning the platform into a Ponzi-style rollover scheme.

Devastated Merchants and Market Consolidation

Although Tmon officially exited court receivership after an 18.1 billion won ($13.5 million) buyout by quick-commerce firm Oasis, the outcome offered zero relief to victims. Creditors received back just 8,000 won for every 1 million won owed—a mocking 0.8% recovery rate that legally wiped away corporate liabilities while leaving vendors with unpayable debts. Furthermore, regulatory laxity during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s former administration delayed critical oversight, forcing lawmakers to scramble after the damage was done.

Prosecutors have indicted CEO Ku Young-bae and key executives on fraud and embezzlement charges exceeding 1.85 trillion won. Meanwhile, the crisis has permanently altered South Korea's e-commerce landscape. Trust in mid-tier shopping portals has collapsed, cementing an oligopoly led by deep-pocketed tech giants capable of offering guaranteed liquidity and short settlement windows.
