Why South Korea's Overprotected Youth Are Turning to Buddhism for Escape

October 10, 2026

South Korea's young adults are facing an intense sense of social isolation and widening life gaps driven by relentless social media comparison and hyper-competition.

  • The root cause: Experts argue that decades of overprotective parenting and an exam-obsessed education system have left young people with zero resilience against failure.
  • The coping mechanism: Millennials and Gen Z are increasingly flocking to temples and Buddhist philosophy for mental refuge, turning it into a secular user's guide for modern life.
  • The shift in focus: Discussions on the YouTube show "Intellectuals' Invitation," featuring monk Boman and broadcaster Han Seok-jun, highlight that interpersonal stress—not just money—is the top pain point for young Koreans.

The Trap of Overprotection and the Philosophy of Relationships

In a society where children are shielded from failure for 20 years, entering the real world brings an inevitable shock. Raised under strict perfectionism without ever learning how to bounce back, many young adults develop low self-efficacy and chronic anxiety. When they finally step out on their own, the constant pressure makes them ill-equipped to handle routine setbacks.

Interestingly, this existential dread is driving young Koreans toward Buddhist teachings, transcending religious boundaries. Rather than a dogmatic faith, many view Buddhist concepts as practical mental frameworks to deal with daily suffering. A prime example is the Buddhist concept of wonjeung-hoego—the inevitable pain of having to deal with people one dislikes. Instead of asking how to get rid of difficult people, the philosophy teaches individuals to ask how to keep themselves from suffering in their presence, shifting the focus away from trying to control others.

Changing the Lens of Perception

The dialogue also tackles the fleeting nature of human thought and emotion. Rather than treating passing thoughts as absolute truths, Buddhist teachings frame emotions as transient phenomena that arise and disappear depending on external conditions. By letting go of rigid biases—such as holding onto past mistakes and refusing to allow others room to change—young people can break free from self-imposed mental prisons.
