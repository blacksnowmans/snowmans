Policy Backtracking and Market Jitters Test South Korea’s Economic Roadmap

November 12, 2026

South Korea’s governing Democratic Party of Korea is facing fierce criticism for watering down real estate tax reforms, undermining policy predictability just as domestic markets face global tech volatility and regulatory pressure.

  • What happened: The government under President Lee Jae-myung originally sought to reform the Comprehensive Holding Tax by offering a 1.4 billion won (about $1 million) tax deduction to resident single-home owners while lowering it to 900 million won (about $650,000) for non-resident property owners.
  • What it changes: Responding to pushback from Democratic Party of Korea lawmakers ahead of elections, the party is moving to apply the higher 1.4 billion won deduction across the board, gutting incentives designed to curb speculative "gap investing" in Seoul.
  • Who is affected: Property buyers, financial markets, and Korean tech giants, who are navigating domestic tax shifts alongside international headwinds, including leadership turmoil at Nvidia and capital shifts driven by Anthropic's expected $1.5 trillion Nasdaq debut.

Politics Trumps Principle in Real Estate Tax Reform

The core objective of the Lee Jae-myung administration’s real estate tax proposal was clear: reward actual residents while penalizing speculative non-resident gap investors who buy properties with tenant deposits. By setting a higher tax-exempt threshold of 1.4 billion won for resident single-home owners and lowering it to 900 million won for non-residents, regulators hoped to direct capital away from property speculation and toward productive investments in capital markets. However, political self-preservation has eroded this mandate. As 13 Democratic Party lawmakers representing Seoul districts refused to take a clear stance, the party capitulated, raising non-resident deductions back to 1.2 billion won (about $870,000) and now weighing a flat 1.4 billion won exemption for all single-home owners.

This policy retreat sends a dangerous signal to the market. In prime Seoul districts such as Gangnam and Yongsan, inquiries into gap investing are already picking up again. Equalizing tax treatment regardless of residency rewards speculators and risks locking capital back into real estate rather than fueling equity market growth. When tax rules flip-flop for political expediency, market participants lose faith in government predictability, exacerbating economic instability.

Global Tech Turbulence and Domestic Market Inertia

While property tax debates stall capital reallocation, external shocks add further complexity. Scandals surrounding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang—ranging from alleged technology leaks involving China to executive misconduct—have wiped out roughly $500 billion (1,300 trillion won) in Nvidia’s market value. For South Korean chipmakers like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, stricter U.S. export restrictions triggered by the controversy may offer a temporary breather by slowing down Chinese competitors. Meanwhile, Anthropic’s planned Nasdaq debut at a target valuation of up to $1.5 trillion threatens to draw liquidity away from broader tech equities, highlighting a global shift toward pure-play AI assets.

At home, South Korea’s financial regulators remain surprisingly passive. While private equity deals like TPG’s acquisition of Lotte Rental showed progress by offering a uniform purchase price of 59,000 won per share to all shareholders, official responses to market stress have drawn criticism. The Financial Services Commission, South Korea's chief financial regulator, introduced minor margin-call adjustments that require manual investor requests, leading critics to dismiss the measures as mere window dressing rather than decisive action to defend the KOSPI’s 5,400 level.
