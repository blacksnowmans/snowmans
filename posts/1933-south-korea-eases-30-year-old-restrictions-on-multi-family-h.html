South Korea Eases 30-Year-Old Restrictions on Multi-Family Housing

November 04, 2026

South Korea is set to overhaul its long-standing building regulations for multi-family homes, allowing for increased height and floor space in a push to boost housing supply across the capital region.

  • What happened: Starting November 12, multi-family housing (multi-unit buildings typically owned by a single landlord) will be permitted to rise to four stories, up from the current three-story limit. Maximum floor area restrictions will also expand from 660 square meters to 1,000 square meters.
  • What it changes: These changes, which have been locked for over 30 years, aim to revitalize the "villa" market, which has struggled with low supply and a damaged reputation following recent rental fraud scandals.
  • Who is affected: Developers, landlords, and young tenants who rely on these smaller housing units as a stepping stone to homeownership in high-cost urban centers like Seoul.

A Shift Toward Practical Supply

The move is a clear attempt by the administration of President Lee Jae-myung (of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea) to pivot away from what critics call "empty political promises" toward tangible, small-scale housing supply. While large-scale apartment complexes often dominate the headlines, the government is now attempting to address chronic housing shortages by incentivizing smaller, more flexible builds.

However, industry experts are skeptical about the immediate impact. Soaring construction costs mean that simply granting developers permission to build higher or larger does not automatically guarantee projects will become financially viable. Critics argue that unless the government addresses the underlying economic burden on builders, these deregulatory measures may remain largely symbolic.

The Dilemma of Speed and Infrastructure

The push for rapid housing supply—specifically the administration’s plan to build 80,000 new homes in the capital region—is already meeting resistance from local residents. In areas like Gimpo, community groups and local officials are pushing back, arguing that adding high-density housing without first securing transportation infrastructure and local jobs will only exacerbate existing "commuter hell."

The opposition party, the People Power Party (the main conservative bloc), has critiqued the government’s approach, calling for a focus on policies that allow young people to actually own their homes rather than just renting. Yet, this rhetoric feels ironic to many, given that the housing market saw extreme volatility and excessive regulation during the term of the now-imprisoned former leader, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Whether this current policy shift can survive the friction of local protests and the reality of high construction costs remains the central challenge for the Lee administration.
