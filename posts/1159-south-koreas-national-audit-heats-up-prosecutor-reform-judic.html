South Korea’s National Audit Heats Up: Prosecutor Reform, Judiciary Pressure, and Nuclear Debates

October 15, 2026

South Korea's annual parliamentary audit entered its second week with high-stakes clashes over sweeping state reforms, brewing tensions between the liberal government and the judiciary, and heated debates over foreign policy.

  • Prosecutorial Reform: Kim Ji-yong, nominee to head the newly proposed Major Crime Investigation Agency, launched a sharp attack during his confirmation hearing, labeling imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol South Korea's "first political prosecutor."
  • Judiciary Under Fire: Ruling party lawmakers escalated pressure on Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, floating impeachment threats and demanding his resignation over stalled rulings.
  • Foreign Policy Friction: The opposition raised alarms over a $116 billion (162조 원) U.S.-South Korea nuclear energy framework, criticizing it as a lopsided deal that benefits American firms.
  • Administrative Relocation: Discussions gained momentum to move the Ministries of Foreign Affairs and National Defense to Sejong, advancing plans to build out the country's administrative capital.

Tackling the Legacy of Political Prosecutors

The confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong set the tone for this week's parliamentary audits. By directly targeting the politicized investigations of the past under the previous administration, the nominee signaled that the newly proposed Major Crime Investigation Agency aims to dismantle the prosecution's traditional monopoly on investigative powers. While the ruling bloc views this as a vital step toward institutional neutrality, critics and opposition lawmakers grilled Kim over insufficient document submissions and questioned how the agency will maintain true independence from the Ministry of the Interior and Safety.

At the same time, the broader political arena is seeing an intense standoff with the judiciary. Ruling party heavyweights have issued severe warnings to Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, signaling that the liberal bloc's patience with delayed legal rulings and perceived foot-dragging on constitutional matters has reached its limit. While the opposition warns against crossing institutional lines, the relentless pressure underscores a pervasive sentiment within the current administration that systemic judicial reform is long overdue.

Foreign Policy and the Battle for Livelihood Issues

Beyond institutional reform, foreign and security policies drew sharp scrutiny. The debate over the massive U.S.-South Korea nuclear framework highlighted a growing unease among smaller progressive parties, who argue that South Korea risks financing a venture where American companies reap the core profits and technological benefits. In the National Defense Committee, bipartisan friction also fractured a resolution condemning North Korean landmine provocations, exposing deep divisions over how the military handles security threats.

Yet amidst the grand political dogfights, local livelihoods remained a flashpoint. Lawmakers clashed over livelihood audits—ranging from the National Pension Service's asset rebalancing rules to the suspension of safety equipment programs for farmers following a series of fatal workplace accidents. As the audit continues, the real test for the current administration will be whether these high-profile investigations translate into tangible protections for everyday citizens rather than mere political leverage.
