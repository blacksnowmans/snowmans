Why Market Panic Over AI Infrastructure is Exaggerated

November 06, 2026

Extreme swings in global semiconductor and artificial intelligence stocks are driven by speculative leverage unwinding and political anxiety rather than a breakdown in corporate fundamentals, according to Lee Sun-yeop, CEO of Seoul-based investment consultancy AFW Partners.

  • What happened: Global tech shares plunged overnight following heavy hedge fund unwinding and fears that U.S. state governments might restrict power-hungry AI data centers.
  • What it changes: Tech giants face higher compliance costs and conditional permits, but the corporate arms race for AI dominance continues unabated.
  • Who is affected: Major memory suppliers—including South Korean giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—remain well-positioned as hyperscalers struggle to secure enough chips.
  • The Korean angle: South Korea's equity market suffered disproportionate losses, exposing structural vulnerabilities to foreign high-frequency trading during global sell-offs.

Political Fears vs. Corporate Reality

Market anxiety recently escalated over threats that U.S. state governors might block data center construction due to soaring local electricity consumption. However, Lee emphasizes that governors cannot act in a vacuum. Unilaterally halting mega-projects requires approval from state legislatures, and key committees in states like Texas are dominated by pro-business Republicans who view job creation as a priority. While local authorities may implement conditional permits requiring tech firms to fund independent power sources—a compromise similar to policy proposals seen during Mr. Yoon Seok-yeol’s term—big tech companies have the capital to absorb these costs or shift investments elsewhere.

Korea's Structural Bug and the Investor Playbook

The recent market action also exposed a glaring vulnerability in South Korea's financial system. When Wall Street stumbles, foreign high-frequency trading (HFT) algorithms frequently turn the Korean market into easy target practice, dragging down defensive domestic stocks alongside tech exporters. Local regulators face growing pressure to normalize market functions to shield investors from predatory trading.

For investors, Lee warns against impulse selling or chasing high-risk three-times leveraged ETFs during volatile swings. Major memory chipmakers—including Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, and U.S. rival Micron—report that hyperscalers have secured less than half of the memory supplies they need. With long-term demand far outstripping supply and tech giants competing for survival, short-term political noise and leverage shakeouts should be seen as market friction rather than a structural collapse.
