Brazil’s Election Lesson: Why Incremental Reform Empowers the Far Right

October 13, 2026

  • Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva issued a candid post-election confession, warning that incremental policies under neoliberalism allowed inequality to fester and opened the door for far-right populism.
    •
  • The Brazilian leader argued that progressive and left-leaning governments often fail because they focus on managing the excesses of the market rather than dismantling structural inequalities, creating a vacuum that the far-right exploits.
  • For South Korea—operating under the liberal administration of President Lee Jae-myung after the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol—the warning hits close to home: half-hearted reforms and appeasement of conservative elites risk alienating the core base.

When Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva took to social media after a polarized election, he offered a brutally honest self-assessment rather than a standard victory speech. Instead of simply blaming a resurgent far-right, Lula asked a harder question: How much of their rise was fueled by the left's own incompetence and failure to address the daily struggles of ordinary people? Lula noted that while neoliberal economic projects promised broad prosperity, they actually widened inequality, left the vulnerable behind, and allowed right-wing agitators to posture as anti-establishment champions by weaponizing public frustration against marginalized groups.

The parallels to South Korea's current political landscape are stark. The progressive bloc, anchored by the ruling Democratic Party, has frequently found itself walking on eggshells, eager to appease conservative media and market elites rather than pushing through the structural overhauls voters demanded. From housing and finance to the unfinished work of prosecuting and reforming powerful institutions like the prosecution service, the temptation to compromise has blunted the edge of reform. When a government elected on a mandate for change spends its time worrying about the sensibilities of the establishment, its core supporters feel betrayed, and public trust begins to crumble.

Avoiding the Trap of Cowardly Compromise

Lula’s warning is that when progressive leaders govern out of fear—worrying about critical headlines, market reactions, or conservative backlash—they lose their political soul. In South Korea, following the unprecedented turmoil that saw Yoon Suk-yeol impeached and imprisoned, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces a similar litmus test. If the ruling party hesitates to challenge entrenched privilege out of political expediency, it risks repeating the mistakes of leaders who traded bold reform for quiet compliance.

History suggests that the far-right does not grow because of its own brilliance, but because the governing center-left leaves a void. South Korea’s reformers must decide whether they will stand firm on the side of struggling working-class citizens and young people crushed by unfair economic structures, or whether they will retreat into cautious, technocratic management that pleases nobody. As Brazil's sobering political reality demonstrates, the moment a reformist government starts pulling its punches to appease its opponents, it has already begun to sign its own political death warrant.
