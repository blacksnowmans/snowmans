South Korea’s Judiciary Rocked by Scandals Over Luxury Drinking Sessions and Administrative Overreach

September 07, 2026

  • Senior Judge Ji Ki-yeon and two attorneys reportedly racked up a 4.09 million won (about $3,000) tab at an exclusive hostess bar in Seoul, triggering anti-graft law investigations.
  • Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae faces intense backlash for allegedly bypassing the judicial recommendation committee and unilaterally rewriting candidate lists for Supreme Court appointments.
  • Critics argue that internal probes and selective warrant issuance are shielding judges from accountability, severely eroding public trust in South Korea’s legal system.
  • The controversies have sparked renewed calls for independent, external oversight bodies to investigate judicial misconduct rather than relying on closed internal reviews.

South Korea’s judiciary is facing a profound crisis of credibility as two explosive scandals lay bare the insular nature of the country's legal establishment. While the judiciary is constitutionally designed to be a bulwark of the rule of law and a check on political power, recent events suggest it has increasingly calcified into an untouchable corporate cartel. From luxury entertainment sessions enjoyed by sitting judges to heavy-handed administrative appointments from the top, the system appears more focused on self-preservation than upholding public standards.

At the heart of the outcry is a luxury drinking session involving Senior Judge Ji Ki-yeon, who allegedly ran up a tab worth about $3,000 with two lawyers at a reservations-only establishment in Seoul's upscale Cheongdam-dong neighborhood. With the per-person cost far exceeding legal limits under South Korea's strict anti-graft legislation, the incident has laid bare how legal professionals sometimes navigate around the law they are sworn to enforce. Compounding the public outrage are reports of frequent smartphone replacements by the judge around key investigative timelines—moves widely interpreted as clumsy attempts to destroy digital evidence. When anti-corruption and internal investigative bodies respond with lax oversight and narrow search warrants, it reinforces the damaging public perception that judges live above the law.

Judicial Independence or Institutional Impunity?

Compounding these ethical breaches is a fierce institutional battle at the apex of South Korea's court system. Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has drawn sharp criticism for his handling of judicial appointments, specifically his attempts to sideline advisory recommendation committees and fill vacancies with figures aligned with his own preferences. Critics argue that treating the Supreme Court bench as a personal fiefdom perverts the constitutional doctrine of judicial independence, twisting it from "freedom from political interference" into "insulation from public accountability."

Constitutional protections for the judiciary were never meant to shield judges from basic ethical scrutiny or democratic procedures. When top legal officials weaponize administrative powers and internal disciplinary bodies default to a "protect our own" mentality, the system stops functioning as a pillar of justice and starts acting as a closed sanctuary. For a younger generation of South Koreans already grappling with intense anxieties over systemic fairness, watching elite jurists bend anti-graft rules and dodge digital forensics only deepens a pervasive sense of alienation. Without rigorous external oversight and a complete overhaul of how judicial misconduct is handled, South Korea's courts risk losing the one asset they cannot function without: public trust.
