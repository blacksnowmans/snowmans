South Korea's Ruling Party is Imploding Under the Guise of "Smart Dictatorship"

October 21, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party, fresh off securing the presidency with liberal leader Lee Jae-myung, is plunging into a severe internal civil war sparked by party leader Kim Min-seok's heavy-handed leadership style and the systematic exclusion of dissenting factions.

  • What happened: Party leader Kim Min-seok unilaterally appointed top supreme council members without consulting party factions, drawing fierce backlash from pro-Chung Cheong-rae lawmakers and widening an internal rift.
  • What it changes: The party's dynamic, open debate culture has been replaced by a stifling atmosphere of surveillance and fear of disciplinary action, alienating both core supporters and moderate swing voters.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung's administration, which is losing critical legislative backing as the ruling party remains bogged down in infighting rather than defending the reform agenda against aggressive prosecutors and conservative opposition attacks.

The Anatomy of an Internal Crackdown

A climate of intense silence has settled over the Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party. According to reports from the Seoul Shinmun, senior lawmakers describe a party where speaking out openly risks triggering internal audits or disciplinary warnings. Rather than functioning as a diverse coalition that successfully expanded its base to win the presidency, the party leadership is increasingly criticized for running a "smart dictatorship"—a system where democratic procedures are hollowed out through technological control and manufactured consensus while real debate is silenced.

This crackdown has fueled nostalgia for former party leader Chung Cheong-rae, whose flexible leadership style welcomed multiple viewpoints and direct communication with party members. By contrast, current leader Kim Min-seok's top-down appointments of Supreme Council members—pushed through without consulting key factions—violated party rules and shattered the fragile trust within the leadership. This heavy-handed approach has exposed a dangerous disconnect between the party elites and their core base.

Alienating the Base and the Peril of Losing "Loyal Hounds"

As Chung Cheong-rae famously warned, a political party cannot hope to win presidential elections if it alienates its core base—the dedicated volunteers and activists who act as the party's shield during crises. Yet, the leadership's authoritarian posture has left these core supporters feeling betrayed, marginalized, and branded as "extremist fandoms" whenever they voice legitimate grievances. Compounding this, the party's massive structural majority in the National Assembly has failed to deliver the tangible political efficacy or judicial and media reforms voters demanded, breeding widespread political apathy.

Outside pressures are mounting in tandem with this internal paralysis, including aggressive media crackdowns and reviving legacy investigations designed to hobble the progressive bloc. While the ruling party squabbles over internal appointments and enforces blind unity, it risks walking straight into the opposition's trap. Unless the leadership drops its coercive tactics, restores internal democracy, and reconnects with the base that brought it to power, the Democratic Party's structural majority will matter little against self-inflicted collapse.
