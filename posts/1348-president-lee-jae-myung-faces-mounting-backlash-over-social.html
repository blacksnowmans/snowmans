President Lee Jae-myung Faces Mounting Backlash Over Social Media Governance

October 20, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s insistence on using personal social media as a primary governing tool is deepening factional divides and drawing public pushback over his administration's policy priorities.

  • Voters push back: A poll by the Korea Society Opinion Institute (KSOI) found that 46.8% of South Koreans oppose President Lee’s continuous direct social media communication, compared to 37.6% who support it.
  • Deepening factional rifts: The president’s frequent posts on platforms like X (formerly Twitter) have amplified internal clashes within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, accelerating the purge of traditional loyalists tied to former President Moon Jae-in.
  • Scrutiny on oversight: Critics accuse the administration of using double standards after the newly formed Major Crimes Investigation Agency launched an aggressive digital forensic search of the work PC belonging to Im Eun-jeong, a prominent internal reform advocate, over her public criticism of agency leadership.
  • Regulatory fallouts: The political friction coincides with financial controversies, including the Financial Supervisory Service's rushed 29-day approval of single-stock leveraged exchange-traded products—well below the typical 62-day average—which resulted in steep losses for everyday retail investors.

Direct Outreach or Factional Echo Chamber?

During a recent press briefing, President Lee defended his online posture, describing social media as an indispensable "lifeline" and "breathing room" without which his political career would not have survived. Presidential aides have pointed to figures like Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum to justify this direct, unmediated communication style. However, critics argue that governing from a smartphone carries very different stakes than grassroots campaigning. Instead of cultivating national unity, Lee's posts frequently take aim at sensitive institutional flashpoints—such as prosecution reform and ruling-party management—raising concerns even among progressive commentators that the messaging serves only to rally hardline loyalists.

Policy Distractions and Institutional Pressure

The controversy around the president's online rhetoric highlights broader administrative strains. While the administration pledges sweeping judicial and financial reforms, actions on the ground have invited harsh scrutiny. The aggressive internal inspection of Im Eun-jeong for questioning agency heads contrasts sharply with the slow progress on high-profile political investigations. Simultaneously, regulatory admissions that market safeguards were fast-tracked ahead of elections have compounded voter skepticism. By prioritizing real-time political disputes over institutional consistency, the administration risks exhausting public patience at a time when pressing economic and cost-of-living challenges demand focused governance.
