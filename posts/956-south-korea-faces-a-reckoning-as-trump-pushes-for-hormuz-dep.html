South Korea Faces a Reckoning as Trump Pushes for Hormuz Deployment

September 22, 2026

  • The demand: The US is pressuring South Korea to send warships to the Strait of Hormuz to protect commercial shipping, tying the request to broader trade and tariff leverage.
  • The stakes: Over 70% of South Korea’s crude oil imports pass through the Hormuz chokepoint, making the issue a critical vulnerability for the energy-dependent economy.
  • The domestic divide: While the government attempts a defense of "commercial rationality," critics argue that pro-US national security advisors are needlessly caving to Washington's aggressive demands.
  • The geopolitical shift: The traditional U.S.-led "petrodollar" security umbrella is fracturing as Middle Eastern allies like Saudi Arabia hedge their bets toward China.

South Korea’s foreign policy is being pushed to the breaking point by the return of Donald Trump’s aggressive transactional diplomacy. Washington is using every tool at its disposal—tariffs, investment demands, and security pressures—to force Seoul into a combat-free deployment in the Middle East. For an economy that relies on the Strait of Hormuz for the vast majority of its oil, this is not a routine alliance request; it is an existential squeeze.

Yet the country’s security establishment remains trapped in a Cold War mindset. Terrified of upsetting Washington, officials continue to entertain astronomical financial demands and infrastructure investments that offer zero economic return. Critics argue that instead of acting as a junior partner willing to serve as a military placeholder, Seoul should leverage its indispensable role in global supply chains—from semiconductors to batteries—and push back against these lopsided terms.

The Petro-Dollar Crack and the Need for an Independent Foreign Policy

The pressure over Hormuz arrives as the global order undergoes a profound restructuring. The decades-old "petrodollar" system—whereby the US guaranteed regional security in exchange for stable oil supplies—is fraying as Middle Eastern nations realize American security guarantees have limits. Saudi Arabia's growing pivot toward Beijing highlights a fast-changing landscape where blind adherence to old alliances no longer guarantees stability.

Rather than clinging to outdated dogmas, analysts argue that South Korea must diversify its diplomatic horizons, prioritize economic sovereignty, and rethink its approach to regional peace. Blindly following Washington into volatile foreign flashpoints risks alienating critical trade partners and trapping the nation in conflicts where it has no business fighting.
