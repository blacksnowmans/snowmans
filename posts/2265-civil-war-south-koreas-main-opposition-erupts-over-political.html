Civil War: South Korea’s Main Opposition Erupts Over ‘Political Purges’ and Failed Leadership

November 12, 2026

The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has plunged into an internal power struggle as senior leaders trade accusations of "political purges" and fabricated disciplinary records ahead of the general elections.

  • Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul and a key conservative heavyweight, has demanded a transition to an emergency leadership committee, citing the party's collapse in support within the capital region.
  • Secretary General Jang Dong-hyeok has reacted with open hostility to the Mayor's demands, sparking a public spat that critics say exposes a total vacuum of leadership within the party.
  • Explosive allegations have surfaced claiming that past disciplinary actions against Han Dong-hoon, a prominent conservative figure, were intentionally "manufactured" by party insiders to facilitate a political purge.
  • Internal distrust is deepening over irregularities in the primary process for the Yongin-gap district, leading to accusations that the leadership is manipulating candidate selections for factional gain.

A Party at War With Itself

The friction between Mayor Oh Se-hoon and Secretary General Jang Dong-hyeok has moved beyond mere policy disagreement into an open battle for the party's future. Oh, who is highly sensitive to the PPP’s dismal polling in the Seoul metropolitan area, argues that the current leadership lacks the strategy to prevent a total electoral rout. Jang’s emotional retort—suggesting he and the Mayor "swap jobs"—highlights a leadership that is increasingly defensive and seemingly unable to maintain institutional discipline. This infighting is not just about personalities; it reflects a deep-seated fear that the party is currently a sinking ship.

The situation is further complicated by the "Han Dong-hoon scandal." Claims that Han’s previous disciplinary record was falsified to sideline him suggest that the PPP’s internal systems have been weaponized for factional warfare. Han has characterized these actions as a "political purge," a sentiment echoed by influential conservative commentators like Cho Kab-je. When a party's internal disciplinary and primary systems are viewed as tools for "fixing" outcomes rather than ensuring fairness, the party loses its legitimacy not just with its members, but with the broader electorate.

Policy Attacks and the Shadow of the Far-Right

In an attempt to pivot back to a national agenda, PPP leaders like Representative Jeong Jeom-sig have launched attacks on the real estate policies of the Lee Jae-myung administration, the current government led by the Democratic Party of Korea. The opposition claims the government is "acting" for the sake of the election rather than solving the housing crisis. However, these attacks are struggling to gain traction. With the PPP consumed by its own internal "civil war," its attempts to critique the government appear more like a distraction than a coherent policy alternative.

The party also faces a crisis of identity as it leans on far-right rhetoric and street protests to maintain its base. By allowing external figures like Cho Kab-je to dictate who should be removed from leadership, the PPP risks alienating the centrist voters it desperately needs to win. This cycle of internal sabotage—which traces back to the fractures seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office before his impeachment—suggests that the conservative movement has yet to find a stable path forward in the post-Yoon era. Without a transparent investigation into the "manufactured" disciplinary records and a move toward a more inclusive leadership, the PPP’s talk of "conservative rebuilding" remains a distant prospect.
