South Korea’s Legal Reform Battle Hits a Constitution-Sized Wall

November 03, 2026

South Korea’s drive to curb abuses of power within its justice system faces a structural bottleneck in the constitution, even as political polarization and prosecutorial misconduct continue to test the nation's democratic guardrails.

  • Prosecutorial overreach exposed: Investigative journalist Jang In-su and former police superintendent Lee Ji-eun pointed out that prosecutorial evidence fabrication harms ordinary citizens alongside high-profile figures, citing an instance where prosecutors rejected police warrant requests for Presidential Security Service Deputy Chief Kim Sung-hun three times.
  • Constitutional overhaul demanded: Legal reformers argue for revoking prosecutors' exclusive constitutional right to request search and arrest warrants, abolishing legacy prosecutorial titles, and transitioning to a dedicated public indictment system.
  • Surge in youth far-right sentiment: Sociological data shows 25.9% of South Korean men in their 20s hold far-right leanings—though less than 10% self-identify as such—driven by social anxiety and gender-based grievance politics exploited by political elites.
  • Media oversight under President Lee: Unlike the insular media echo chamber during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration, President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea has been urged to remain open to critical journalists, despite hostility from partisan online factions.

The Bottleneck in Korea’s Judicial Power

For decades, South Korean prosecutors have wielded extraordinary authority over both investigations and indictments. The core barrier to fundamental reform lies in the South Korean Constitution itself, which grants prosecutors exclusive authority to request search and arrest warrants. As demonstrated by prosecutors repeatedly blocking police warrant applications targeting connected figures like Presidential Security Service Deputy Chief Kim Sung-hun, this monopoly transforms the warrant process into a protective shield for elites rather than a safeguard for civil rights.

To dismantle this system, legal reform advocates call for amending the constitution to remove prosecutorial warrant exclusivity. Stripping away the legacy status of "prosecutor" and restructuring the institution into a public indictment office staffed by indictment officers represents the necessary final step. Without strictly separating investigative duties from prosecution, structural abuses will continue to target vulnerable citizens outside the public eye.

Rising Polarization and Grassroots Vigilance

Beyond courtroom battles, South Korea faces deepening social divisions. Sociological surveys indicate that 25.9% of men in their 20s exhibit far-right leanings, exposing a stark gap between underlying bias and self-perception. Conservative political figures from the main opposition People Power Party, including Na Kyung-won and Lee Jin-sook, have targeted these demographics by amplifying gender rivalries and nativist rhetoric amid slowing economic mobility.

Countering state overreach and toxic partisanship requires strong grassroots engagement. Within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, veteran party members and independent media act as vital checks against questionable candidate nominations at the local level. As the country moves forward under President Lee Jae-myung, prioritizing constitutional principles over factional loyalty—and ensuring the administration welcomes constructive criticism—remains essential for South Korea’s democratic health.
