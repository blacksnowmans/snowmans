Why Warren Buffett Reads the Same Book Twice: A Neuroscience Perspective

September 30, 2026

  • Reading a single book three times is scientifically more effective for long-term memory than reading ten different books once.
  • The brain treats non-repeated information as "spam" to be deleted; deep learning only occurs when the brain is forced to retrieve information.
  • The "three-read" method—skimming, active recall, and internalizing—transforms passive consumption into a tool for real-world application.
  • This approach is particularly effective for high-impact books, helping readers avoid the "illusion of competence" common in rapid, single-pass reading.

The Brain’s Survival Filter

Ever finish a book, feel inspired, and then realize a week later that you can’t recall a single core concept? This isn't a failure of your intelligence; it is a feature of your brain. From a neuroscientific standpoint, the brain is an energy-intensive organ designed to filter out information not deemed critical for survival. According to the "forgetting curve" established by German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, we lose more than half of what we learn within 24 hours unless it is reinforced.

Most readers fall into the trap of the "illusion of competence." When you read a text, it feels familiar, so your brain assumes you have mastered it. However, this is akin to recognizing a song’s melody but being unable to sing the lyrics. True memory and understanding are formed not when you scan the page, but when your brain struggles to retrieve information. To make a book "stick," you must trick your brain into thinking the information is vital for survival—which is done through repetition and active recall.

The 3-Step "Mastery" Reading Method

To move from passive consumption to internalizing knowledge, you should adopt a structured approach rather than aimless binge-reading. This is the strategy used by high-achievers like Warren Buffett:

  • Step 1 (The Map): Read the book once quickly, without underlining or note-taking. The goal is simply to understand the overall landscape of the author’s argument. Wait at least a week before starting the next phase to allow your brain to process the information.
  • Step 2 (The Excavation): Re-read the book with a pencil in hand. Focus only on the most critical passages. Crucially, spend five minutes before you open the book trying to recall its main points. This "retrieval" process is where the actual neurobiological change happens.
  • Step 3 (The Application): On the third read, identify one single, transformative idea or sentence that you can apply to your own life. Write it down. When you translate abstract text into a personal, physical experience, it moves from "dead information" to a usable skill.

You do not need to read every book this way. Select only the few volumes that stop you in your tracks or change your perspective. In an age of digital distraction, slowing down to digest one book deeply is far more efficient for career and personal growth than skimming a dozen titles you will inevitably forget.
