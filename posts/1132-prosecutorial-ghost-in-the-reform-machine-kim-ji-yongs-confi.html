Prosecutorial Ghost in the Reform Machine: Kim Ji-yong’s Confirmation Hearing Stumbles

October 14, 2026

South Korea’s landmark push to overhaul its justice system ran into a glaring paradox on October 14, 2026, as Kim Ji-yong, the nominee to lead the newly created Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA), struggled to defend his anti-reform past during a contentious parliamentary confirmation hearing.

  • The Hearing: Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), led by Representative Youn Kun-young, aggressively questioned Kim over his sudden conversion into a judicial reformer after decades spent defending prosecutorial turf.
  • The Controversies: Kim faced intense scrutiny for convenient memory lapses regarding past high-profile decisions, including his approval of charges shielding conservative figure Han Dong-hoon and the selective prosecution of former presidential secretary Lee Kwang-chul.
  • What It Changes: The MCIA is the centerpiece of the Lee Jae-myung administration’s drive to end the prosecution service’s 70-year monopoly on both investigating and indicting crimes, meaning the agency's inaugural chief will set the tone for post-reform law enforcement.
  • Who Is Affected: The Korean judiciary and the general public, who risk seeing a hard-won structural overhaul compromised by installing an insider shaped by the very prosecutorial culture the agency was built to dismantle.

Selective Memory and Institutional Reflexes

Under sharp cross-examination, Kim insisted he fully supports separating investigation from indictment. Yet his career record suggests the opposite. While serving as a senior prosecutor at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, Kim organized press conferences to fight investigative realignment and joined a collective statement in November 2020 defending Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol against disciplinary action by the Ministry of Justice.

That track record collided with reality during the hearing. When pressed by DPK lawmaker Youn Kun-young on whether he had approved controversial indictments during his time in the appellate leadership, Kim initially claimed he could not remember, before backtracking to admit it was probable. His defense—that he had "internally disagreed" with politically driven cases while never lodging a formal objection on paper—drew open ridicule. It exposed the self-preserving opportunism typical of elite prosecutors who bend to power rather than upholding the law.

Can the Old Guard Lead the New Order?

The creation of the MCIA was intended to be an institutional clean break, ending decades of politicized justice by stripping prosecutors of primary investigative authority. However, appointing a former prosecutor who resisted those very changes raises serious doubts about whether the new agency can establish genuine independence or merely provide a new home for old habits. Kim’s simmering friction with internal whistleblowers like prosecutor Lim Eun-jung further fuels concern that he prioritizes organizational hierarchy over accountability.

For the Lee Jae-myung administration, advancing a nominee who retreats into bureaucratic evasiveness risks squandering the moral capital behind its judicial reforms. Real reform cannot simply be a matter of changing stationery and agency signs. If the leadership of the MCIA remains trapped in the old prosecutorial mindset, the system will have changed its name without changing its character.
