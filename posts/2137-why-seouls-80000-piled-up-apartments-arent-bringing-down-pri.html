Why Seoul's 80,000 Piled-Up Apartments Aren't Bringing Down Prices

November 09, 2026

Seoul apartment listings have surged by 33 percent over the past three months to surpass 80,000 units, yet property prices continue to defy gravity amid a tense standoff between stubborn sellers and hesitant buyers.

  • Inventory surge: Active apartment listings in Seoul jumped 33 percent following the government’s August 3 tax reform measures, crossing the 80,000 threshold as multi-home owners weighed their tax burdens.
  • The transaction cliff: Rather than triggering competitive price cuts, the influx has stalled the market into an impasse—sellers refuse to lower asking prices, while prospective buyers wait on the sidelines for prices to drop.
  • Diverging districts: While Seoul's ultra-wealthy "three Gangnam districts" (Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa) have begun recording select discounted trades, outer districts and mid-tier complexes are holding firm or notching new highs.
  • The spring reckoning: The market faces a make-or-break test ahead of the June 1 Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax assessment deadline, which will determine whether sellers capitulate with fire sales or pull properties off the market entirely.

The Illusion of a Buyer's Market

In standard market economics, a 33 percent surge in supply over a single quarter would trigger an immediate slide in prices. In Seoul, however, the sheer volume of listings conceals a glaring mismatch in price expectations. The vast majority of properties hitting portals are not distressed sales; they are listed with ambitious, wait-and-see asking prices. While headline price growth has decelerated—slowing from weekly gains of 0.1 percent to 0.03 percent—slower growth is not a decline. For most of the capital, prices are still inching upward week after week.

The policy puzzle facing President Lee Jae-myung's administration is that regulatory nudges have multiplied listings without breaking the psychology of property owners. Following years of policy whiplash during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, sellers remain confident that Seoul real estate will outlast short-term policy pressures. Without an urgent fear of steep capital losses, multi-home owners are choosing to hold out rather than negotiate.

Spring Tax Deadlines and the 2021 Playbook

The real inflection point will arrive in early 2025. South Korea's Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (jongbuse)—a national property tax levied on owners of high-value real estate or multiple homes—uses June 1 as its annual ownership benchmark. If adjusted official home values and tax rates threaten owners with tens of millions of won (tens of thousands of dollars) in holding costs, the current bluffing game could break, turning stubborn listings into genuine fire sales between February and May.

Yet analysts warn against assuming an inevitable market crash, pointing to the lessons of 2021. During that cycle, tight regulations also caused a sudden flood of listings, only for inventory to evaporate almost overnight when owners chose to transfer titles to family members as gifts or switch to rentals instead of selling at a discount. If borrowing costs ease or regulatory pressure wavers before spring, Seoul's 80,000 listings could vanish just as quickly, leaving would-be buyers stranded once again.
