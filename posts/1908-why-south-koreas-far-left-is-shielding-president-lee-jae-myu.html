Why South Korea’s Far-Left Is Shielding President Lee Jae-myung

November 03, 2026

South Korea’s hardline nationalist left has launched a concerted effort to defend President Lee Jae-myung against falling approval ratings, using tailored polling data to dismiss internal liberal criticism as minor background noise.

  • What happened: The Jinbo Policy Institute, a think tank affiliated with the minor far-left Progressive Party, released a survey by polling firm STI showing President Lee’s approval at 37%, arguing that only 3.5% of core progressive voters have actually abandoned him.
  • What it changes: The report attributes approval drops primarily to soft centrist and conservative voters leaving over foreign policy, framing internal liberal critiques as mere factional noise and urging the administration to double down on hardline leftist stances.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, and moderate voters who risk being alienated if the government retreats into an ideological echo chamber.

Spinning Polling Data to Silence Party Critics

The Progressive Party—and specifically its dominant Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance faction, which traces its roots to the anti-U.S. National Liberation (NL) student movements of the 1990s—has historically been deeply hostile to mainstream liberal governments. Far-left activists within this sphere routinely lambasted former liberal administrations over trade and foreign policy. Yet, as President Lee Jae-myung faces a slump in public support (37.0% positive against 59.8% negative in their cited data), this faction has emerged as an aggressive shield for the president.

By claiming that core supporters remain steadfast while blaming voter attrition on soft centrists who reject hardline security policies, the Progressive Party is offering the ruling bloc a convenient defense. The STI poll specifically included questions on restoring inter-Korean dialogue and accelerating the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON) from the United States—classic NL nationalist priorities that lie far from the public's immediate concerns over real estate and economic stability. By using these items to define what constitutes a "true" supporter, the survey attempts to delegitimize reasonable internal criticism as statistical noise from just 3.5% of the electorate.

A Risky Ideological Trap for the Ruling Bloc

This unusual protectionism stems from deep political ties in Seongnam, the satellite city south of Seoul where President Lee built his early political career alongside local activist networks. For the NL faction, President Lee serves as a crucial bridge to pull mainstream government policies toward their ideological goals. However, dismissing minor drops in core support as irrelevant ignores how thin election margins actually are in Korean politics.

Rather than addressing governance stumbles, economic distress, or communication breakdowns that alienate moderate voters, this factional defense encourages the administration to isolate itself. By wrapping President Lee in a protective bubble of hardline rhetoric, the far-left risks trapping the ruling party in a narrow ideological fortress at the expense of broader national unity.
