Why South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Is Facing a Backlash From His Own Base

October 12, 2026

South Korea’s progressive government is facing a deep political crisis not from the conservative opposition, but from the core activist base that brought President Lee Jae-myung to power, as critics accuse his administration of abandoning structural reform in favor of technocratic compromise.

  • Fading Reform:
    •  Critics, including prominent commentators, argue that President Lee Jae-myung has fallen into "reformism without reform"—prioritizing short-term approval ratings and economic efficiency over sweeping institutional overhauls.
  • The "Pro-Lee vs. Anti-Lee" Trap:
    •  Internal ruling party divisions over who is loyal to the president are being dismissed by core activists as elitist politics that ignore the decentralized, grassroots nature of the movement.
  • Foreign Policy Flashpoints:
    •  Over 550 civil society groups have mobilized against potential foreign troop deployments, warning that cozying up to Washington at the expense of core principles is alienating the government's base.
  • A New Era of Politics:
    •  Voters who backed Lee's rise expect systemic normalization—including the personnel purging of entrenched elites—rather than traditional boss-follower loyalty.

The Illusion of Efficiency and the Demand for Personnel Purges

When imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol was ousted through impeachment, the millions who filled Seoul's plazas were not simply looking for a change in personnel. According to commentators and core progressive voices, the public mandate was to dismantle a corrupt establishment and rewrite South Korea's governing systems. Yet, the current administration is increasingly viewed as settling into managerial pragmatism—tinkering with existing structures rather than breaking them down.

A recurring theme in the current debate is the necessity of a thorough personnel purge within powerful state institutions like the prosecution service, echoing historical precedents like Kim Young-sam’s dismantling of the military's "Hana-hoe" faction. Critics argue that retaining compromised figures in high offices under the banner of "practicality" or "lack of seasoned talent" leaves the door open for future abuses, rendering institutional tweaks meaningless.

The Death of Boss Politics and the Rise of "Deliberative Democracy"

The ruling Democratic Party's internal squabbling ahead of its national convention—marked by factional warfare and derogatory labeling—reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the modern South Korean electorate. The days of blind obedience to party bosses are over. Today’s voters operate in a horizontal, digitally connected sphere of deliberative democracy, constantly evaluating whether the government is sticking to its reformist promises.

Discontent over shifting policy lines, coupled with controversies surrounding foreign deployments and the silencing of critical intellectuals, has eaten away at the administration's core support. Observers note that if President Lee can realign with the foundational demands of the movement that swept him into office, he can heal the rift. Without that course correction, however, even a powerful progressive majority risks watching its political capital dissolve like a sandcastle.
