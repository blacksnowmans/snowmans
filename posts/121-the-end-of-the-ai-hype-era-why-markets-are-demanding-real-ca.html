The End of the AI Hype Era: Why Markets Are Demanding Real Cash Flow

September 03, 2026

  • Massive capital spending:
    •  Major tech companies are pouring hundreds of billions of dollars (over 700 trillion won) into artificial intelligence infrastructure, yet general public adoption sits stubbornly at around 10%.
  • The shift in valuation:
    •  Traditional metrics like earnings per share (EPS) and share buybacks are taking a back seat as companies drain cash reserves to fund AI, leaving stock prices more vulnerable.
  • Survival of the disciplined:
    •  As financial markets grow increasingly conservative, only hardware and tech firms that can prove actual commercial margins and efficient cash flow will survive the current cost-of-capital squeeze.

Global stock markets are walking on thin ice as the era of blindly cheering "AI is the future" comes to a close. Wall Street and global investors are no longer satisfied with grand visions; they are asking a cold, hard question: after pouring in astronomical sums of money, where are the actual returns? This is the core reality of the growing AI bubble debate, forcing a stark departure from the uncritical optimism of past tech cycles.

The gap between staggering capital expenditures (CAPEX) and a modest 10% consumer adoption rate is unsustainable. Companies funding infrastructure expansion through debt can no longer rely on simple earnings beats to keep shareholders happy. Instead, investors are shifting their focus to cash flow efficiency, custom semiconductors like ASICs, and real-world commercialization. Firms that indiscriminately scale up debt-fueled investments without clear monetization strategies face a harsh reckoning as financial institutions turn cautious.

In our view, this market anxiety is actually a healthy filter rather than a catastrophe to be feared. The ongoing correction forces a much-needed separation between hype-driven speculation and genuine technological execution. For international investors navigating this volatility, the key is to stop worrying about broad market pullbacks and start auditing individual portfolios for real financial staying power. The defining question for the rest of the year is simple: which companies are building a sustainable industrial foundation, and which are just running up a tab?
