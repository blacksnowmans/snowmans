South Korea's Housing Crisis: Why Tenants are Forced to Buy

September 21, 2026

South Korea’s rental market is experiencing a massive shift, as a shrinking supply of jeonse (long-term, lump-sum deposit rentals) drives desperate tenants into the home-buying market, further fueling price instability.

  • Supply collapse: The number of jeonse properties available has plummeted by over 40% compared to stable periods, with many landlords opting to sell their units instead.
  • Panic buying: Tenants, fearing eviction and realizing that monthly mortgage payments are now comparable to the costs of renting, are increasingly choosing to purchase homes, even in less desirable areas.
  • Policy misfires: Government efforts, such as the "Jeonse Security Trust" program and restrictive tax policies, are being criticized for ignoring the realities of the private rental market and further discouraging supply.
  • Rising inequality: Lower-income individuals are being pushed toward the outskirts of the capital region, widening the gap in housing quality and access to infrastructure.

Policy Disconnect and the "Office-Bound" Regulatory Approach

The core of the current crisis lies in a disconnect between government policy and market reality. The government’s attempt to introduce a "Jeonse Security Trust"—a program designed to protect deposits by offering landlords a 4.35% monthly rental return—has been met with indifference. Only about 5–10% of landlords, mostly wealthy investors, find the terms attractive. For the vast majority, the inability to access their capital for other investments makes the program a non-starter. By forcing bureaucratic solutions onto a complex private market, the government has essentially ignored the needs of both landlords and tenants.

Furthermore, recent tax reforms aimed at punishing "multi-home owners" have backfired. By pressuring long-term private rental providers—who previously acted as a stabilizing force by keeping rent increases capped at 5%—to sell their properties or exit the market, the government is inadvertently shrinking the very pool of rental stock that is currently in short supply. This, combined with arbitrary cuts to deposit insurance limits, has stalled the supply of nearly 150,000 housing units in the Seoul metropolitan area.

Leadership and the Erosion of Trust

Criticism is also mounting regarding the presidential administration's communication. The President’s recent suggestion that the state might engage in large-scale housing purchases if prices collapse has only deepened market confusion. Rather than providing a sense of security, the statement has been viewed as out-of-touch, leaving potential homebuyers uncertain about whether they should wait for a crash or rush into a market that feels increasingly volatile.

The prevailing view among critics is that the administration is surrounded by advisors who lack a nuanced understanding of current market dynamics. By relying on outdated data—such as fearing a 2022-style market collapse despite current signs of consumer resilience to 3% interest rates—policymakers are creating "fear-based" regulations. For the average South Korean, particularly young adults, the dream of homeownership is increasingly becoming an unattainable privilege rather than a realistic life goal. Without a pivot toward listening to ground-level experts and providing consistent, realistic support for housing supply, the government risks deeper public backlash.
