Why Korea's Post-Holiday Blues Are a Structural Crisis, Not Just Bad Stress

September 28, 2026

  • The root cause: Korean media discussions and psychological data point to in-law obligations and unsupportive husbands as the primary drivers of holiday burnout, far outweighing simple fatigue.
  • The mental toll: Severe exhaustion transforms mundane household labor and social anxiety into debilitating depressive symptoms, distorting everyday interactions into perceived hostility.
  • The medical fix: Experts argue that psychiatric medication—far from being a social taboo—offers necessary cognitive breaks, such as eight-hour windows of relief from acute anxiety.
  • What changes: With year-end holidays approaching, individuals are urged to abandon the cultural expectation of silent endurance and prioritize their mental health over family obligations.

For many in South Korea, the end of a major national holiday does not bring rest; it brings a profound psychological reckoning. While outsiders might view holiday stress as standard family friction, the aftermath exposes deep-seated structural inequalities in Korean domestic life. The burden falls heaviest on wives, who face an unequal distribution of housework alongside authoritarian family dynamics that leave them isolated even within their own marriages.

What makes this exhaustion dangerous is how it feeds on itself. Medical insights highlighted in local discourse reveal that depression does not just make people sad—it magnifies daily stress tenfold. A brain drained by overwork and social pressure begins to misinterpret ordinary interactions as active hostility, plunging individuals into deep cycles of isolation and resentment. When society tells people to simply endure, it ignores the biological reality of mental exhaustion, turning temporary holiday friction into chronic depressive illness.

Breaking the Taboo Around Psychiatric Care

Relying on mental fortitude is no longer working, and medical professionals are pushing for pragmatic solutions. While basic counseling helps filter out immediate distress, it has limits when brain chemistry is already depleted. Here, psychiatric medication functions not as a weakness, but as a brake for an overheated engine. Providing an overstressed mind with chemical relief—such as hours free from racing thoughts and physical anxiety—is a rational tool for survival, not a social failure.

The urgency of these interventions becomes glaringly clear as the calendar rolls forward. South Korea's relentless cycle of seasonal pressures means that the exhaustion of a major holiday bleeds directly into year-end gatherings and holiday obligations. If individuals do not break the habit of prioritizing family harmony over personal sanity, the burnout will only compound. Protecting one's mental health may require difficult choices—including skipping family gatherings entirely or setting hard boundaries—but reclaiming agency over one's life is the only way out of the cycle.
