U.S. Court Clears Path for DRAM Price-Fixing Trial Against Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron

November 17, 2026

A U.S. federal court has rejected a motion to dismiss an antitrust class-action lawsuit against the world's top three memory chipmakers—South Korea's Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, alongside U.S.-based Micron Technology—clearing the case to proceed to a full trial on the merits.

  • What happened: The court upheld five core claims raised by plaintiffs alleging that the three dominant memory manufacturers colluded to artificially restrict DRAM supply and inflate prices, citing the plausible appearance of coordinated action.
  • Who is affected: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—which together account for the bulk of global memory output—and Micron now face an extended legal battle in the United States over allegations of anticompetitive supply manipulation.
  • The core dispute: Plaintiffs argue that industry-wide DRAM production cuts and the aggressive conversion of fabrication lines toward high-bandwidth memory (HBM) were an orchestrated effort to squeeze standard memory supply, while the manufacturers insist these were independent, rational responses to changing market demand.
  • What changes: Beyond the risk of potentially massive damages, the ruling exposes the chipmakers' fab investment strategies, wafer-cut decisions, and shipment data to rigorous legal discovery, creating the risk of judicial interference in how they manage production.

When Market Discipline Looks Like Collusion

For decades, the memory industry has relied on aggressive output management to survive punishing boom-and-bust cycles. When demand plunged, curbing wafer input and shifting capacity toward higher-margin products—such as HBM tailored for artificial intelligence—was treated as standard cyclical defense. By permitting this class action to proceed, however, the U.S. court is treating that synchronized retrenchment not as ordinary corporate prudence, but as potentially unlawful coordination among an oligopoly.

Central to the court's reasoning is the claim that the three firms moved in lockstep, an alignment plaintiffs characterized as "hawks flying in formation." While the defendants maintain that every production cut and line conversion was an autonomous business decision dictated by the broader tech downturn, the court found enough circumstantial smoke to warrant a detailed examination of internal corporate records.

Operational Autonomy Under the Microscope

For Samsung and SK Hynix, the most acute threat is not merely the prospect of heavy fines or settlement payouts, but the chilling effect on everyday executive strategy. If standard portfolio reallocations—such as reducing legacy DRAM capacity to feed surging demand for AI chips—are treated as evidence of an illicit market squeeze, memory producers risk losing the operational flexibility needed to steer through fast-moving tech cycles.

With an early dismissal no longer an option, South Korea's two semiconductor champions now face a protracted legal struggle in the U.S. Just as the race to cement leadership in next-generation AI memory heats up, their core manufacturing and pricing decisions will be argued before an American jury.
