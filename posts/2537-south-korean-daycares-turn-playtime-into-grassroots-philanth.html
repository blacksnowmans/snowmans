South Korean Daycares Turn Playtime into Grassroots Philanthropy

November 20, 2026

Hundreds of daycare centers across South Korea are transforming annual flea markets into a massive wave of grassroots philanthropy, donating millions of won to support vulnerable neighbors ahead of the winter season.

  • Daycare centers in cities like Seosan, Jeju, and Ulsan have raised amounts ranging from about $200 (277,000 KRW) to over $1,500 (2.1 million KRW) through "Anabada" bazaars—a Korean movement focused on saving, sharing, exchanging, and reusing items.
  • The movement extends beyond cash; children in Mokpo donated 10 boxes of handmade kimchi, while centers in Gyeonggi Province donated prize money from choir competitions to international NGOs like World Vision and Save the Children.
  • These events serve as "living economy" lessons, teaching toddlers the value of resource circulation and social responsibility by having them price, sell, and donate proceeds from their own used toys and clothes.
  • Local governments and welfare agencies are increasingly formalizing these efforts into regional safety nets, ensuring these small-scale donations reach the most isolated residents in the community.

From Flea Markets to Financial Support

The "Anabada" spirit—a term coined during the 1997 financial crisis to encourage frugality—has found a permanent home in South Korea’s early childhood education. In mid-November, the Haedeun Daycare in Jeju reported raising approximately $1,500 (2.09 million KRW), while the Beonyeong-ro Centridge Daycare in Ulsan contributed $1,580 (2.2 million KRW) to local welfare funds. These are not corporate donations; they are the result of children selling toys they once loved and parents purchasing used household goods.

This trend represents a shift in how South Korean society views daycare centers. Once seen strictly as facilities for supervision, they are now acting as hubs for local welfare. By partnering with organizations like the ChildFund Korea (a major domestic social welfare agency) or the Community Chest of Korea, these centers ensure that the "small change" collected by children is funneled into professional systems that support at-risk youth and the elderly living alone.

Beyond Cash: Building a Local Safety Net

The variety of these donations highlights a thoughtful approach to local needs. In Mokpo, the Saekkum Damneun Uri Daycare provided kimchi—a vital winter staple—directly to low-income households. In Okcheon, the Hyangsu Daycare donated the entirety of a cash prize they won at a fire safety song contest. These actions teach children that excellence can be used for the public good, rather than just individual reward. This is a crucial counter-narrative in a country often criticized for its hyper-competitive educational environment.

However, there is a risk that these activities could devolve into "performance-based" events. As these charity drives become widespread, there is pressure on daycare directors to produce high donation totals or "photo-op" moments for local politicians. For this movement to remain meaningful, the focus must stay on the process: the moment a child decides to part with a toy to help a neighbor. The true value is not the $1,500 check, but the cultivation of "collective efficacy" in the next generation of citizens.
