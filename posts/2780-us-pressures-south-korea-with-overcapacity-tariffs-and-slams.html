US Pressures South Korea With Overcapacity Tariffs and Slams $650M Coupang Fine

November 27, 2026

The US Trump administration has opened an aggressive new front in trade pressure against South Korea, threatening tariffs of up to 25% on key manufacturing sectors while the White House and State Department launched an unprecedented attack against Seoul over a $650 million antitrust fine on e-commerce giant Coupang.

  • What happened: Washington unveiled "overcapacity" tariffs targeting 14 economies, setting South Korea’s baseline cap at 15% while keeping a 25% threat open, and publicly rebuked Seoul over a 900 billion won (about $650 million) regulatory fine against Coupang.
  • What it changes: US trade pressure on South Korea has expanded beyond traditional manufacturing tariffs into domestic digital regulation, treating Korean platform oversight as a non-tariff trade barrier.
  • Who is affected: South Korean chipmakers and exporters face cost uncertainty under a rising US tariff environment, while domestic tech regulators and foreign firms operating in Korea face heightened diplomatic friction.

A Tariff Cap Carrying a 25% Threat to Key Sectors

While South Korea officially kept its baseline overcapacity tariff cap to 15%, the Trump administration left open an additional 25% tariff threat aimed squarely at critical sectors like semiconductors. US officials labeled South Korea's industrial policies and state support as evidence of market-distorting overcapacity. Seoul firmly rejected the classification, refusing to let Washington frame its export-driven growth model as unfair overproduction. The broader US push pushed average American tariff rates back up to 13.9%, hitting allies like Japan (15%) and trade partners like Vietnam (up to 35%) and China (up to 40%).

Digital Regulation Becomes the New Bilateral Battleground

Simultaneously, trade friction spilled directly into domestic regulatory policy. The White House and the US State Department issued rare public warnings criticizing the Korea Fair Trade Commission (FTC), South Korea's antitrust regulator, for levying a 900 billion won (about $650 million) fine against Coupang, the US-listed Korean e-commerce giant. US officials claimed the sanction breached bilateral non-discrimination promises, warning that Washington is reviewing potential response measures.

The Korean government countered that the fine was a legitimate, non-discriminatory legal action to maintain fair market order. However, with Washington linking domestic corporate oversight to trade policy, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces a difficult balancing act: maintaining regulatory sovereignty while protecting export-dependent industries from further US trade retaliation.
