Why AI Hardware is Driving a Massive Rally in Korean Tech

September 07, 2026

The global stock market has shifted its focus from macroeconomic indicators like interest rates and inflation to a singular, relentless driver: the massive infrastructure build-out required for artificial intelligence. For investors in South Korea, this means the spotlight is firmly back on the country’s semiconductor giants.

  • The Catalyst: The race between models like OpenAI’s "ChatGPT 6.0" and Anthropic’s "Astra" is fueling an unprecedented demand for GPU clusters—the backbone of AI infrastructure.
  • The Shift: Investors are moving away from obsessing over minor employment data fluctuations and toward high-growth "AI agent" ecosystems.
  • Key Beneficiaries: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, South Korea's two largest semiconductor manufacturers, are seeing renewed demand as AI systems require exponentially more memory to process data.

The Case for Memory Semiconductors

While many investors worry about interest rate hikes, the reality of the AI era is that data center power and memory requirements are effectively "rate-proof." According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), power consumption by data centers is set to double by 2030. As AI agents become more sophisticated, the power draw per rack is soaring to 120kW, making high-performance memory an essential, non-negotiable component of modern infrastructure.

The advice for those holding Samsung Electronics or SK Hynix shares is straightforward: do not sell into the initial rebounds. The market is undergoing a structural change, not a cyclical one. For those who entered at the market peak and are currently underwater, now is the time to manage position sizes to lower average entry costs rather than selling out of fear.

Hardware: The Backbone of the AGI Era

The current rally in US and Korean tech is not a temporary event; it is the realization of the "AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) era." As software companies like OpenAI and Anthropic engage in aggressive competition, the underlying hardware—the "picks and shovels" of the AI gold rush—becomes the most valuable asset. This structural expansion is trickling down to Korean parts and equipment manufacturers, such as Hanmi Semiconductor, Wonik IPS, and Eugene Technology, as data center construction continues to ramp up.

My Take: Watching this market, it is clear that capital is accurately tracking the path of technological evolution. The rise of sophisticated AI models isn't just a software update; it is a fundamental shift in how the world processes information. Holding memory stocks today is analogous to owning the shovel manufacturer during a gold rush. While concerns regarding power grids and ESG compliance are valid, history shows that technology eventually evolves to solve these bottlenecks. Investors should look past the short-term noise of macroeconomic data and focus on the companies building the physical foundations of the AI future.
