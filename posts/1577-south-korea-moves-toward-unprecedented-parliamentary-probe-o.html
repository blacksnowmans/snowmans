South Korea Moves Toward Unprecedented Parliamentary Probe of Supreme Court Chief

October 28, 2026

South Korea is bracing for an unprecedented constitutional crisis as the National Assembly prepares to launch a formal investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, marking the first time in the country’s history that the head of the judiciary will face a parliamentary probe.

  • The Trigger: The ruling Democratic Party (DP), South Korea's liberal majority party, initiated the move citing concerns over Cho's refusal to reappoint certain Supreme Court justices and allegations of opaque "cash bonuses" distributed within the judiciary.
  • The Timeline: The DP aims to begin the probe on November 5 and conclude it by November 12, seeking a rapid fact-finding process.
  • Political Conflict: The main opposition party, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), is fiercely resisting the move, framing the inquiry as a political attack intended to subjugate the judiciary and threatening to link it to potential impeachment proceedings against President Lee Jae-myung.

A Clash Over Judicial Independence and Transparency

The core of the dispute lies in the tension between judicial independence and the democratic demand for accountability. By blocking the reappointment of specific Supreme Court justices, Chief Justice Cho has been accused of attempting to maintain the judiciary as a "fortress of conservatism" that resists the reform agenda of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Critics argue that this is not merely an exercise of administrative power, but a move that hampers legal progress and keeps judicial decisions biased.

Furthermore, the controversy surrounding the distribution of mysterious cash bonuses has drawn parallels to the scandals involving former leader Yoon Seok-youl, who is currently imprisoned. Opposition within the DP and the smaller Rebuilding Korea Party (led by Cho Kuk) argue that the judiciary cannot be a "sacred" institution exempt from public oversight. They contend that the use of taxpayer-funded budgets without transparent records is a fundamental failure of public service standards.

Strategic Rifts Within the Opposition

The approach taken by the liberal bloc reveals a strategic divide. The Democratic Party, wary of a political backlash that could portray the judiciary as a "political victim," has opted for a parliamentary investigation as a precursor to potential impeachment. They seek to build a foundation of evidence—a "smoking gun"—before taking the extreme step of removing the Chief Justice.

In contrast, the Rebuilding Korea Party is pushing for a more aggressive stance, demanding both an investigation and immediate impeachment proceedings. They argue that caution is indistinguishable from complacency. While the DP and the Rebuilding Korea Party currently differ on the pace of the intervention, both agree that the current state of judicial opacity is unsustainable. Meanwhile, the People Power Party’s loud opposition has only fueled public suspicion, with many observers asking: if the judiciary has nothing to hide, why are they working so hard to block the inquiry?

As November approaches, the focus remains on whether the parliamentary probe can uncover verifiable truths or if it will devolve into a stalemate. For a nation that has struggled to reform its judicial and prosecutorial powers, this investigation serves as a critical test of whether the legislature can successfully rein in a judiciary that critics say has long operated as a law unto itself.
