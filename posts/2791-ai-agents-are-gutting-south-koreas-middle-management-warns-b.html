AI Agents Are Gutting South Korea's Middle Management, Warns Big-Data Guru

November 27, 2026

South Korean big-data expert and author Song Gil-young argues that AI agents are dismantling 250 years of industrial division of labor, eliminating middle managers and leaving only self-reliant "original humans" able to survive.

  • A new survival framework: In recent lectures and writing, Song—who previously coined Korean trend concepts including "nuclear individuals" (2023) and "lightweight civilization" (2025)—introduces "Sugo-inryu" (roughly "effort-refocused humanity") as a 2027 benchmark, predicting AI will absorb administrative busywork while demanding deeper core craft from humans.
  • Corporate hierarchies upended: According to Song, corporate chairmen in their 70s and 80s are now learning to write AI prompts hands-on, while junior staff—the stereotypical South Korean assistant manager ("Kim Dae-ri")—can use AI agents to plan, execute, and verify work directly, bypassing traditional department heads ("bujang").
  • Job redesign over mass layoffs: Research from South Korea's state-backed Korea Research Institute for Vocational Education and Training shows major domestic firms scaling back routine management roles in favor of hands-on job redesign, echoing global AI exposure estimates from Goldman Sachs, the IMF, and PwC.
  • Three rules for staying relevant: To avoid becoming a replaceable "copy human" who cannot bake a loaf of bread despite 30 years at a large bakery firm, Song urges workers to execute tasks directly rather than delegate, publish work publicly as proof of authorship, and attach their own names instead of hiding behind corporate titles.
  • Solo business and real-world proof: Rather than spending hundreds of thousands of dollars (hundreds of millions of won) on retail leases and staff, 40-to-60-year-olds are building one-person businesses around curated personal tastes, while U.S. universities and employers revive oral exams to catch AI-generated work.

Why Korea's Corporate Model Makes This Shift Acute

Song's diagnosis lands with particular force in South Korea, where white-collar identity has long been fused to corporate brands and rigid hierarchy. In an economy where employees typically exit large companies around age 60—leaving decades of post-corporate life—spending 30 years as a narrow administrative cog is no longer a safe bargain when software agents replicate coordination work instantly.

There is a sharp realism in Song's pivot back toward pre-industrial artisans, updated for digital platforms. Stripping out commercial rent, hiring, and back-office overhead lowers the barrier for mid-career workers to monetize niche expertise, yet it also removes the institutional safety net that South Korean conglomerates once provided.

Ultimately, the hardest hurdle Song identifies is cultural rather than technical. Publishing unfinished ideas under your own name invites public scrutiny in a status-conscious society, and his advice to embrace feeling embarrassed by work written ten years ago reframes imperfection as proof of growth. In an era flooded with polished AI output, visible human provenance may become the only durable career moat left.
