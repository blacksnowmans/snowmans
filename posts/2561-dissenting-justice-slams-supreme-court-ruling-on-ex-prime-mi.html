Dissenting Justice Slams Supreme Court Ruling on Ex-Prime Minister’s Role in Martial Law Coup

November 20, 2026

A dissenting opinion by Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyung-mi argues that former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo’s convening of the Cabinet prior to the December 3, 2024 martial law declaration was not routine statecraft, but an act of insurrection designed to provide legal cover for an unlawful self-coup.

  • The judicial divide: While the Supreme Court majority cleared Han of possessing the specific intent to disrupt constitutional order prior to the declaration, Justice Oh argued that Han's actions before and after the decree formed an indivisible continuum of complicity.
  • A procedural sham: The dissent details how Han, having already reviewed martial law decrees around 8:00 p.m., concealed the agenda from fellow ministers and rushed to gather an 11-member quorum solely to rubber-stamp the measure in a unilateral two-minute meeting.
  • Outdated precedent: Oh criticized the majority for relying on an earlier March 2025 Constitutional Court ruling that rejected Han's impeachment, pointing out that crucial CCTV evidence from the presidential office only emerged after Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was ousted from office in April 2025.
  • Legal precedent at stake: The dissent warns that excusing senior officials because they observed formal administrative motions effectively rolls out a red carpet of legitimacy for future power grabs.

The Mechanics of a "Procedural Accomplice"

The core of the legal dispute lies in how the court framed Han’s timeline. The Supreme Court majority artificially bisected the evening of December 3 at the moment martial law was publicly broadcast, treating Han's pre-declaration maneuvers as standard bureaucratic coordination. Justice Oh rejected this boundary, noting that Han spent the evening reviewing unconstitutional decrees, assembling ministers under false pretenses, overseeing a token two-minute session, and later spending 16 minutes with the interior minister discussing implementation measures like cutting electricity and water.

Under South Korean criminal law, the crime of insurrection requires an intent to subvert constitutional institutions. In Justice Oh's analysis, Han knew Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s resolve was unyielding and recognized the fatal constitutional defects in the decree. By choosing to push through the appearance of State Council deliberation rather than rallying ministers to resist, Han knowingly traded the Cabinet's constitutional role as a check on executive power for an alibi that aided the coup.

A Lasting Warning on Bureaucratic Duty

This dissent addresses a perennial vulnerability in South Korea's institutional design: the weaponization of bureaucratic procedure. In a classic self-coup, power is not seized by outside forces with tanks alone; it is subverted from within under the guise of existing executive authorities. When institutional gatekeepers treat deliberative mandates as mere box-checking exercises, they strip democratic safeguards of their substantive purpose.

While the majority opinion shields Han from criminal liability through a narrow interpretation of intent, Justice Oh’s dissent establishes an enduring standard for administrative accountability. It insists that high-ranking civil servants cannot retreat behind formal procedures to wash their hands of facilitating constitutional crises, leaving a stark record of how state systems can be turned against the public they were built to protect.
