A Telegram Dispute Exposes Cracks in President Lee Jae-myung’s Progressive Base

November 02, 2026

A frosty Telegram exchange between South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and his former chief economic architect, Professor Choi Bae-geun, has laid bare an escalating ideological rift inside the ruling progressive camp.

  • The private spat goes public: Choi Bae-geun, an economics professor at Konkuk University who helped craft Lee’s flagship economic platforms, revealed he left a Telegram message from the president unread after Lee asked "What about me has changed?", following presidential social media posts accusing internal critics of being self-serving job seekers disguised as radical reformers.
  • A pivot to the center: Insecure over winning the presidency with 49.42% of the vote following the impeachment and ouster of Yoon Suk-yeol, Lee's inner circle has pursued a "structural majority" strategy, actively courting moderate and conservative support while sidelining traditional progressives.
  • Controversial appointments and censorship: Discontent exploded over the appointment of conservative prosecutor Kim Ji-yong to head the new Serious Crime Investigation Office—a body intended to strip prosecutors of direct investigative powers—and reports that government portals censored Choi's warnings regarding stock-market volatility and high semiconductor reliance.
  • Failing to win either side: Despite attempts to broaden his appeal, a Realmeter poll released on October 26 placed Lee's approval rating at just 35.3%, with negative evaluations reaching 62.3%.

The Anatomy of a Broken Alliance

The fracture between Lee and Choi is not a petty personal grievance; it signals the structural unraveling of the coalition that brought the Democratic Party of Korea back to power. Choi provided the theoretical backbone for Lee’s "Basic Society" and "Basic Finance" pledges during the 2021–2022 presidential race. To see him openly break with the Blue House highlights how far the administration has drifted from its populist, reformist roots.

Rather than addressing policy criticisms on their merits, the presidential office allowed online rumors to fester—specifically insinuating that Choi attacked the administration because he was passed over for the Bank of Korea governorship. Choi vehemently denied seeking high office on YouTube, condemning the smear campaign as coordinated digital violence. When an administration dismisses legitimate policy dissent as sour grapes from disgruntled job seekers, it shuts down healthy internal debate and surrounds the executive with loyalists.

The Mirage of the "Structural Majority"

The strategic miscalculation driving this wedge is the administration’s belief that its progressive base is a captive audience that can be taken for granted while the government tacks right. From appointing prosecutors who once resisted institutional reform to introducing high-leverage single-stock ETFs to prop up the stock market, the administration appears fixated on superficial headline numbers rather than systemic redistribution.

While the government points to an expansionary 2026 budget of 727.9 trillion won (around $530 billion) and small business debt-relief programs, working-class households continue to face heavy debt and steep living costs. By treating working-class loyalists as guaranteed votes while chasing an elusive center-right coalition, President Lee risks alienating the very voters who shielded him during his political exile under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure. A leader who asks "What have I changed?" instead of asking why his defenders feel abandoned is a leader heading toward severe political isolation.
