South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Hits 37% Approval as Judicial Clashes Deepen

October 07, 2026

  • South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has fallen to 37.1%, while negative ratings reached a high of 61.4%, according to an October 2026 poll by Zowon C&I.
  • Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae refused to attend a parliamentary audit, sparking severe backlash from lawmakers and accusations of undermining the separation of powers.
  • The drop in support is driven by accumulated economic fatigue, policy confusion, and eroding backing among critical demographics like voters in their 30s.
  • The ruling Democratic Party faces a tight race against the conservative People Power Party, raising pressure on the administration to push forward with judicial and economic reforms.

Support Slumps Amid Economic Fatigue

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing a severe political slump. According to a Zowon C&I survey conducted in early October 2026 among 2,000 adults, President Lee's positive approval rating dropped to 37.1%, down more than 12 percentage points from early in the year, when it neared 50%. Meanwhile, negative ratings surged to 61.4%. The ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party, is now locked in a dead heat with the conservative People Power Party, which sits at 39.6% in party support.

Regional dissatisfaction is widespread. Outside of the traditional liberal stronghold of Jeolla, negative ratings have surpassed 50% nationwide, including in the crucial Busan-Ulsan-Gyeongnam region. Analysts point to lingering public frustration over cost-of-living pressures, housing policy debates, and economic instability affecting younger generations as key drivers of the slump. Rather than backing down, the administration is being urged to tighten its reform agenda rather than rely on cautious defensiveness.

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae Stokes Judicial Tensions

Adding to the political turmoil, Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has triggered a major constitutional standoff by refusing to appear as a witness at a parliamentary audit scheduled by the National Assembly. Citing the separation of powers and judicial independence, Cho's refusal has drawn fierce criticism from progressive lawmakers, who accuse him of acting more like a political player than a guardian of the law. Internal discontent within the judiciary has also boiled over, with lower court judges and union representatives expressing deep frustration with his leadership.

For the Lee administration, this judicial friction presents a high-stakes opportunity. Observers argue that confronting institutional resistance and pushing for robust judicial reform could allow the government to rally its core supporters and reclaim a clear political narrative. Stalled momentum can only be reversed, proponents of reform argue, by directly tackling political roadblocks rather than appeasing conservative critics.
