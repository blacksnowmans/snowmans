South Korea's Prosecution Reform Hits Turbulence Over First Agency Chief Pick

September 29, 2026

  • South Korea is dismantling its 70-year-old prosecution system, replacing the Supreme Prosecutors' Office with a new investigative agency (the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency) and a separate prosecution service.
  • The nomination of Kim Ji-yong as the new agency's inaugural chief has sparked internal party backlash and accusations of compromised vetting.
  • Critics warn that hastily stripping away investigative safeguards risks creating massive legal blind spots that will harm everyday citizens rather than reform the system.

South Korea's sweeping overhaul of its judicial system is running into a familiar problem: political compromise. As the country prepares to shutter its traditional prosecution apparatus, the appointment of Kim Ji-yong to head the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency has left even ruling-party supporters scratching their heads. Rather than breaking cleanly with the past, the administration appears to be recycling old personnel and watered-down tactics in the name of pragmatism.

At the heart of the controversy is the fate of supplementary investigative powers—safeguards that allow prosecutors to catch police oversights and prevent cases from being quietly buried. With arrest warrant issuance rates dropping and political jockeying intensifying within the presidential office's young and inexperienced personnel team, critics argue that the administration's vetting apparatus is failing. Appointing figures tied to past investigative biases to lead a brand-new agency undercuts the entire rationale of the reform.

The Illusion of Radical Change

True reform requires more than swapping out agency nameplates and shuffling titles. When administrations prioritize safe, uninspired appointments over genuine structural overhaul, they risk repeating the mistakes of their predecessors. The current friction—spanning from debates over minority party representation in the National Assembly to backroom personnel choices—shows that South Korea's political class remains tethered to old habits of horse-trading and institutional self-preservation.

If the government wants the public to believe that this restructuring marks a true departure from the past, it needs to prove that personnel decisions match its soaring rhetoric. Without rigorous vetting and a willingness to break with entrenched political insiders, South Korea's much-touted judicial revolution risks becoming just another exercise in rearranging deckchairs.
