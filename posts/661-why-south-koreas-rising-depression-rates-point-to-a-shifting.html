Why South Korea's Rising Depression Rates Point to a Shifting Culture, Not a Weakening Will

October 04, 2026

  • South Korean psychiatrist Kim Chong-ki explains that depression is a physical illness tied to brain metabolism, not a failure of personal willpower.
  • Official depression diagnoses in South Korea have surged by over 30% in the past five years, driven largely by a more human-centric society that finally gives clinical names to hidden suffering.
  • The stigma around psychiatric care remains high, particularly for adults in their 30s and 40s facing intense family and societal pressures like traditional holiday stress.

For years, South Korean society has treated mental health through a harsh, dualistic lens: if your mind hurts, the common advice is simply to change your attitude or try harder. In a recent appearance on the YouTube channel "Jobdahan Balgyeon," psychiatrist Kim Chong-ki dismantled this notion. Mental pain is not a ghost in the machine; it is the physical state of the brain itself. Treating depression as a lack of willpower is as absurd as telling someone with a broken leg to just walk it off.

Kim argues that the spike in reported depression cases does not mean modern Koreans are inherently weaker than past generations. Instead, it reflects a profound cultural shift toward humanism. Society is finally talking about inner pain rather than brushing it aside for the sake of economic output. By bringing these struggles into the open, more people are trading silent endurance for actual medical care. Psychiatry is reframed not as a sign of failure, but as a vital form of self-care.

The Burden of Modern Expectations and the Power of Listening

Much of South Korea's mental health strain stems from rigid social roles, particularly during major holidays like Chuseok, which routinely flood psychiatric clinics with patients in their 30s and 40s trapped in grueling family dynamics. Yet, the remedy often starts with simple human connection rather than complex medicine. Kim points out that psychiatrists are not mind-readers; their most important tool is active, non-judgmental listening. As South Koreans navigate an increasingly isolated and high-pressure world, shifting from quick fixes and victim-blaming to genuine empathy may be the most effective medicine we have.
