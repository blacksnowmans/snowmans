President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Ratings Plunge Amid Growing Public Distrust

October 13, 2026

  • Approval Crisis: President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has plummeted to 35.2%, with disapproval hitting 61.4% according to Realmeter, a leading South Korean polling agency.
  • Seoul’s Warning Sign: Approval in Seoul has fallen to 32%, dipping below support levels in the traditional conservative stronghold of Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province.
  • Opposition Resurgence: For the first time, the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative opposition party) has overtaken the ruling Democratic Party in overall polling.
  • Third-Party Rise: The Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Cho Kuk, is surging toward 10% support, signaling a significant shift in the progressive political landscape.

A Deepening Rift Between the Government and Supporters

The latest data from Realmeter is more than a statistical dip; it is a profound signal of disillusionment. For a government that rose to power on the promise of systemic reform, the erosion of support in the capital—the traditional bellwether for South Korean elections—is a critical failure. The data highlights a growing "trust gap," as supporters increasingly view the government not just as ineffective, but as failing to uphold the values they were elected to protect.

This frustration is exacerbated by President Lee’s leadership style, particularly his late-night social media activity. Critics, including figures from across the political spectrum, argue that the President’s tendency to use social media to label opponents or criticize internal dissenters is undermining the dignity of his office. Instead of serving as a unifying force, the leadership is being accused of alienating its own base through inflammatory rhetoric, leaving supporters feeling "betrayed" rather than just disappointed.

The "Revolving Door" of Accountability

The government's recent handling of internal dissent has further damaged its credibility. The inspection of Prosecutor Im Eun-jeong—a figure long championed by progressives for her history of opposing corruption during the era of the disgraced former leader, Yoon Seok-yeol—is now being viewed as a hypocritical "double standard." By targeting a whistleblower while simultaneously appearing indecisive on key reform issues, such as the special counsel investigation into opposition leader Han Dong-hoon, the Democratic Party is struggling to project a coherent vision.

The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party suggests that voters are looking for alternatives as they lose faith in the Democratic Party’s willingness to push for meaningful change. If the ruling party continues to play it safe to avoid a "backlash," it risks losing the very supporters who once saw it as the engine of national reform. To reverse this trajectory, the government must move beyond reactive politics and address the deep-seated grievances of an electorate that feels its trust has been squandered.
