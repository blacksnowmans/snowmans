Why South Korea's Obsession with Sleep Deprivation Is Backfiring

September 10, 2026

  • The myth of the hustle: South Korean society has long treated sleeping less as a badge of honor and getting a full night's rest as laziness, a cultural pressure that ignores basic human biology.
  • Sleep as a fixed trait: Neurological and historical examples—from Albert Einstein sleeping up to 11 hours to Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak—show that sleep length and circadian rhythms ("short sleepers" vs. "long sleepers," morning vs. evening types) are innate biological constants, not habits to be fixed.
  • Institutional damage: Rigid school and work schedules that penalize evening types and long sleepers crush creativity, worsen academic performance, and damage mental health.
  • Taking back control: Experts suggest a simple 10-second daily sleep log and explicitly defining one's own sleep identity to reclaim personal agency and improve maturity and focus.

For decades, waking up early and burning the midnight oil has been treated as the ultimate proof of dedication in South Korea. Admit to sleeping 11 hours, and you are likely to face dirty looks and accusations of sloth. But ignoring our bodies' natural rhythms in the name of grit is not noble—it is a form of biological violence that destroys creativity and personal well-being.

Human beings are wired with wildly diverse sleep architectures. While "short sleepers" like Steve Jobs could function on minimal rest, equally revolutionary figures like Albert Einstein and Apple's foundational engineer Steve Wozniak were classic "long sleepers." Forcing a creative, late-night thinker into a rigid, early-bird corporate mold does not make them more productive; it simply burns them out. Studies consistently show that matching individuals to their natural sleep patterns drastically improves cognitive performance, emotional stability, and academic achievement.

Stop Treating Sleep Like an Obstacle

The damage starts early. South Korea's grueling education system—forcing teenagers into late-night cram schools and early morning classes—wages war on adolescent biology. While reforms like later school start times have proven to help students recover emotional control and focus, the overarching culture still views rest as lost productivity. This is a profound mistake. Sleep deprivation does not fuel ambition; it punches a hole in the gas tank and blames the car for not moving fast enough.

Ultimately, maturity and emotional intelligence begin with a good night's rest. When people are chronically sleep-deprived, their flaws and irritability take over, leaving no room for their strengths to shine. Defining your own sleep needs—saying out loud, "I am a person who needs to sleep from 11 PM to 7 AM"—acts as a psychological anchor that forces both your brain and your daily schedule to realign. It is time to drop the social hypnosis that equates exhaustion with excellence, and treat sleep for what it truly is: our most fundamental weapon for a functioning life.
