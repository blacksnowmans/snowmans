Ruling Party to Force Through 65-Year-Old Retirement Age Legislation

November 28, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party (DP) has announced it will bypass stalled labor-management negotiations to unilaterally legislate an extension of the legal retirement age to 65 by the end of this year.

  • Deadlock broken by force: After 10 months of failed talks between labor and business groups, the DP is moving to draft its own bill to prevent a "poverty cliff" for aging workers.
  • Policy divergence: Business lobbies demand a "re-employment" model with lower wages to manage costs, while labor unions insist on a blanket legal extension to ensure job security.
  • Healthcare reform: The party also launched a special healthcare committee to address the collapse of medical infrastructure in rural provinces, notably including representatives from the Korean Medical Association (KMA), the nation's largest doctors' group.
  • Livelihood focus: The moves are part of a broader year-end push by the Lee Jae-myung administration to secure budgets for 1.19 million public housing units and stabilize the domestic economy.

The End of Negotiation: Why the DP is Moving Alone

The decision to push for a legal retirement age of 65 marks a significant shift in the Democratic Party’s strategy. For nearly a year, the party attempted to mediate between management and labor. However, the gap remains insurmountable. Management argues that a blanket extension will freeze hiring for the youth and burden companies with high seniority-based salaries. They prefer a system where workers are retired and then rehired on new, lower-paying contracts. Labor, led by the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU), views this as a "trick" to exploit cheap labor and demands the legal age be raised to match the starting age for national pension eligibility.

By aiming for legislation before the year ends, the DP is signaling that it will no longer wait for a consensus that may never come. This "speed battle" is designed to provide a safety net for the 50-60 age demographic, a critical voting bloc currently facing a multi-year gap between their current retirement age (60) and the age they can actually begin drawing their pensions. While the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, is expected to resist the move as an "anti-market" measure, the DP is leveraging its 170-plus seats in the National Assembly to frame this as an essential survival issue for a hyper-aging society.

Healthcare and Housing: Anchoring the "Basic Society"

Parallel to the retirement debate, the launch of the Special Committee on Healthcare under Representative Kim Yoon—a prominent medical policy expert—aims to fix the "emergency room merry-go-round," where patients are frequently rejected by hospitals due to staffing shortages. The inclusion of the KMA’s vice president in this committee is a rare moment of cooperation in a landscape often defined by strikes and government-doctor standoffs. The goal is to move beyond the current medical crisis by building a "Basic Society" framework where high-quality care is guaranteed regardless of whether a citizen lives in Seoul or a remote province.

The Interpretation

The DP is currently operating on a "delivery-first" mandate. Under President Lee Jae-myung, the party is attempting to shed its image as a purely political entity and rebrand as a "problem-solving" ruling power. By tackling retirement, regional healthcare, and the supply of 1.19 million public housing units simultaneously, they are trying to create a narrative of stability and competence. This is a direct response to the economic anxiety felt by the middle class, who are squeezed by high interest rates and the rising cost of living.

However, the risk of "legislative driving" is significant social friction. Bypassing management's concerns on retirement could lead to reduced corporate investment or a quiet freeze on entry-level hiring. The DP’s challenge will be to balance these aggressive reforms with tax incentives or subsidies for companies—essentially "buying" industrial peace with the national budget. For the DP, the calculation is simple: the cost of inaction is higher than the cost of a political fight.
