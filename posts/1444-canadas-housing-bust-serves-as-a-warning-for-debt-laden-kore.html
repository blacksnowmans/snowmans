Canada’s Housing Bust Serves as a Warning for Debt-Laden Korean Households

October 25, 2026

Runaway ownership costs and an unsustainable mountain of household debt have punctured Canada’s housing bubble, offering a sobering warning for South Korea’s equally debt-heavy property market.

  • Crushing carrying costs: According to RBC Economics, the share of household income required to cover homeownership costs has reached an astonishing 84.1% in Vancouver and 65.2% in Toronto, well above sustainable thresholds.
  • Severe real price drops: While Vancouver’s home prices are nominally down 14% from their 2022 peaks, accounting for 13.4% cumulative inflation reveals a punishing 24.2% drop in real terms, wiping out the equity of buyers who put down 20%.
  • The refinancing cliff: Over one million Canadian mortgage holders face five-year loan resets this year, transitioning from pandemic-era rates of 1.5–3% to rates between 4.5% and 6.5%.
  • Speculative retreat: A ban on foreign buyers, lowered immigration targets, and soaring maintenance costs have triggered panic selling in urban condominiums, halving national home transactions from 2021 highs.

The "Asset Rich, Cash Poor" Mirage

On paper, Canadian households appeared wealthier over the past decade as home values soared by nearly 150%, masking an alarming jump in the debt-to-disposable-income ratio from 134% to 186%. Yet paper wealth cannot pay monthly bills. Homeowners must service thirty-year debts in cash alongside sharply rising property taxes, maintenance fees, and insurance premiums. When basic carrying costs consume over 60% of take-home pay, even high-net-worth owners become house-poor, triggering voluntary fire sales and strategic defaults.

The pain is concentrated in the metropolitan condo sector, where investors engaged in aggressive gap investment during the pandemic. Unlike detached single-family homes underpinned by end-user demand, investor-heavy condos have seen listing inventories surge as rental yields fail to cover debt obligations. Similar stress is rippling through Australia, where 95% of mortgages are on variable rates and recent crackdowns on negative gearing tax perks have accelerated price declines in Sydney and Melbourne.

Why Korean Markets Are Anxiously Watching

For South Korean market watchers, Canada’s trajectory feels uncomfortably familiar. South Korea also struggles with one of the world's highest household debt-to-income ratios, with personal wealth disproportionately locked in residential real estate. During the low-rate pandemic boom, young Korean buyers engaged in "yeong-kkeul" (gathering every penny of debt possible) to purchase high-priced apartments in and around Seoul, betting on guaranteed long-term gains.

Canada’s experience demonstrates that asset prices cannot remain divorced from median incomes indefinitely. When monetary tightening hits and external speculative liquidity recedes, paper gains evaporate quickly against stubborn inflation and rising interest rates. If Canadian and Australian property markets are any guide, the myth of real estate invincibility ultimately shatters on the cold reality of household cash flow.
