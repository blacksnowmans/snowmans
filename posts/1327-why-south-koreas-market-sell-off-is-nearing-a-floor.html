Why South Korea's Market Sell-Off Is Nearing a Floor

October 19, 2026

South Korea's benchmark Kospi index is working through an oversold phase, backed by positive real interest rates and strong semiconductor fundamentals that point to a year-end recovery.

  • What happened: The Kospi suffered a sharp pullback, driven by forced liquidations of single-stock leverage products tied to major tech firms and lingering worries over U.S. monetary policy.
  • What it changes: South Korea's real interest rate has turned positive for the first time—with September consumer inflation dropping to 2.9% against the central bank's 3% base rate—removing a key headlock on local equities.
  • Who is affected: Retail investors nursing losses from the October dip, alongside foreign institutional buyers who have begun rotating back into undervalued domestic chipmakers.

The Numbers Tell a Different Story Than the Panic

For investors opening brokerage accounts in recent weeks, the market has felt punishing. Stubborn inflation, geopolitical jitters, and a wave of mechanical selling in leveraged exchange-traded products wiped out recent gains. Yet a closer look at the macro data reveals a market undergoing forced technical liquidation rather than a fundamental collapse. The Kospi's 12-month forward price-to-earnings ratio has cratered to roughly 5x, sitting well below historic lows seen during the 2008 global financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic.

At the same time, corporate earnings power is heading in the opposite direction. Driven by relentless demand for advanced artificial intelligence hardware, global chip giants like Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) reported blowout third-quarter results—including a 68.7% jump in net profit and a staggering 58.7% operating margin. Even as critics chatter about a potential semiconductor "peak," TSMC's aggressive capital expenditure plans through 2028 prove that total global demand for advanced packaging and high-bandwidth memory remains immense.

Looking Ahead to Year-End

Local sentiment has also been clouded by political ghosts and lingering domestic friction following the tumultuous era of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol. But markets ultimately trade on liquidity and earnings, not past political noise. With foreign investors shifting back to net buyers in Seoul, and U.S. equities holding firm through historical seasonal strength, the conditions for a year-end relief rally are steadily falling into place.
