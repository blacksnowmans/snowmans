A New White Paper on the Cheonan Sinking Reignites South Korea’s Most Polarizing Defense Taboo

November 10, 2026

Sixteen years after the sinking of the South Korean warship ROKS Cheonan, author Chae Soo-beom has published the Cheonan Citizen White Paper, compiling extensive court trial transcripts and depositions to reopen questions surrounding an event long treated as an untouchable state consensus.

  • A foundation in courtroom records: Unlike dozens of speculative books published over the past 16 years, Chae’s dossier bypasses internet theories to focus strictly on cross-examinations, witness testimonies, and official records produced during years of civil and criminal litigation.
  • Mainstream publishing cold shoulder: Major commercial presses turned down the manuscript due to political sensitivities, forcing independent imprint Ingan & Jayeonsa (Human & Natural History) to step in and release the work.
  • Unresolved operational questions: The volume documents discrepancies raised during trials, including broadcast footage of a helicopter transporting an unidentified two-meter object from the scene and questions regarding the initial search-and-rescue coordination between South Korean and U.S. naval forces.
  • Retaliation instead of debate: When public verification was proposed—including an open challenge allowing the Ministry of National Defense to set the venue and testing rules—defense authorities declined and instead met scrutiny with criminal complaints.

Deconstructing an Inviolable Security Narrative

The core grievance driving the *White Paper* is Chae’s observation that the sinking has devolved into a matter of "religion." In modern South Korean politics, accepting the official joint investigation verdict has frequently functioned as an ideological litmus test. Questioning the timeline or scientific conclusions rarely produced public debate; instead, dissenters were branded as heretics or North Korean sympathizers. By cataloging what naval officers, divers, and officials admitted under oath on the witness stand, the book seeks to rescue the tragedy from partisan warfare and return it to evidentiary scrutiny.

The dossier also highlights the hypocrisy that defined the political handling of the crisis at the time. While conservative officials publicly projected an uncompromising, near-war footing against Pyongyang, press accounts detailed how envoys simultaneously attempted backchannel diplomacy—even attempting to pass envelopes of cash to North Korean officials to arrange summits, an initiative that backfired into an international embarrassment. Treating national defense as a disposable tool for domestic political management inevitably deepened civilian skepticism.

The Institutional Price of Suppressing Scrutiny

The treatment of investigative reporting around the incident set a troubling precedent for South Korean civil liberties. When public broadcaster KBS aired an evidence-based critique on its investigative flagship Tracking 60 Minutes, the Korea Communications Commission hit the production team with disciplinary sanctions. Although the judiciary eventually ruled those government sanctions illegal, the message to newsrooms was unmistakable: official defense pronouncements were not to be investigated too closely.

Chae’s compilation illustrates that state silence and legal intimidation do not settle historical controversies—they only defer them. For a younger generation growing up years after the event, blind faith in institutional declarations cannot replace open verification. Transparent, adversarial scrutiny remains the only durable path toward resolving South Korea’s lingering national security wounds.
