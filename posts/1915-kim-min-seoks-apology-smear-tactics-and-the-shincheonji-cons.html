Kim Min-seok’s Apology: Smear Tactics and the "Shincheonji" Conspiracy in the Ruling Party

November 03, 2026

Kim Min-seok, a prominent figure in the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), has officially retracted his claims of a secret alliance between political rivals and the Shincheonji religious sect, signaling a fragile attempt to end a months-long internal crisis within South Korea's ruling party.

  • The Retraction: Kim admitted during a general meeting of DPK lawmakers that there was "no evidence" to support his allegations of a "three-party secret alliance" involving the Shincheonji Church of Jesus—a controversial religious group often characterized as a cult—and internal party rivals.
  • Political Fallout: The apology follows an investigation by a party task force which confirmed the existence of Shincheonji members within the party ranks but found zero proof of the high-level political conspiracy Kim had alleged during the July party convention.
  • Internal Backlash: Jung Chung-rae, a senior DPK lawmaker and the primary target of the smear, has refused to accept a private apology, demanding instead a public admission of guilt and suggesting that Kim should face professional consequences for "irresponsible" factional warfare.
  • Collapsing Support: Recent polling shows the DPK's approval rating has fallen to 35.5%, with a sharp 17-point drop among the party’s core 40-something demographic, largely attributed to public fatigue over these internal power struggles.

The High Price of Factional Paranoia

The "Shincheonji" allegation was never just a minor dispute; it was a weaponized conspiracy theory designed to delegitimize rivals within the party by linking them to one of the most socially stigmatized groups in South Korea. By deploying this "cult" label, Kim Min-seok sought to consolidate power during the leadership race. However, the strategy backfired. Instead of purifying the party, it triggered a "politics of suspicion" that paralyzed the DPK’s legislative agenda and alienated moderate voters who expected the ruling party to focus on the economy rather than internal witch hunts.

The timing of this retreat is driven by survival. With President Lee Jae-myung’s administration facing a dip in support, the DPK cannot afford a prolonged civil war. The 17% drop in support among voters in their 40s—the bedrock of the DPK’s base—is a flashing red light for the leadership. It suggests that even loyalists are losing patience with a party hierarchy that prioritizes factional purity over governance and policy. Kim’s emotional apology, reportedly delivered on the verge of tears, reflects the heavy political toll this failed gambit has taken on his own standing.

Strategic Pivots and the Search for Unity

To distract from this internal mess, the DPK is attempting a rapid pivot back to "hardball" opposition against the People Power Party (PPP), the country’s main conservative opposition. The party leadership has announced plans to fast-track a "Special Prosecutor Act" targeting what they call "fabricated prosecutions" by the investigative authorities. This move is a classic consolidation tactic: when a party is divided from within, it seeks an external enemy to force its members back into a single line. By shifting the focus to legal battles and the judiciary, the DPK hopes to bury the Shincheonji scandal under a new layer of partisan conflict.

However, the path forward remains treacherous. While the party is aggressive on the prosecutor bill, it has notably cooled its rhetoric regarding a probe into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Internal sentiment suggests that the public is wary of the DPK overreaching its authority to pressure the judiciary. If the DPK continues to prioritize these high-stakes political battles while ignoring the "bread and butter" issues that its base actually cares about, this apology will be remembered not as a turning point for unity, but as the moment the party’s internal trust finally evaporated.
