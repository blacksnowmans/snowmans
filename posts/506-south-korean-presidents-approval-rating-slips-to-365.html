South Korean President's Approval Rating Slips to 36.5%

September 30, 2026

  • Falling Support: The President's approval rating has dropped to 36.5%, with disapproval reaching 60.3%, fueled by a loss of confidence among core 40- and 50-year-old voters.
  • Opposition’s Passive Lead: While the conservative People Power Party (PPP) holds a slight lead in party support, analysts suggest this is due to the administration's failures rather than the party's own strategic success.
  • Public Sentiment: A clear majority of the public views recent criticism from prominent political commentator Rhyu Si-min as "constructive" rather than politically motivated, signaling a disconnect between the ruling party and the electorate.

A Warning from the Core Electorate

The latest polling from News Tomato, a local news agency, paints a grim picture for the current administration. The slide in approval is not a temporary dip but a result of accumulated frustration across the board. Most concerning for the government is the erosion of support among voters in their 40s and 50s—traditionally reliable demographics who have now shifted toward a disapproval rating exceeding 50%.

This dissatisfaction has crossed regional and ideological lines. Even in traditionally liberal strongholds, approval ratings are struggling against rising disapproval. With nearly 60% of independent, middle-of-the-road voters now voicing disapproval, the administration is losing the social mandate required for stable governance. The data suggests that citizens are no longer interested in excuses; they are reacting to a perceived lack of progress on the issues that affect their daily lives, such as employment and housing costs.

The Paradox of the Opposition

Perhaps the most ironic aspect of this political landscape is the state of the opposition. Despite frequent gaffes and a lack of clear, innovative policy proposals, the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) remains highly competitive. The ruling PPP’s current lead in party support is widely viewed as a reflection of the public’s exhaustion with the government rather than a genuine surge in enthusiasm for the opposition.

The situation highlights a dangerous cycle: the current administration is failing to address the cost-of-living crisis, yet the opposition remains stuck in internal power struggles rather than presenting a compelling alternative. This has left the electorate in a state of deep cynicism. Unless the ruling bloc can pivot to address substantive economic concerns, it risks complete alienation from a public that is increasingly viewing the entire political class with skepticism.

Rhyu Si-min and the Sound of Dissent

Recent controversy surrounding commentator Rhyu Si-min—who labeled the government's current trajectory a "path to certain failure"—has exposed the ruling party's intolerance for internal critique. While some hardline supporters attacked Rhyu as a "suicide bomber" for his comments, the broader public disagrees. According to the same poll, 58% of citizens view his critique as "constructive," even among voters in traditional conservative strongholds.

When a government treats necessary feedback as an act of betrayal, it effectively closes itself off from reality. The current administration’s refusal to engage with genuine criticism may be its most significant weakness. History shows that when power stops listening to the public, the path to a broader political reckoning usually follows shortly after.
