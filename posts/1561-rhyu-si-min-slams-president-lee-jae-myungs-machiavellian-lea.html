Rhyu Si-min Slams President Lee Jae-myung’s "Machiavellian" Leadership

October 27, 2026

Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal intellectual and former cabinet minister, has launched a blistering critique of President Lee Jae-myung, accusing him of using deceptive "Machiavellian" tactics to consolidate power while abandoning core democratic reforms.

  • Live Direct Action: Rhyu will appear on Park Si-young TV, a popular liberal YouTube news channel, on October 28 to detail the administration’s failures in personnel, policy, and political direction.
  • "Machiavellian" Tactics: Rhyu argues the President delegates "dirty work" or unpopular tasks to subordinates while personally appearing only for popular, choreographed events.
  • Prosecution Reform Betrayal: The critique alleges the current administration is allowing the revival of elite, centralized prosecution units, effectively reversing years of reform efforts.
  • Base Erosion: Recent polling suggests a sharp decline in support for the ruling Democratic Party among its core strongholds, including the Honam region and voters in their 40s and 50s.

The "Machiavellian" Trap and the Prosecution Pivot

Rhyu Si-min, who served as Health Minister under the Roh Moo-hyun administration and remains a highly influential voice among South Korean liberals, is signaling a major rift within the ruling camp. His primary accusation is that President Lee Jae-myung is practicing "Machiavellian politics"—a style of governance where the leader avoids responsibility for controversial decisions while monopolizing public credit. Rhyu contends that this "prince-like" behavior is a relic of the past that cannot survive in a modern democracy where citizens monitor power via social media.

A central point of contention is the handling of the prosecution. Rhyu alleges that the administration has allowed the "Central Investigation Department" style of aggressive, politically motivated prosecution to return. He points to the Sillajen case—a biotech firm involved in a massive fraud scandal—as a primary example. Prosecutors and conservative media previously used the firm’s collapse, involving roughly $95 million (132.5 billion won) in illegal transactions, to attempt to frame Rhyu and other liberal figures. Rhyu argues that by failing to fully separate investigative and prosecutorial powers, the Lee administration is keeping these "cabinet files" active to keep political rivals in check.

Internal Purges and the "Fake Tolerance" Frame

The tension has escalated following the President’s recent "expression of non-punishment" regarding a defamation case against Rhyu. While the President’s office framed this as an act of magnanimity, Rhyu and his supporters view it as a deceptive PR stunt. They argue the original complaint—filed by conservative groups over Rhyu’s criticism of the President—had no legal merit to begin with. By "forgiving" Rhyu, the President effectively framed a legitimate critic as a criminal who was only spared by the leader’s grace.

A House Divided

Rhyu also highlighted what he calls a "reconstruction" strategy within the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party). Instead of expanding the party’s reach, he claims the current leadership is attempting to demolish the party’s historical foundations—the legacies of former Presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—to build a singular power base. This has led to coordinated attacks on veteran party figures, including Representative Jung Chung-rae and former President Moon.

The political fallout is already visible in the numbers. While some telephone surveys show stable support, automated (ARS) polls—often seen as a better gauge of the "active" political base—show the President’s approval rating sliding to around 35%. The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, further suggests that the Democratic Party's traditional base is looking for a more principled alternative to President Lee’s pragmatic, yet increasingly polarizing, governance.
