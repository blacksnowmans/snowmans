The Sleep Crisis: Why South Korea’s "Night Culture" is Broken

September 10, 2026

  • The Core Issue: South Korea is facing a severe sleep crisis, with over 1.3 million people suffering from sleep disorders—a trend driven by excessive smartphone use and post-pandemic irregular sleep patterns.
  • The Medical Reality: Experts warn that "Chungonjeung" (a common Korean term for spring lethargy) is not a medical condition but a mask for chronic sleep debt accumulated over years.
  • The Stakes: Chronic sleep deprivation is not just about fatigue; it prevents the brain from cleaning out toxic proteins, significantly increasing the risk of dementia and long-term cognitive decline.

The Smartphone and Pandemic Effect

Modern South Koreans have spent years treating sleep as a "disposable resource" rather than a physiological necessity. This culture of burning the candle at both ends—fueled by late-night streaming and the glorification of working through fatigue—has hit a wall. According to data from the National Health Insurance Service, over 640,000 people are currently seeking professional medical help for sleep disorders, with women in their 50s being the most affected demographic.

The decline in sleep quality has two major milestones. The first was the proliferation of smartphones (starting around 2007–2010), which turned the bed—a place meant for rest—into a hub for endless digital stimulation. The second was the COVID-19 pandemic, which dismantled physical routines like commuting, cementing irregular sleep-wake cycles that have persisted long after lockdowns ended.

Beyond "Quick Fixes": The Science of Rest

Many turn to folklore or social media trends to fix their sleep, but experts suggest these are often counterproductive. For instance, the "Bladder Method"—drinking 500ml of water before bed to force an early wake-up call—is dismissed by sleep specialists as a dangerous misunderstanding of biology. A tired body needs deep, uninterrupted sleep; forcing yourself awake only worsens the sleep debt.

True restorative sleep relies on a few fundamental, yet often ignored, principles:

  • Brain Cleaning: During deep sleep (slow-wave sleep), cerebrospinal fluid flushes out metabolic waste products that build up during the day. Failing to hit this "golden time" (ideally between 11 p.m. and 1 a.m.) means the brain starts the next day effectively "dirty."
  • The Alcohol Trap: Many use alcohol to fall asleep, but alcohol acts like a "scar" on the brain. While it may induce unconsciousness, it prevents the brain from entering deep, restorative sleep stages, leading to fragmented nights and long-term cognitive impairment.
  • The Power of Myokines: Simple walking isn't enough for those over 40. True rest requires the secretion of myokines—proteins released during vigorous strength training—which play a critical role in stabilizing sleep patterns.

Ultimately, the path to better sleep is boring but effective: cut the alcohol, strictly limit screen time before bed, and engage in strength-based exercise. In an age of high-tech digital sleep aids and, the most "advanced" solution remains the most ancient: aligning our bodies with the natural rhythm of the day.
