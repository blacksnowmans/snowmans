South Korea's Main Opposition Party Mired in Digital Manipulation Scandals and Factional Infighting

September 03, 2026

  • South Korea's main liberal opposition party is facing intense scrutiny over alleged manipulation of its official YouTube channel metrics and internal factional warfare.
  • Leaked Telegram messages revealed that the party's ethics watchdog was weaponized to target political rivals and protect specific factions.
  • The scandals have alienated core supporters, who are increasingly using superchats to voice anger and betrayal rather than offering genuine backing.
  • The turmoil highlights a broader detachment from public concerns, underscored by policy reversals that favor a tiny minority over everyday citizens.

South Korean politics has long blurred the line between drama and farce, but recent scandals within the Democratic Party of Korea—the country's main liberal opposition—have plumbed new depths. Rather than debating policy or addressing the economic anxieties of ordinary citizens, party operatives appear consumed by factional backstabbing, digital metrics manipulation, and internal power struggles. The release of embarrassing text messages has laid bare a political culture driven more by tribalism and survival than public service.

At the heart of the current crisis is the party's official digital outreach. Metrics from the Democratic Party's dedicated media channels—showing suspiciously inflated viewer numbers and rapid-fire "likes"—have raised eyebrows across South Korea's digital landscape, pointing to orchestrated engagement rather than organic support. Meanwhile, donations via superchats, ostensibly a tool for cheering on creators, have transformed into digital blunt instruments. Disillusioned supporters are using financial contributions not to encourage the party, but to send desperate, angry warnings demanding that leadership wake up.

Ethics Watchdogs Turned Factional Weapons

Perhaps the most damaging revelation involves the party’s ethics inspection unit. Designed to be a neutral, cold arbiter of party standards, leaked Telegram messages exposed how the body was co-opted as a private political blade. Discussions centered on how to "neutralize" and "seize control" of figures linked to rival internal factions. While leadership has scrambled to cut ties and dismiss officials involved, the damage is done. When an internal watchdog becomes a tool for purging political enemies, the entire apparatus loses its moral authority, leaving rank-and-file members convinced that any future disciplinary actions are just disguised hit jobs.

Losing the Plot on Policy and Public Trust

This inward-looking obsession with factional survival has exacted a heavy toll on policymaking. A recent high-profile about-face on property tax policies—scrapped in less than a month—vividly illustrated a party adrift. By scrambling to appease the top 3% of asset holders while ignoring the crushing economic reality facing the other 97%, the party abandoned its foundational commitment to economic fairness. Slogans about "party member sovereignty" ring hollow when ordinary members are treated merely as disposable shock troops for internal turf wars rather than the true owners of the political movement.

Ultimately, these scandals point to a systemic rot. South Korean voters are far too sophisticated to be permanently fooled by manufactured digital enthusiasm and petty messaging wars. If the opposition hopes to regain public trust, it must log off from partisan Telegram chats, dismantle its factional machinery, and refocus on the hard work of representing the economic well-being of the public.
