Controversy Dogs Inaugural Serious Crimes Chief Nominee in Confirmation Grilling

October 14, 2026

Kim Ji-yong, tapped to lead South Korea's newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, faced fierce scrutiny during his National Assembly confirmation hearing over his record of approving controversial, politically motivated prosecutions during his career as a senior prosecutor.

  • Amnesia under questioning: Pressed by Representative Yoon Kun-young of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, Kim initially claimed he could not recall approving the questionable indictment of former prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong—a case widely viewed as shielding conservative figure Han Dong-hoon—before reversing himself under pressure and admitting he had signed off.
  • A trail of acquittals: Lawmakers pressed Kim on key cases he oversaw, including the indictment of Jeong and charges brought over an emergency travel ban placed on disgraced former Vice Justice Minister Kim Hak-ui; both prosecutions were ultimately thrown out with final acquittals by the Supreme Court.
  • Selective justice allegations: Representative Jeong Chun-saeng of the progressive Rebuilding Korea Party attacked Kim for double standards, highlighting how his department prosecuted former presidential secretary Lee Kwang-cheol while shielding internal prosecutor Yoon Dae-jin under identical circumstances.
  • Reform contradictions: Critics noted the irony that Kim, nominated under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung to lead an agency created specifically to strip prosecutors of direct investigative powers, had previously signed joint statements opposing those very reforms and defending Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol in 2020.

A Flawed Record Meets Judicial Reform

The creation of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety represents the culmination of South Korea's long push to separate investigative power from indictment, ending the prosecutorial monopoly on criminal justice. Installing a figure who spent years at the core of that monopoly raises fundamental questions about institutional intent. Kim's shifting testimony during the hearing—moving from claiming complete memory loss to arguing that he had internally opposed indictments he nevertheless formally signed—displayed the classic evasion tactics of the elite prosecutorial corps rather than the transparency expected of a reform leader.

Moreover, Kim's defense that he simply remained silent during internal investigations because "no one asked" for his objections speaks volumes about organizational complicity. When senior officials refuse to resist political weaponization within their ranks, selective prosecutions inevitably follow. As lawmakers pointed out, individuals spent years enduring grueling legal battles over charges that the Supreme Court ultimately dismissed as groundless, while the officials who signed those indictment sheets advanced their careers unchecked.

Accountability in the Post-Yoon Era

Significantly, the most damaging cross-examination came not from the main conservative opposition People Power Party—which dismissed the clash as internal squabbling—but from the ruling Democratic Party and its allies. With Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol now imprisoned following his impeachment and removal from office, the political appetite to completely eradicate weaponized prosecutorial practices is at an all-time high among the governing coalition. Kim's reluctant concessions on the hearing floor underline the lingering shadow of that era and signal that the Lee Jae-myung administration will face fierce internal pushback if it attempts to staff reform institutions with figures tied to past abuses.
