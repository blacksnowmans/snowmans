The Hidden Decline of Korea’s Luxury Mixed-Use Skyscrapers

October 04, 2026

South Korea’s iconic “jusam-bokhap”—luxury high-rise buildings that combine residential apartments with commercial facilities—are losing their status as the nation's premier real estate investments as they age.

  • Falling Rankings: Early prestige premiums have vanished; luxury high-rises are now being overtaken by traditional, spacious apartment complexes in market value.
  • The Land-Share Trap: Because these buildings are constructed on commercial land with high floor-area ratios (often 700%–800%), residents own very little land share per unit, making long-term redevelopment mathematically unfeasible.
  • Lifestyle Mismatches: As these buildings reach 15–20 years of age, their structural limitations—such as a lack of green space and proximity to commercial entertainment districts—are driving families toward more traditional, gated residential complexes.

The Structural Illusion of Value

When high-rises like the Tower Palace in Dogok-dong—once the ultimate symbol of Korean wealth—first appeared, they represented a "new world" of convenience. However, real estate value is fundamentally split between the building (the structure) and the land. While a building’s value depreciates as it ages, the land’s value typically grows with inflation. In mixed-use high-rises, the building structure accounts for 70–80% of the property’s total value, meaning the asset "shrinks" significantly as the structure becomes obsolete. Unlike traditional apartment complexes, where land ownership is substantial, the tiny land-share in these high-rises offers no "anchor" to support long-term price growth.

History is already proving this trend. In 2006, the high-rise Acro Vista in Seocho-dong was priced nearly twice as high as the nearby Sampoong Apartments. As the "new-construction" shine wore off, the market corrected itself. Today, the prices for these two have converged, with the traditional apartment complex showing far more resilient growth. The same phenomenon is playing out across the country: once a mixed-use building hits the 10-year mark, it consistently loses its "top-tier" status to traditional complexes that offer better landscaping, space for children, and genuine redevelopment potential.

The Future: From Status Symbol to Niche Living

While the investment case for aging mixed-use high-rises is weak, they are not without their merits. They offer superior security, high-speed elevator infrastructure, and "all-in-one" convenience that many traditional complexes lack. This has led to a shift in their target demographic: they are increasingly becoming the choice for single professionals, DINK (double income, no kids) couples, and retirees who prioritize proximity to city-center jobs over school districts or large parks.

The notable exception remains projects like Acro Seoul Forest in Seongsu-dong, which commands sky-high prices despite being a mixed-use structure. However, experts point out this is due to its "cheat code" location—permanent, unobstructed views of the Seoul Forest and the Han River—rather than the building’s inherent construction quality. For most investors, the lesson is clear: do not be dazzled by a fancy lobby or a panoramic view. In the long run, the value of a home is tied to the land it stands on, not the height of its floors.
