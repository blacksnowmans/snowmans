South Korea’s Rebuilding Korea Party Hits Record 9% Support in Polling Surge

October 22, 2026

Support for the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party has broken the 9% threshold for the first time, signaling a notable shift in South Korea’s traditionally rigid two-party landscape.

  • Record numbers: A mid-October Media Tomato survey placed the Rebuilding Korea Party at 9.0%, positioning it behind the main conservative opposition People Power Party (37.2%) and the ruling liberal Democratic Party (36.2%).
  • Demographic strongholds: Support jumped to 16.3% among voters in their 40s and reached 17.8% among self-identified progressives, up 7 percentage points in a single week.
  • Regional impact: The party is emerging as a key variable in competitive swing areas, including the Seoul metropolitan region and the central Chungcheong provinces.
  • Key drivers: Voter impatience with traditional political compromises and recent pushback against heavy-handed legal actions against government critics have fueled demand for a sharper progressive alternative.

A Two-Track Strategy for the Progressive Camp

The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party highlights a growing tactical split among liberal voters. While the ruling Democratic Party maintains the majority of liberal support at 61.2%, Cho Kuk’s party capturing 17.8% of progressives indicates that a significant segment of the left wants a clearer, more uncompromising reform agenda. Rather than simply stealing votes from the ruling party, this "two-track" alignment appears to expand the broader progressive base by capturing voters frustrated with standard party politics.

Voters in their 40s—a critical economic demographic that traditionally anchors the liberal base—are driving this movement. Their 16.3% support for the third party shows a willingness to split their choices when the main ruling party appears overly cautious. In tight battlegrounds across Seoul and surrounding urban centers where margins of victory are razor-thin, a sustained 9% third-party bloc could completely alter future electoral strategy.

Historical Hurdles and the Quest for Sustainability

South Korean political history is filled with third parties that surged briefly before being swallowed up by the major conservative and liberal platforms. Minor parties that relied exclusively on temporary public frustration or personal brand recognition rarely built lasting power. For the Rebuilding Korea Party to survive beyond a temporary protest vote, it must establish clear policy agendas and organizational depth that set it apart from the mainstream Democratic Party.

Meanwhile, political debates surrounding overzealous legal complaints against critics of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration demonstrate why voters are seeking additional checks on power. When political friction leads to legal suppression rather than open debate, moderate and progressive voters often seek out third-party alternatives. How the establishment responds to these shifting dynamics will determine whether this polling surge is a fleeting wave or a lasting realignment of Korean politics.
