South Korea’s Ruling Party Pushes Property Tax Relief Ahead of Elections

November 10, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea is pushing to raise the property tax exemption threshold to 1.4 billion won ($1.05 million) to ease burdens on homeowners ahead of upcoming parliamentary elections, creating a rare policy rift with President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

  • What happened: Lawmakers in the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) are advocating for raising the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax exemption cap for single-home owners to 1.4 billion won ($1.05 million), with proposals to include non-resident owners.
  • The political rift: President Lee Jae-myung’s administration remains cautious, seeking to protect policy consistency and avoid accusations of handing out tax cuts to high-income homeowners.
  • Market impact: Housing supply in Seoul’s prime districts is drying up as owners hold onto assets expecting lower taxes, while plans to lift Land Transaction Permit Zones in suburban cities like Gwacheon and Bundang threaten to reignite speculative buying.
  • Who is affected: High-value single-property owners stand to gain substantial relief—taxes on a non-resident home officially assessed at 2 billion won ($1.5 million) could drop from 5.3 million won ($4,000) to 1.9 million won ($1,425)—while renters and prospective first-time buyers face renewed price pressure.

Political Calculus Over Tax Principles

The push for tax cuts highlights how property policy in South Korea is increasingly dictated by election strategy. DPK lawmakers representing the key Seoul metropolitan area are eager to placate middle-class voters who feel squeezed by housing levies. A legislative faction of roughly 20 DPK lawmakers is pushing a pragmatic approach centered on lowering overall housing costs rather than aggressively suppressing property values. However, the main opposition conservative People Power Party (PPP) has slammed the effort as short-sighted electioneering while focusing its attacks on government errors rather than proposing concrete alternatives.

At the same time, tax policy consistency is suffering. The government has frozen the official property valuation ratio—the base metric for real estate taxes and health insurance premiums—for four consecutive years. While this freeze prevents sudden tax spikes for ordinary residents, critics argue it distorts tax fairness by allowing official assessments to lag far behind actual market prices. The valuation system has effectively turned into a political lever managed around approval ratings.

Deregulatory Signals and Market Uncertainty

Compounding market confusion is the potential lifting of Land Transaction Permit Zones in key satellite cities like Gwacheon and Bundang by late December. Removing these designations would allow gap investing—purchasing properties with existing tenant leases—reducing the upfront cash needed to buy a 1.2 billion won ($900,000) home by roughly 180 million won ($135,000). While intended to revive sluggish suburban markets, critics warn it will simply invite cash-rich speculators back into the market.

Though the government is pitching pragmatic alternatives, such as a profit-sharing public housing model that requires buyers to put up only 30% of the property value, consumer confidence remains shaky. As long as tax and regulatory policy changes are driven by short-term political calculations rather than stable planning, home buyers and sellers alike are likely to sit on the sidelines.
