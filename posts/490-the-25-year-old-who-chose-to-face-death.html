The 25-Year-Old Who Chose to Face Death

September 30, 2026

A recent viral account from a young funeral director in South Korea is challenging the common instinct to shy away from the finality of life, framing the profession not as a grim chore, but as a deeply personal act of love.

  • The Incident: At 25, a young man was invited to observe an embalming process. Overwhelmed by the visceral reality of death, he fled the facility, only to return after witnessing the raw grief of the family.
  • The Turning Point: Seeing the family’s desperate attempt to connect with their loved one, the narrator realized he did not want to leave his own parents' final moments to a stranger.
  • The Shift: He committed to becoming a funeral director specifically to ensure he could personally care for his parents when their time comes.

From Terror to Vocation

In modern South Korea, death is often compartmentalized. It is sanitized, kept behind the cold steel doors of hospital morgues and professional funeral homes. For most, this distance is a relief. When the young man first stepped into the morgue, his reaction was purely primal; the sights and smells triggered an immediate fight-or-flight response, and he spent an hour outside, debating whether to quit forever.

What brought him back wasn't professional ambition, but the sight of the family mourning. Seeing them touch, talk to, and plead with the deceased made him realize that "distance" is not the same as peace. His fear transformed into a quiet resolve: he didn't want to be the person standing on the sidelines of his own family’s tragedy. He wanted to be the one capable of providing dignity to those he loved most.

Redefining Dignity

The funeral director’s story serves as a mirror for our own avoidance of mortality. We treat funeral directors as people who perform a "3D job"—difficult, dirty, and dangerous—yet we rely on them to bridge the impossible gap between the living and the dead. By choosing to learn these skills for the sake of his parents, the narrator strips away the professional clinical veneer and restores the ancient, familial tradition of seeing one’s kin through to the end.

This is a powerful reminder that our attitude toward death defines how we live. By refusing to let his fear dictate his actions, this young man found a way to turn the most daunting human experience into an act of profound devotion. It suggests that perhaps the greatest regret at life’s end is not the fear of death itself, but the fear that prevented us from saying a proper goodbye.
