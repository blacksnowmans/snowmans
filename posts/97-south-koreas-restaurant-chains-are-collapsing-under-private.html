South Korea’s Restaurant Chains are Collapsing Under Private Equity and Inflation

September 27, 2026

  • The crisis: South Korea's dining and franchise sector is undergoing a massive wave of bankruptcies, driven by soaring material costs, minimum wage hikes, and aggressive corporate expansion.
  • Familiar names hit: Legacy brands like the Korean-style stew giant Nolboo have filed for court receivership, while burger chains and kimbap shops see rapid store closures.
  • The private equity factor: Analysts and franchisees point to private equity firms and founders engaging in "exit strategies," cashing out through quick sales while leaving mounting debts and legal liabilities to franchisees.
  • Who loses: Local small business owners face financial ruin, while corporate headquarters and investors prioritize short-term profit margins and executive dividends.

Walking through any neighborhood in South Korea right now, it is nearly impossible to miss the yellow "For Rent" signs pasted across shuttered storefronts. Nearly one million self-employed small business owners are either weighing closure or have already pulled down their shutters. Once considered bulletproof investments, family-run snack bars, franchise restaurants, and legendary domestic dining brands are crumbling under the weight of an unforgiving economic climate.

The collapse of iconic brands like Nolboo—the 1990s comfort food giant famous for popularizing bossam (boiled pork belly) and budae jjigae (army stew)—illustrates the systemic rot. After changing hands through multiple private equity firms, including Morgan Stanley Private Equity, Nolboo expanded into 16 chaotic sub-brands without strengthening its core business. By mid-2026, crushed by plunging sales and an 82% capital impairment rate, the company filed for corporate rehabilitation. Meanwhile, neighborhood kimbap (rice roll) shops are caught in an impossible squeeze: the price of dried seaweed has quadrupled since 2022, and South Korea's minimum wage has surpassed 10,300 won (about $7.50) per hour, forcing thousands of mom-and-pop stores out of business as they lose customers to cheap convenience store alternatives.

Private Equity 'Exit Strategies' Leave Franchisees Holding the Bag

The crisis is not just about inflation; it is about corporate exploitation. Major burger franchises like Frank Burger expanded past 700 locations by heavily overpromising profit margins to prospective owners. South Korea's Fair Trade Commission later penalized the company, revealing that actual operating profits for store owners were a meager 3%, compared to the 28% advertised by headquarters. Courts have since ordered hefty compensation payouts to defrauded franchisees.

Yet, instead of fixing broken supply chains or supporting struggling store owners, founders and private equity backers are rushing to cash out. Frank Burger’s parent company pushed to sell its stake for roughly 50 billion won (about $36 million) amid ongoing lawsuits and plunging revenues. A similar fate hit Shabu All Day, an all-you-can-eat shabu-shabu brand that scaled to 170 locations before being acquired by the Philippine food giant Jollibee for 130 billion won (about $95 million). Within months of the buyout, raw material costs spiked and over twenty locations shut down, leaving franchisees buried in high-interest debt while the parent company engineered a clean exit.

Even Ediya Coffee, a domestic coffee pioneer with over 4,000 locations, is struggling in the brutal middle ground between ultra-cheap chains like Mega Coffee and premium giants like Starbucks. As its operating cash flow turned negative and short-term debt surged to 54.3 billion won (about $39 million), the company still managed to increase shareholder dividends to 9.5 billion won (about $7 million) in 2025. When foreign private equity and domestic conglomerates treat dining brands purely as financial assets to be squeezed, the human cost falls entirely on local franchisees risking their life savings.
