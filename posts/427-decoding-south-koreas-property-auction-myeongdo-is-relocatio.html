Decoding South Korea’s Property Auction "Myeongdo": Is Relocation Money Legally Required?

September 08, 2026

  • What happened: Successfully winning a property at a South Korean court auction does not automatically grant immediate possession; buyers must navigate "myeongdo"—the complex process of clearing out the previous occupant.
  • What it changes: New property owners often face demands for "relocation money" (*isabi*) from former owners or tenants, forcing a high-stakes psychological and financial standoff.
  • Who is affected: Real estate investors and auction bidders in South Korea who must decide whether to pay off occupants or pursue costly, time-consuming legal evictions.

For foreign investors looking at South Korea's bustling real estate market, court auctions (*gyeongmae*) offer a tempting route to below-market properties. But winning the bid is only half the battle. The real hurdle begins the moment ownership is registered: the grueling process of clearing out the property, known locally as *myeongdo*. While newcomers often assume the new owner is legally obligated to pay the departing occupant moving expenses, South Korean law requires no such thing. Relocation money is not a legal right; it is a negotiation tactic.

Legally speaking, former owners, debtors, and junior tenants with no priority rights have zero legal standing to resist a new owner. Yet, offering a modest payout is standard practice among seasoned investors. Why? Because eviction battles are brutal. If an owner refuses to negotiate and forces a legal eviction, they must prepay court execution costs ranging from 3 million to 7 million won (roughly $2,200 to $5,200), not to mention months of lost mortgage interest on loans that often run into hundreds of millions of won. Furthermore, bitter occupants have been known to intentionally trash properties—pouring concrete down drains or destroying plumbing—turning a minor inconvenience into a massive repair bill.

The Playbook: Legal Leverage Meets Cold Calculation

Successfully clearing a property requires a strict "legal action first, negotiation second" philosophy. The moment an investor pays the final balance on an auction win, they must immediately file for a formal "possession order" (*indo myeongryeong*)—the most powerful legal tool available to a buyer. Once the court issues this order and a formal warning notice (*gyegojang*) is slapped on the front door, 90% of stubborn occupants wave the white flag out of sheer psychological pressure.

When it comes to calculating the right amount of relocation money, buyers must ignore the occupant's inflated demands and instead use potential legal expenses as their ceiling. A smart investor typically offers 50% to 70% of what a forced eviction would cost, keeping payouts to a reasonable 2 million to 3 million won range (about $1,500 to $2,200). Crucially, seasoned investors never hand over cash in advance. Payment must strictly follow the principle of simultaneous exchange: the keys are handed over only after the occupant clears out every last belonging and the property is inspected.

Ultimately, clearing an auction property in South Korea is not an exercise in charity or emotional warfare—it is pure risk management. By combining the unyielding sword of the law with cold, calculated pragmatism, investors can protect their assets, minimize downtime, and secure their newly acquired properties without losing their sanity.
