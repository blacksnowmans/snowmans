South Korea Passes Landmark Democracy Merit Act Amid Opposition Boycott

November 22, 2026

On November 21, South Korea’s National Assembly passed a long-contested bill granting official state honors and benefits to pro-democracy activists, following a 24-hour filibuster and a total boycott by the conservative opposition.

  • What happened: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) unilaterally passed the Democracy Merit Act with 178 votes in favor out of 178 lawmakers present, after members of the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) walked out in protest.
  • What it changes: The law grants official state recognition to individuals who were killed, injured, or went missing while protesting past authoritarian dictatorships, providing their families with modest medical assistance and state support for memorial projects.
  • Who is affected: Relatives and surviving victims of South Korea's historical pro-democracy movements gain long-delayed legal honor, while partisan hostility deepens between the ruling and opposition blocs.
  • The backdrop: The legislation had stalled for years due to repeated presidential vetoes during Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency, but regained momentum under the current Lee Jae-myung administration.

A Fierce Clash Over Privilege and History

The passage of the bill highlights a deep ideological rift in South Korean politics. The conservative opposition PPP aggressively slammed the measure as a "self-serving privilege bill" designed for former 1980s student activists, claiming it squanders taxpayer money on retirement safety nets for ruling-party allies. DPK lawmakers, including National Assembly committee chair Shin Jeong-hoon, strongly rejected these accusations. They clarified that rumors of extravagant perks—such as preferential university admissions or unchecked financial grants—are entirely false, emphasizing that candidates will undergo strict screening by a state veterans' review committee to exclude serious criminal offenders.

Political Fallout and Historical Framing

By pushing the legislation through, the DPK aims to settle an enduring historical grievance and solidify state recognition for those who risked their lives to dismantle military rule. Under Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency, executive vetoes repeatedly thwarted earlier iterations of the act, deepening frustration among democracy advocates and victims' families. With those presidential vetoes now a thing of the past under the Lee Jae-myung administration, the DPK successfully fulfilled a key legislative objective.

However, the manner of the bill's passage guarantees ongoing political friction. Because the vote proceeded without opposition participation, the PPP continues to attack the ruling party for steamrolling legislation. While the law marks a major milestone in codifying state respect for South Korea's democratic pioneers, the political battle over its narrative and implementation is far from over.
