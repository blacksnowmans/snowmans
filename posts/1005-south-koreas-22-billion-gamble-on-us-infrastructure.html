South Korea’s $22 Billion Gamble on U.S. Infrastructure

September 22, 2026

South Korea is currently navigating a high-stakes economic crisis as it faces immense pressure to commit 30 trillion won (about $22 billion) to U.S. infrastructure projects, primarily a massive gas power plant in Texas, amid threats of sweeping American tariffs.

  • What happened: Faced with U.S. President Donald Trump’s protectionist trade threats, the South Korean government signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) to invest $20 billion in the U.S., which has since ballooned into a $22 billion (30 trillion won) project in Texas.
  • What it changes: The deal risks draining South Korea’s foreign exchange reserves and tying up massive capital in a project with no guaranteed tenants or stable electricity sales contracts, potentially threatening national economic stability.
  • Who is affected: South Korean taxpayers and the nation’s overall economic health are at risk, as the government is prioritizing American political demands over long-term commercial viability.

The Price of "Alliance"

The core of the issue is a fundamental power imbalance. While the U.S. frames these capital outflows as "investments," critics argue the structure is designed to extract Korean capital to bolster the U.S. domestic economy ahead of election cycles. With the U.S. refusing to adopt "risk pooling"—a standard financial strategy to share the burden of massive infrastructure projects—Korean firms are being forced to shoulder the entire financial weight of the 6.3-gigawatt Texas power plant project alone.

The financial math is equally troubling. Even with an optimistic projection of earning 60 trillion won over 20 years, the return on investment is roughly 6%—a figure that fails to account for current U.S. inflation and soaring long-term Treasury yields. When combined with the lack of stable Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) to guarantee electricity prices, the project appears less like a sound investment and more like a political favor that ignores basic market logic.

Media Complicity and the Need for Sovereignty

A significant portion of the South Korean press has exacerbated the situation by acting as an unofficial mouthpiece for U.S. interests. By framing any pushback against American demands as a threat to the "alliance" that could lead to catastrophe, these outlets have effectively stifled the necessary public and legislative oversight. Instead of questioning why the government is prioritizing the appeasement of U.S. political needs over the protection of its own treasury, the media has fostered a climate of fear.

True diplomacy requires mutual respect, not one-sided sacrifice. If the South Korean government continues to prioritize political optics over the "commercial rationality" of its investments, it risks repeating the errors that led to past economic crises. To safeguard its future, Seoul must shift from a posture of quiet compliance to one that demands concrete, legally binding protections for its capital, ensuring that "alliance" remains a partnership of equals rather than an instrument of economic exploitation.
