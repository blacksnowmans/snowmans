South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Mounting Internal Friction Ahead of Crucial Elections

November 10, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is gripped by widening friction with the presidential office as anxieties mount over upcoming parliamentary elections, where an opposition supermajority could expose President Lee Jae-myung to impeachment.

  • The existential stakes: If the conservative opposition, led by the People Power Party, captures 200 or more seats in the 300-member National Assembly, it would clear the constitutional threshold to pass a presidential impeachment motion.
  • A passive presidential office: President Lee’s administration is clinging to a top-down relationship with the party, adopting a wait-and-see stance on the assumption that next year’s budget rollout, economic stimulus, and stock market stabilization will naturally reverse sluggish approval ratings.
  • Leadership and messaging confusion: Democratic Party Chairman Kim Min-seok faces high public disapproval, while mixed signals—from backroom talks on party realignments to sharp, cryptic messages posted directly to the president's personal X account—continue to confuse supporters and alienate moderate voters.
  • Stalled policy and judicialized disputes: Paralyzed by disputes between party factions over key issues like the comprehensive real estate holding tax, the party has repeatedly failed to resolve internal scandals through its own committees, leaving disputes to prosecutorial investigations instead.

The Flawed Parallel of Past Administrative Triumphs

President Lee’s lingering optimism appears rooted in his tenure as governor of Gyeonggi Province, South Korea’s most populous province surrounding Seoul. During that period, Lee repeatedly overcame political rough patches through decisive administrative action and visible policy execution. But managing national politics is fundamentally different from running a local government: it involves macroeconomic head winds, structural legislative gridlock, and complex legal conflicts that cannot simply be managed by waiting for economic indicators to tick upward.

Pundits often compare the Democratic Party’s current predicament to the 19th general election, when the ruling conservative Saenuri Party retained a 152-seat majority despite then-President Lee Myung-bak’s dismal 27% approval rating. That turnaround was achieved only because an emergency committee led by Park Geun-hye aggressively purged established party insiders, distanced the party from the unpopular administration, and courted swing voters. Today’s Democratic Party shows little sign of that ruthless self-discipline, opting instead for temporary band-aids.

Factional Feuds and the Collapse of Self-Correction

Rather than rallying around a coherent governing platform, factions within the party remain deadlocked. Self-styled "New Lee" loyalists have largely focused their energy on attacking rival intra-party factions—such as figures tied to the previous Moon Jae-in administration—rather than holding the current leadership accountable or clarifying key policies like property tax reform. The leadership's hesitancy leaves middle-class and swing voters fatigued by ideological posturing while everyday cost-of-living concerns go unaddressed.

Worse, the party has increasingly abdicated its capacity for internal dispute resolution. In-house investigative panels dominated by party leadership allies have repeatedly dragged their feet and minimized controversies, pushing internal disputes out of the political arena and directly into the hands of law enforcement. This "judicialization of politics" deepens internal division and reinforces voter perceptions of political hypocrisy. Without an overhaul of its top-down governing style and a disciplined appeal to the political center, the ruling camp risks stumbling into an electoral disaster of its own making.
