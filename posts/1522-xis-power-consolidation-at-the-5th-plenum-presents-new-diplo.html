Xi’s Power Consolidation at the 5th Plenum Presents New Diplomatic Math for South Korea

October 27, 2026

The Chinese Communist Party has opened its 20th Central Committee’s Fifth Plenum in Beijing, prioritizing intense internal discipline and high-level purges to formalize President Xi Jinping’s path to a fourth term over urgent economic stimulus.

  • Unprecedented purges: An extraordinary 85 senior cadres and military officials were absent from the plenum due to sweeping anti-corruption crackdowns across the party and armed forces.
  • Ideology over economic relief: Despite severe domestic economic slowdowns and a real estate crisis, Beijing prioritized strict party discipline and ideological loyalty over market-oriented stimulus packages.
  • Mao-style single-leader rule: The meeting marks a shift toward absolute, centralized leadership, effectively dismantling long-standing collective leadership mechanisms.
  • Diplomatic stakes for Seoul: China's heightened internal volatility and aggressive nationalism increase security and supply-chain risks for South Korean President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

Ideology Takes Center Stage Over Economic Reality

Instead of addressing market anxieties surrounding China's sluggish growth and real estate distress, the Fifth Plenum opened with explicit calls for "comprehensive and strict" party management. The absence of 85 high-ranking officials underscores how aggressively Xi has wielded anti-corruption investigations to clean out potential dissent across top civilian and military posts, including key figures in the Rocket Force. Facing domestic dissatisfaction and intensifying competition with the United States, Beijing has chosen to manage its domestic troubles through fear, total obedience, and ideological control rather than pragmatic economic reform.

What China’s Internal Shakeup Means for Seoul

A Chinese regime relying on internal crackdowns and heightened nationalist rhetoric creates immediate spillover risks for East Asia. Rising friction in the Taiwan Strait and volatile regional supply chains mean South Korea cannot treat Beijing's power restructuring as a distant affair. For President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, navigating this environment requires a sharp departure from the rigid, ideologically tilted foreign policy errors seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency. Seoul must execute a pragmatically balanced foreign policy—protecting national security and economic interests while maintaining stable diplomatic channels with its neighbor.
