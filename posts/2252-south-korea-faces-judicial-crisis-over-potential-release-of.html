South Korea Faces Judicial Crisis over Potential Release of Dec. 3 Insurrection Figure

November 12, 2026

South Korea's judicial system faces intense public backlash as former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo—sentenced to 15 years in prison for his role in the Dec. 3 insurrection—nears potential release on Nov. 20 due to Supreme Court trial delays.

  • Insurrectionist Release: Former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo could be released as Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae’s Supreme Court delays rulings and refers his case to the En Banc bench, sparking outrage over alleged judicial favoritism toward figures from Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's disgraced administration.
  • Judicial Double Standards: Critics point out that while the Supreme Court reviewed 70,000 pages of records in just nine days during the 2025 presidential election period, it held only one hearing in three months for the Dec. 3 insurrection trials.
  • Impeachment Push: Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the minor opposition Cho Kuk Innovation Party are urging the DPK leadership to impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, accusing the court of sabotaging lower-court judges like presiding judge Lee Jin-kwan.
  • US Economic Pressure: Concurrently, newly elected US House Majority Leader Jim Jordan has published a congressional report accusing South Korea of targeting American companies like Coupang, raising fears of renewed US tariff threats.

Selective Justice and the Push to Impeach the Chief Justice

The potential release of Han Duck-soo represents a severe rupture in South Korea’s judicial credibility. The Supreme Court under Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae has systematically sidelined firm lower-court judges. For instance, the high court has sat on a recusal motion involving former Deputy Prime Minister Choi Sang-mok for five months, effectively blocking presiding judge Lee Jin-kwan from proceeding. By transferring Han’s case to the En Banc bench past its legal deadline, the court is widely suspected of attempting to dismantle his 15-year sentence by re-evaluating the Cabinet meetings held during the coup attempt.

Despite clear signs of judicial stalling, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea remains hampered by internal hesitation. DPK Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok and lawmaker Lee Seong-yoon have openly criticized party leadership for hiding behind "caution" instead of utilizing constitutional impeachment powers. Allowing key orchestrators of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s failed December 3 martial law declaration to walk free risks signaling to the military and public that insurrection carries few long-term consequences.

External Vulnerabilities Amid Domestic Turmoil

This domestic judicial crisis coincides with mounting external threats to South Korea's economic security. The election of US House Majority Leader Jim Jordan—a hardline ally of Donald Trump who previously supported 25% tariffs on South Korean exports—presents an immediate challenge. Jordan’s House Judiciary report specifically accuses Seoul of discriminating against American firms like e-commerce giant Coupang while favoring domestic and Chinese joint ventures.

With Washington engulfed in its own political battles—including the US House referring an impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee—South Korea cannot afford internal paralysis. If President Lee Jae-myung's administration and the ruling DPK fail to decisively resolve domestic judicial obstruction, South Korea risks entering critical trade and diplomatic negotiations with a weakened stance.
