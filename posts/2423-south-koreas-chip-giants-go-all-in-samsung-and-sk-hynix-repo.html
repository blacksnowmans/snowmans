South Korea’s Chip Giants Go All-In: Samsung and SK Hynix Report Record Spending to Secure AI Dominance

November 17, 2026

Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix have poured a combined $63 billion into production facilities through the first three quarters of 2024, a record-breaking investment aimed at cementing their lead in the global artificial intelligence (AI) memory market.

  • Samsung Electronics, the world’s largest memory chipmaker, saw its cumulative nine-month revenue surpass 500 trillion won (about $360 billion) for the first time, with its semiconductor unit contributing 98% of total operating profit.
  • SK Hynix, South Korea's second-largest chipmaker and a key Nvidia supplier, reported that U.S. sales now account for 62.5% of its revenue, largely driven by an estimated $25 billion (35 trillion won) in sales to the American AI powerhouse.
  • The massive spending is fueling a "trickle-down" effect for the broader ecosystem; KCTech, a domestic equipment maker, secured a $50 million (69.9 billion won) contract, while specialized supplier Unisem reported a 632% jump in operating profit.
  • SK Hynix committed $1.05 billion to strengthening the domestic supply chain, centered around the 2027 opening of the "Trinity Fab," a dedicated testbed for local suppliers in the Yongin Semiconductor Cluster, a massive industrial complex being built south of Seoul.

The Capital Wall Strategy

Samsung and SK Hynix are effectively building a "capital wall" to keep competitors at bay. By spending a combined $63 billion (88 trillion won) on facilities and $37 billion (52 trillion won) on R&D in just nine months, the two giants are making it nearly impossible for trailing rivals to catch up in the high-stakes race for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). However, this aggressive expansion comes with risks. Samsung’s dependence on semiconductors for 98% of its profit means its bottom line is extremely sensitive to memory price fluctuations. Furthermore, rising raw material costs—which grew by over $7 billion for Samsung this year—could squeeze margins if the current AI-driven price surge begins to plateau.

SK Hynix’s pivot toward the U.S. market is equally striking. Its revenue from U.S. clients increased by roughly $73 billion in a single year, a direct result of the frantic demand for AI servers. While this has led to record-breaking profits, it also leaves the company's fate tethered to the capital expenditure budgets of a handful of North American Big Tech firms. Any "cooling off" in AI infrastructure investment in Silicon Valley would be felt immediately in the SK Hynix ledger.

The Local Ecosystem Rebounds

For the first time since the post-pandemic slump, the recovery is reaching South Korea’s "So-Bu-Jang" sector—an acronym for materials, components, and equipment. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) that provide the specialized tools for chipmaking are finally seeing the rewards of the giants' massive capital expenditure. KCTech’s new contract with SK Hynix represents nearly 20% of its total annual revenue, and Gigavis, which makes inspection equipment, secured a Chinese contract worth 85% of its total sales from last year.

This broadening of the recovery suggests that the "AI boom" is no longer confined to the top-tier manufacturers. The planned Trinity Fab in Yongin will further institutionalize this relationship, allowing local vendors to verify their technology on-site in a real production environment before attempting to go global. For the Korean economy, which is heavily weighted toward this single industry, the 3Q data confirms that the bet on AI is currently paying off across the entire value chain, despite the rising costs of doing business.
