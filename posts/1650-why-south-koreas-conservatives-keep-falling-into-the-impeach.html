Why South Korea’s Conservatives Keep Falling into the Impeachment Trap

October 29, 2026

South Korea’s conservative movement faces a profound structural crisis following the unprecedented impeachments of both former President Park Geun-hye and Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, driven by an inability to abandon obsolete governance tactics.

  • Repeated constitutional turmoil: The People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, suffered catastrophic damage after twice seeing its leaders removed from office—most recently through the insurrection scandal surrounding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • Illusion of political recovery: Conservative leaders risk misinterpreting brief dips in public approval for the ruling liberal camp as a sign of voter forgiveness, mistaking temporary rebounds for actual political redemption.
  • The Toynbee parallel: Applying historian Arnold Toynbee’s framework, the conservative leadership devolved from a "creative minority" solving social challenges into a "dominant minority" blinded by its past success.
  • Permanent voter realignment: The public’s decisive rejection of authoritarian tendencies shows that reliance on top-down control no longer works in modern South Korean democracy.

A Pattern of Ruin: From Park Geun-hye to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol

The fall of South Korea’s conservative establishment is not a series of accidents, but a structural outcome. When former President Park Geun-hye was impeached in 2017, the conservative faction scrambled to limit political fallout. However, during the presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the administration became entangled in a severe constitutional crisis that inflicted far deeper damage on the entire party structure. Today, with Mr. Yoon imprisoned and the conservative bloc relegated to the opposition, the People Power Party continues to rely on superficial fixes rather than genuine institutional reform.

Rather than reflecting on how these crises dismantled public trust, many conservative figures mistakenly view slight shifts in public opinion or missteps by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, under President Lee Jae-myung, as an opportunity to regain power. This reflects a dangerous political illusion. Passive gains derived from an opponent's stumbles cannot substitute for actual party reform, especially when voters clearly remember recent threats to democratic order.

Applying Toynbee: The Failure of the Dominant Minority

British historian Arnold Toynbee observed that civilizations advance under "creative minorities" who effectively answer societal challenges. However, once established, these leaders frequently transform into a "dominant minority." Trapped by what Toynbee called the "idolization of the ephemeral self," they insist on applying outdated formulas to fundamentally altered realities. South Korea's conservative governments repeatedly fell into this trap, attempting to deploy past authoritarian levers and coercive measures against a politically mature civil society that demands dialogue and accountability.

As power isolated them, conservative leaders grew increasingly hostile to criticism, suppressing opposition and surrounding themselves with flatterers. When political leaders treat power as an absolute right rather than a public mandate, the electorate inevitably revokes its consent. Unless the People Power Party extracts the rotten roots of its past governance style, temporary political cycles will not save it from permanent irrelevance.
