KOSPI Briefly Breaches 5,000 as Nvidia Turmoil and U.S. Curbs Rattle Seoul

November 17, 2026

South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI stock index briefly fell below the 5,000 mark on November 17, 2026, driven less by deteriorating domestic chip fundamentals than by a simultaneous shock from U.S. export controls, Nvidia leadership uncertainty, and hawkish Federal Reserve rate signals.

  • The market drop: Following a 5% plunge in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, the KOSPI hit an intraday low of 4,984 before recovering to around 5,050, while the Korean won weakened to 1,339 per dollar.
  • Foreign outflows, thin domestic cushion: Overseas investors dumped nearly 1 trillion won (about $747 million) in Korean equities by midday after selling over 1 trillion won the previous session, while retail investor cash deposits stood shrunken at 87 trillion won (about $65 billion).
  • Washington’s triple hit: The U.S. Commerce Department moved to block Chinese remote access to advanced AI chips via Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil; Fed Chair Kevin Warsh warned of potential AI downside scenarios as markets priced in a 95.7% chance of a December 25-basis-point rate hike; and Nvidia’s board reportedly ruled out returning Jensen Huang as CEO despite public backing from President Donald Trump.
  • Chipmakers caught in the crossfire: South Korean memory chipmaker SK Hynix rebounded about 1.5% after sliding over 7% a day earlier, while domestic rival Samsung Electronics remained under pressure alongside tech peers in Japan and Taiwan.

Noise versus structural shift

Much of this sell-off reflects liquidity strain and headline shock rather than a sudden collapse in Korean corporate earnings. While a SemiAnalysis report cutting 2028 and 2030 AI accelerator spending forecasts by 28% and 40% amplified fears of an AI bubble, analysts such as Roh Geun-chang at Semicon Research Lab note that major AI developers are still dedicating roughly 60% of their computing resources to next-generation model research, and Anthropic’s revenue growth actually accelerated to 14% in October.

Where smart money is rotating

Even if the AI buildout is not ending, the market’s center of gravity is shifting away from Nvidia’s near-monopoly toward a multipolar race featuring Google, Amazon, AMD, and Broadcom. Third-quarter filings from major U.S. funds underscore that pivot: investor Stanley Druckenmiller exited AMD, ARM, and Lam Research while adding data-storage firms Western Digital and Seagate alongside SpaceX and gold miners.

For Seoul’s semiconductor heavyweights, that transition cuts both ways. Tighter U.S. enforcement against chip smuggling—estimated by market observers to account for 10% to 20% of sector revenue through indirect demand—and Nvidia’s internal turbulence will keep volatility elevated in the near term, yet diversifying AI buyers could eventually reduce reliance on a single Silicon Valley customer if South Korea’s domestic investors regain the appetite to absorb foreign selling.
