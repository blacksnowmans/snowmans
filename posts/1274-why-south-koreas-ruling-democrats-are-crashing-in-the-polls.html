Why South Korea’s Ruling Democrats Are Crashing in the Polls

October 18, 2026

A precipitous drop in public support for South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and President Lee Jae-myung stems from a collapse in morale among core progressive voters following controversial concessions on prosecution reform.

  • The Plunge: A mid-October survey placed President Lee’s disapproval at 64.0% against a 31.7% approval rating, while the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) opened a commanding 51.7% to 28.4% lead in general election voting preference.
  • The Trigger: Grassroots outrage boiled over after the administration tapped Kim Ji-yong—a 24-year veteran prosecutor—to lead the newly created Serious Crime Investigation Agency, an independent body created precisely to curb prosecutorial overreach.
  • The Core Grievance: Progressive voters view the appointment as a betrayal of long-promised structural reforms, reducing a historic effort to separate investigation from indictment into a mere bureaucratic reshuffle.
  • The Electoral Risk: Talk of the DPK plunging below 100 legislative seats has rattled the party, threatening critical swing battlegrounds across the Seoul metropolitan area and the central Chungcheong provinces.

A Compromised Reform Mandate

The sudden alienation of the ruling party’s base is not standard midterm fatigue; it is a breakdown in political efficacy. Progressive voters propelled the DPK to power on promises to rein in the country's formidable prosecution service. Appointing an elite former prosecutor who previously defended the prosecution's direct investigative powers to lead the independent investigative agency strikes many supporters as hypocrisy. Critics and party loyalists argue it resembles a conglomerate creating a new subsidiary simply to distribute executive posts to the old guard.

When voters are repeatedly told to "wait" while foundational campaign pledges are watered down through backroom political compromise, passive acquiescence turns into active disengagement. Presidential aides brushed off early objections as factional purism, but the chilling effect among volunteers and regular party members is already palpable across key districts.

Why Panic Math Misses the Core Problem

Anxiety inside the National Assembly has revived an old rule of thumb that a president’s approval rating multiplied by three predicts the ruling party’s seat tally, suggesting the DPK could stumble to an catastrophic 95 seats. Yet mechanically translating national polling into seat totals misreads South Korea’s first-past-the-post electoral system. In close-fought constituencies, individual candidate appeal and local ground games frequently defy top-line national sentiment.

Moreover, recent polling has repeatedly miscalculated real margins. Following the historic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, conservative respondents frequently withheld their true preferences during direct phone interviews, leading to significant conservative outperformance on election day. The danger facing President Lee is not an arbitrary forecast of 95 seats, but the risk that disillusioned supporters will simply stay home in tight races won by single percentage points. Rebuilding that eroded trust will require executive action on core promises rather than dismissive lectures from the presidential office.
