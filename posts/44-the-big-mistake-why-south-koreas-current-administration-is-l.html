The "Big Mistake": Why South Korea’s Current Administration is Losing Its Grip

September 24, 2026

The South Korean government is facing a growing crisis of confidence, driven by a top-down, inflexible policymaking style that ignores realities on the ground and alienates its own core supporters.

  • Agricultural Uproar: A sweeping, state-led investigation into farmland ownership—pushed by the administration—has left rural residents feeling targeted and suspicious, effectively turning farmers against the government.
  • Policy Inflexibility: The administration’s reliance on "I’ve done this before, so I know how it works" rhetoric, reminiscent of past conservative leaderships, is failing to resonate in contexts beyond urban redevelopment.
  • Communication Failure: The government is struggling to counter negative media narratives by relying on obscure, limited-reach channels, while ignoring more influential, pro-reform platforms favored by its base.
  • Stalled Reforms: Despite promises to reform the prosecution, the administration remains hesitant to take on entrenched "institutional cartels," leading to deep frustration among progressives.

The Perils of "I Know Best" Governance

The administration's recent stumble stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of rural dynamics. While the president may have achieved success with urban redevelopment projects during their time as a provincial governor, that same "bulldozer" approach has backfired in the countryside. By launching a full-scale farmland investigation without consulting those it impacts most, the government has created a PR disaster that it is ill-equipped to manage.

Critically, the government’s public relations strategy is nearly invisible to the general public. Instead of engaging with popular independent media outlets that actually reach and persuade the voting base, officials are relegated to minor government-run channels. This isolation allows conservative media outlets to dominate the narrative, further eroding the president’s approval ratings.

Stalled Reform and the Cost of Hesitation

Perhaps the most damaging issue for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the liberal main opposition, currently leading government policy) is the perceived abandonment of judicial and prosecutorial reform. These were foundational campaign promises, yet the pace of change has been glacial. The base is not looking for caution; they are looking for the dismantling of the existing legal-political cartel that has long wielded disproportionate power in South Korea.

The administration’s tendency to surround itself with or promote voices that are actively critical of its most loyal allies—such as figures who spearheaded past reform movements—is an ironic and self-defeating strategy. If the government continues to prioritize political optics over the actual, difficult work of reform, it risks losing the very support that brought it to power. The time for "defensive" politics is over; the administration must decide whether it wants to govern through consensus with the people or remain shackled by fear of the establishment.
