Korea’s Semiconductor Ambitions Face Real-World Hurdles: Power, Water, and 260,000 Missing Workers

November 15, 2026

South Korea’s plan to build a global semiconductor "Mega Cluster" in Yongin is colliding with a harsh reality: a severe shortage of skilled labor and physical infrastructure that cannot keep pace with multi-billion dollar corporate investments.

  • Samsung Electronics is committing $4.48 billion (approximately 6 trillion won) to advanced High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) packaging facilities in Asan and Onyang to challenge the dominance of Taiwan’s TSMC.
  • President Lee Jae-myung, leader of the ruling Democratic Party, recently visited Samsung’s Onyang campus to signal government support for domestic "back-end" processing capabilities.
  • The government has unveiled a roadmap to develop 10 domestic AI chip types by 2030, targeting autonomous vehicles and robotics to diversify the nation's reliance on memory chips.
  • A massive labor crisis looms, with industry projections estimating a shortfall of 260,000 workers by 2030, even as specialized vocational schools see record-high application rates.

The gap between South Korea’s high-tech roadmaps and its physical reality is widening. While the government and companies like Samsung and SK Hynix—the world’s second-largest memory maker—announce massive "fab" (semiconductor fabrication plant) projects, the supporting infrastructure remains stuck in planning. In Yongin, a satellite city south of Seoul, housing developments are moving forward, but the essential rail and road networks to transport thousands of workers are bogged down in bureaucratic disputes between local and central authorities.

There is also a growing sense of "subsidy envy" regarding international competitors. While TSMC received over $5 billion in government subsidies in the third quarter alone, Korean firms are largely limited to tax credits. Without direct cash injections to offset the massive initial costs of building advanced 2-nanometer production lines, Korean industry leaders argue they are fighting on an uneven global playing field. Investment is a race of speed, and tax breaks often arrive too late to influence the initial break-ground decisions.

The Packaging War and the AI Roadmap

As traditional chip-shrinking technology hits its physical limits, "packaging"—the process of stacking and connecting chips—has become the new battlefield. Samsung’s $4.48 billion investment in HBM packaging is a defensive move to prevent being sidelined in the AI boom. HBM is a high-performance memory essential for AI processors like those made by Nvidia. However, building the factory is only half the battle; the industry must also foster a local ecosystem of "Sobu장" (an acronym for materials, components, and equipment) companies to ensure supply chain resilience.

The government’s plan to develop 10 types of domestic AI chips by 2030 is ambitious but faces a steep climb toward "market adoption." Historically, Korean "fabless" companies (firms that design chips but outsource manufacturing) have struggled because local car and robot manufacturers often prefer established global brands like Qualcomm or Nvidia. To succeed, the Lee Jae-myung administration must move beyond just funding R&D and actively create a "test-bed" through public sector procurement and state-backed pilot programs.

The 260,000-Worker Deficit

The most critical threat to Korea’s semiconductor crown is not technology, but people. Projections show the industry will be short 260,000 workers by 2030. This shortage spans the entire spectrum, from construction crews needed to build the fabs to Ph.D.-level researchers designing Neural Processing Units (NPUs). While specialized high schools like Yongin Semiconductor High are seeing high competition—4.84 applicants for every spot—it is not enough to offset the "brain drain" of elite students toward medical schools or overseas tech firms.

Furthermore, internal stability is at risk. Labor unions, specifically the Chemical and Food Workers’ Union, are demanding direct negotiations with Samsung Electronics regarding sub-contractor conditions. In the ultra-precise world of semiconductor manufacturing, where a single hour of downtime can cost millions of dollars, maintaining stable labor relations is as essential as a steady power supply. If the government and industry cannot solve the human equation—through better pay, better infrastructure, and educational reform—the world’s largest chip cluster may find itself with state-of-the-art machines but no one to run them.
