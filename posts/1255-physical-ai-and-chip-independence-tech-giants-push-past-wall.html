Physical AI and Chip Independence: Tech Giants Push Past Wall Street’s Bubble Fears

October 18, 2026

Despite mounting Wall Street skepticism and tech-stock sell-offs over capital expenditure fatigue, global technology giants and industrial manufacturers are aggressively expanding physical AI integration and self-reliant computing infrastructure.

  • Hardware recruits software brains: Hyundai Motor Group, South Korea's automotive giant, recruited Amazon's former Alexa AI chief to head its robotics subsidiary Boston Dynamics, underscoring the industry pivot from conversational models to mechanical robotics.
  • Infrastructure bypasses Nvidia: Tech companies are racing to control their computational foundations—Anthropic inked a lease for its first dedicated data center in Canada, while Microsoft deployed its custom Maia AI accelerator chips to reduce dependence on expensive Nvidia hardware.
  • Industrial labs hit the ground: In South Korea, vice-ministerial officials from the Ministry of SMEs and Startups and the Ministry of Science and ICT visited the KAIST (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology) Physical AI Demonstration Lab to accelerate deploying automated intelligence into small manufacturing lines.
  • Copyright reckoning hits Korea: Anthropic’s ongoing copyright litigation confirmed that 21 South Korean books—including works by Nobel laureate Han Kang and acclaimed novelist Hwang Sok-yong—were scraped without consent, forcing model builders toward content watermarking.

From Text Prompts to Factory Floors

The industry's center of gravity is decisively shifting away from virtual text generation toward physical machinery and autonomous agents. By pairing veteran Silicon Valley software leadership with Boston Dynamics' mechanical systems, Hyundai Motor Group is signaling that the next commercial frontier lies in robotics that patrol facilities and tighten factory bolts. In South Korea, where population aging is depleting shop-floor expertise, the state-supported push to deploy physical AI at KAIST is less about tech hype than an urgent economic survival strategy to automate traditional assembly lines.

The Capital Crunch and Regulatory Moats

Behind the hardware rollout lies an intense capital battle. Anthropic’s outreach to private equity giant Blackstone for chip financing—offering double-digit returns—reveals that equity fundraising alone can no longer foot the spiraling electric bills and data-center costs of frontier models. Cloud operators like Microsoft are answering this squeeze by deploying in-house silicon like Maia for inference tasks, aiming to crush operating overhead as Wall Street scrutinizes returns on heavy capital spending.

At the same time, leading American model builders are aggressively lobbying for statutory safety guardrails. While framed as public risk management, stringent regulatory burdens effectively erect barriers that block undercapitalized startups from competing. For South Korea, which suffered institutional setbacks during Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office when foundational science and R&D budgets were abruptly slashed, closing the computing gap against Silicon Valley's private-equity-backed infrastructure will require hyper-specialization in edge devices and domestic mobile agents.
