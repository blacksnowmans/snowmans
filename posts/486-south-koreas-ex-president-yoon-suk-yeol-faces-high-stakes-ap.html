South Korea's Ex-President Yoon Suk Yeol Faces High-Stakes Appeal Over 2022 Election Lies

September 09, 2026

  • Former South Korean President Yoon Suk Yeol's appeal trial for violating the Public Official Election Act has begun, threatening him with a potential upholding of his lower court conviction.
  • If the guilty verdict and invalidation of his election are finalized by the Supreme Court, the conservative People Power Party could be forced to return about $29 million (39.7 billion won) in state election subsidies.
  • The Seoul High Court has rejected delay tactics and signaled a swift ruling, aiming for a verdict by late September.

South Korea’s political landscape is bracing for a seismic legal outcome as former President Yoon Suk Yeol fights to overturn a unprecedented lower court sentence. A lower court previously handed down a 1.5-year prison term suspended for three years—a sentence that effectively voids his past election win. At the heart of the case are false statements Yoon made during the 2022 presidential campaign regarding his involvement in introducing a lawyer to a corruption-scandalized tax official, as well as denials concerning past associations.

The financial fallout for the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, could be catastrophic. Under South Korean law, a definitive election-invalidating ruling requires a party to return its state-provided campaign refunds. For a major political party, coughing up nearly $30 million is a crippling blow that risks pushing the organization toward financial insolvency.

Fast-Tracked Trial Shatters Delay Tactics

Yoon’s legal team has attempted to stall proceedings by drawing parallels to other high-profile political trials—such as those involving opposition figures—arguing that his case should likewise be paused. Legal analysts view this as a classic political diversion tactic designed to run out the clock.

However, the Seoul High Court’s Criminal Division 2 has shown zero appetite for drawn-out arguments. Dismissing defense claims that Yoon's false statements were merely off-the-cuff, 2-to-3-second remarks, the court has scheduled aggressive weekly hearings and made clear its intent to hand down a ruling by late September. With objective evidence, including past recorded phone calls and contemporaneous text messages, heavily contradicting Yoon's denials, the judiciary appears determined to swiftly close the chapter on what it views as a calculated attempt to mislead voters.
