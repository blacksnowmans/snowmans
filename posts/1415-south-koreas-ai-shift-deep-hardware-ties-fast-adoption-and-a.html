South Korea’s AI Shift: Deep Hardware Ties, Fast Adoption, and a 99.98% Bottleneck

October 23, 2026

Global AI giants are cementing manufacturing and developer alliances across South Korea, but runaway corporate adoption is colliding with an acute, near-total dependence on foreign silicon.

  • Hardware billions: Nvidia and Tesla are committing a combined 3.7 trillion won (about $2.7 billion) toward Samsung Electro-Mechanics, the electronic component affiliate of Samsung Group, to expand AI substrate packaging with a dedicated line in the southern port city of Busan.
  • Deploying "Physical AI": Heavy manufacturers like HD Hyundai Robotics and refiner S-Oil are moving models out of chat windows and onto factory floors, using autonomous machines for shipyard welding and AI monitoring at petrochemical facilities.
  • Fast-moving user base: South Korea now ranks third globally in usage of the Dot platform and in the top five for ChatGPT Pro, prompting OpenAI to actively court local engineers and IT service provider LG CNS to deploy enterprise AI to 30,000 employees.
  • The single-vendor hazard: National Assembly auditing revealed that South Korean public institutions rely on Nvidia for 99.98% of their AI computing chips, effectively locking out domestic semiconductor startups from state-backed verification contracts.

From Prompt Boxes to Factory Floors

South Korea’s role in the global artificial intelligence boom is rapidly pivoting from high-volume software consumption to critical physical integration. While OpenAI is actively targeting Korean developers to leverage one of its fastest-testing enterprise markets, the real commercial weight is shifting to manufacturing. Component makers like LG Innotek are co-developing optical sensing modules with Nvidia for robotic vision, while substrate manufacturers are capturing multi-billion-dollar backorders to resolve packaging bottlenecks for next-generation data centers.

At the same time, the country's industrial backbone is accelerating the deployment of "physical AI." Rather than chasing generic chatbot novelty, heavy industries—facing demographic decline and grueling labor environments—are testing autonomous systems in shipyards, petrochemical complexes, and municipal logistics hubs. AI is being treated less as an intellectual curiosity and more as an operational necessity to manage plant safety, optimize power loads, and offset skilled labor shortages.

The Structural Catch Behind the Boom

This rapid integration carries clear sovereign risks. The revelation by lawmaker Kim Jong-min that public sector agencies depend on Nvidia for 99.98% of their AI accelerator purchases demonstrates how vulnerable the country’s core infrastructure is to external pricing and supply constraints. Without state-backed pilot programs to test and deploy homegrown neural processing units (NPUs), domestic chip architects risk being sidelined before they can establish commercial reference points.

South Korea finds itself balancing on a knife-edge: it is an indispensable tier-one hardware foundry and testing ground for Silicon Valley, yet its domestic institutions and enterprises remain thoroughly beholden to foreign platform architectures. Bridging that gap will require less blind enthusiasm for imported models and far more state urgency in clearing outdated network regulations while securing local compute sovereignty.
