Shake-up in Seoul: Understanding the Presidential Reshuffle

September 23, 2026

The sudden resignation of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik has triggered intense speculation in South Korean politics, signaling a broader strategic realignment within President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

  • Leadership Changes: Kang’s departure is being interpreted as a move to clear the decks for a potential mayoral run, fueling rumors of a wider cabinet reshuffle.
  • Strategic Maneuvering: High-profile figures like Representative Han Jun-ho have gone silent on social media—a classic tell-tale sign in Korean politics that major appointments are imminent.
  • Redefining Governance: The administration is consolidating power through a "four-person" strategy team tasked with balancing policy execution, political networking, and internal "Red Team" oversight.

The "Four-Person" Power Structure

President Lee’s second term is anchored by a four-way division of labor designed to stress-test his policy agenda. Kim Min-seok acts as the Chief Strategy Officer, while the departing Kang Hoon-sik served as the Chief Operating Officer. Jeong Sung-ho manages human networks and candidate selection for future elections, while former party leader Song Young-gil serves as the "Red Team"—the designated internal critic.

This structure is highly calculated. By assigning Song to challenge the administration's own policies, such as pushing back against overly restrictive financial regulations in real estate, the President creates an internal safety valve. It is a pragmatic, albeit risky, approach to governance that aims to prevent the "echo chamber" effect common in long-term administrations.

Reform and the Path Forward

The reshuffle extends to the Ministry of Justice, where the search for a new minister has highlighted a rift between traditional party reformers and the "New Lee" loyalists. With names like Representative Park Ju-min surfacing, the administration is clearly debating how to exert democratic control over the prosecution, a historically powerful and controversial agency in Korea.

President Lee’s recent focus on personally refining his public remarks—even delaying a press conference by 30 minutes to polish his message—suggests a leader who views communication as a core responsibility rather than a formality. Whether this focus on detail translates into effective systemic reform remains the central question. As the administration faces inevitable resistance from entrenched interests, its ability to maintain this "four-person" balance will determine whether these changes lead to genuine progress or simply further bureaucratic gridlock.
