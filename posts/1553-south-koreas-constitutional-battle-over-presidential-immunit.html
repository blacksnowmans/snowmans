South Korea’s Constitutional Battle Over Presidential Immunity and Judicial Reform

October 27, 2026

A fierce constitutional clash has erupted in South Korea over Article 84 of the Constitution, as the judiciary hints at resuming criminal trials against sitting President Lee Jae-myung while the ruling party hesitates to push full judicial accountability.

  • What happened: Judicial officials signaled that criminal trials initiated before President Lee took office could theoretically resume, exploiting literal interpretations of constitutional immunity rules.
  • What it changes: Instead of filing an immediate impeachment motion against Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea opted for a parliamentary investigation, drawing criticism for tactical hesitation.
  • Who is affected: The executive branch faces potential governance paralysis, while a public already deeply skeptical of the legal system sees judicial authority wielded as a political leverage tool.

The Constitutional Intent of Presidential Immunity

Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution explicitly protects a sitting president from criminal prosecution during their term, barring cases of treason or foreign aggression. While conservative legal figures claim the word "prosecution" applies only to filing new charges rather than continuing existing trial proceedings, the constitutional rationale is clear: shielding the chief executive from judicial distraction to ensure stable state governance. Forcing a sitting president to regularly attend trial court sessions severely paralyzes executive duties.

International precedents in major presidential systems support this protective shield. France constitutionalized strict immunity barring sitting presidents from any court summons, civil suits, or criminal proceedings, while established constitutional practice in the United States similarly shields incumbent presidents. Treating the presidency as subject to trial court discretion undermines democratic elections and shifts ultimate political authority to an unelected judiciary.

Legislative Inaction and Public Distrust in the Judiciary

Despite holding a commanding majority in the National Assembly, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's main center-left ruling party, has drawn sharp backlash from supporters for its passive stance. By choosing a drawn-out parliamentary probe over decisive impeachment proceedings against Chief Justice Cho, DPK leadership risks losing critical timing while allowing entrenched legal factions to maneuver politically.

This political standoff comes amidst unprecedented public cynicism toward the court system. According to 2024 OECD data, public trust in South Korea’s judicial system stands at just 33%, well below the OECD average of 54% and far behind nations like Norway (77%) or Denmark (75%). Rebuilding democratic balance requires concrete legislative action: amending the Criminal Procedure Act to explicitly halt trial proceedings for incumbent presidents, dismantling the bureaucratic National Court Administration, and ending corrupt revolving-door privileges for former judges.
