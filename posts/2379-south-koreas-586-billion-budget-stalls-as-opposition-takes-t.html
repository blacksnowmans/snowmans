South Korea’s $586 Billion Budget Stalls as Opposition Takes to the Streets

November 16, 2026

South Korea’s national budget process has ground to a halt as the main conservative opposition party abandons the National Assembly in favor of nationwide street rallies against the administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

  • The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition party, staged large-scale protests in Busan and Gangwon Province to condemn a "Special Counsel Bill on Fabricated Prosecutions" and pressure the government.
  • Negotiations for the 821 trillion won (approx. $586 billion) 2025 budget are frozen, with the PPP vowing to block all proceedings unless the government accepts its terms regarding the 162 trillion won ($116 billion) "Future Response Fund."
  • Opposition leaders are demanding the immediate resumption of President Lee Jae-myung’s election law trials, which they claim have been stalled for over two years, arguing that "even a president is not above the law."
  • Political rhetoric has turned increasingly vitriolic, with police cracking down on extreme "hate banners" that feature synthesized funeral images of the president, while the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) accuses the opposition of mimicking far-right extremist tactics.

The Death of Legislative Compromise

By moving the primary political battlefield from the floors of the National Assembly to the streets, the PPP is signaling a total breakdown in the culture of negotiation. While these rallies are effective at consolidating the conservative base and signaling strength, they paralyze the state's ability to address pressing economic issues. The 821 trillion won budget is not just a collection of numbers; it represents the "bloodline" of the country, funding welfare, local government, and future industries. By linking these essential funds to a battle over judicial outcomes and special counsel bills, the opposition has effectively taken the national economy hostage.

The core of the dispute lies in the PPP's refusal to cooperate on the "Future Response Fund." This massive investment pool is a cornerstone of the Lee Jae-myung administration’s long-term industrial policy, but the opposition views it as a political slush fund or a mismanagement of tax revenue. However, the opposition's own internal stability is in question. Factions within the PPP are currently embroiled in leadership disputes, with some members threatening legal injunctions against their own party hierarchy. This internal chaos suggests that the street protests may be an attempt to deflect attention from a party that is struggling to maintain a unified front.

A Global Convergence of Radical Politics

The current domestic tension mirrors a broader, worrying rise in far-right populism seen globally. From the Reform UK party in Britain to the Alternative for Germany (AfD) and Spain’s Vox, populist movements are increasingly using social anxiety and aggressive rhetoric to bypass traditional democratic norms. In Korea, this manifests as a dangerous convergence between mainstream opposition politics and radical YouTube conspiracy culture. When established parties adopt the unrefined language of street activists—labeling political rivals as enemies to be "imprisoned" or "removed" rather than partners to be debated—the democratic "exit ramp" of compromise is effectively sealed off.

This radicalization extends into the social sphere, where far-right groups like the Parents' Association for Children's Education Rights (PACER) are now targeting the Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education. By demanding the resignation of Superintendent Jung Keun-sik and attacking human rights guidebooks intended to prevent discrimination in schools, these groups are dragging the education system into a partisan ideological war. When political discourse is reduced to "funeral banners" and conspiracy theories about academic degrees, the victims are not just the politicians being attacked, but the health of the civil society that must live with the resulting polarization.
