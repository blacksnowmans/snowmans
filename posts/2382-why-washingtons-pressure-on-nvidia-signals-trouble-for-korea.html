Why Washington’s Pressure on Nvidia Signals Trouble for Korean Chipmakers

November 16, 2026

South Korean tech commentators are reading recent personal and espionage controversies surrounding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang not as Silicon Valley gossip, but as a hardball move by U.S. China hawks to choke off AI chip backdoors—putting South Korea’s semiconductor supply chain directly in the crossfire.

  • The alleged squeeze on Nvidia: A widely circulated Korean analysis argues that U.S. security hawks—frustrated by the limits of steep tariffs, failed Asia pivots, and Pentagon wargames known as the Overmatch Brief showing U.S. vulnerabilities in a Taiwan conflict—are targeting Huang to force Nvidia’s open-source, China-facing strategy into line.
  • Closing Southeast Asian loopholes: Washington’s intensifying scrutiny targets indirect export routes through Singapore and Malaysia, where third-country entities have allegedly helped channel high-end AI chips into China, raising fears of U.S. secondary sanctions across the region.
  • A fractured tech order: As Washington rallies allies behind a U.S.-led semiconductor control bloc dubbed "Pax Silica," China and emerging economies are building a rival World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO), accelerating a "World minus one" ecosystem that bypasses U.S. rules.
  • Korean memory giants caught in the middle: South Korea has already leaned deeply into Pax Silica, leaving domestic suppliers of high-bandwidth memory (HBM)—critical components inside Nvidia’s AI systems—exposed to severe revenue shocks if Nvidia’s indirect Asian sales channels are shut down.

Seoul’s Narrowing Path

The uncomfortable takeaway for South Korea is that Washington’s tech blockade is becoming less predictable just as Southeast Asian nations play both sides with remarkable agility. While Singapore joins Pax Silica and quietly weighs WAICO, and Indonesia does the reverse, Seoul has locked itself tightly into the U.S. compliance perimeter without securing the same room to maneuver.

There is also a deeper risk in mistaking great-power summitry around forums like the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) meeting for a return to stable global order. As the Korean commentary notes, today’s major powers show little appetite for supplying shared stability—a dynamic known as the Kindleberger Trap, where no dominant state wants to pay the cost of maintaining open rules. In that vacuum, Washington relies on blunt unilateral leverage over individual firms and allies alike.

For Seoul and its flagship memory makers, blind adherence to Cold War-style bloc politics could turn a global technology lead into a captive subcontracting role for Washington. South Korea cannot afford to alienate the U.S. security umbrella, yet it also needs the diplomatic nerve—and irreplaceable chip technology—to keep commercial options alive in a bifurcating Asian market.
