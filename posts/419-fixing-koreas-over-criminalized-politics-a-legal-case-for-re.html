Fixing Korea's Over-Criminalized Politics: A Legal Case for Reform

September 08, 2026

  • What happened: Prominent legal scholar and politician Cho Kuk has pushed back against the ruling Democratic Party's push to drop charges (공소취소) against President Lee Jae-myung, explaining that doing so for a case already on appeal is legally impossible and unconstitutional.
  • The root issue: South Korea's Public Official Election Act contains a vague "acts and behavior" clause added in 2000, which critics argue allows prosecutors to weaponize ordinary political speech and memory gaps into criminal offenses.
  • The proposed fix: Rather than chasing legally dead-end withdrawals, lawmakers should pass a pending amendment to strike the vague clause, which would trigger legal dismissals (면소 판결) for affected politicians across party lines—including both President Lee and former President Yoon Suk Yeol.

South Korea’s political class remains trapped in a loop of judicial warfare, with lawmakers and supporters fixated on dropping ongoing trials rather than governing. As the legal reality sets in, a sharp debate has emerged over how to untangle the country's severe over-criminalization of routine political speech.

The core delusion among some ruling-party hardliners is that prosecutors or special laws can simply "cancel" indictments (공소취소) for cases currently sitting in appellate courts. Legal experts point out that under South Korea's Criminal Procedure Act, pulling an indictment is strictly a first-instance (1심) privilege. Any attempt to pass a special law overriding this for appeals cases would immediately trigger constitutional challenges, backfiring on the judiciary and handing political opponents an easy legal victory.

The Flaw in the Election Act and the Two-Track Solution

The true origin of South Korea's endless legal-political spiral lies in the Public Official Election Act itself. A 2000 legal revision added the vague concept of "acts" (행위) to laws regarding false statements. This single addition transformed ordinary political disagreements, conversational nuances, and lapses in memory into fertile ground for aggressive prosecutorial investigations. By weaponizing this clause, prosecutors have gained sweeping power to drag politicians from both sides of the aisle into lengthy court battles.

To break this cycle, scholars like Cho Kuk argue for a two-track approach. First, the National Assembly must stop chasing impossible indictment withdrawals and instead pass an already-pending amendment to strip the toxic "acts" clause from the election law. Doing so would remove the legal basis for these politically charged prosecutions, forcing courts to issue proper dismissals (면소 판결) for figures like Yoon Suk Yeol and Lee Jae-myung alike. Second, actual evidence fabrication by state prosecutors must be thoroughly investigated through independent channels, providing legitimate grounds for dropping specific compromised cases.
