South Korea's 140 km/h Speed Limit Debate

September 30, 2026

South Korea is actively debating raising its maximum highway speed limit to 140 km/h (about 87 mph), sparking a broader conversation about whether the country's world-class road infrastructure is being held back by outdated 1970s regulations.

    The Proposal: Pushed by new high-speed highway projects—such as the proposed 2.6 trillion won (about $1.9 billion) Gwangju-Yeongam expressway—policymakers are eyeing higher speed caps to match modern automotive capabilities. The Legal Bottleneck: South Korea's Road Traffic Act hard-caps highway speeds at 120 km/h, even on roads specifically engineered for 140 km/h, creating a mismatch between modern infrastructure and rigid bureaucracy. The Safety Dilemma: While modern Korean vehicles easily cruise past 180 km/h, data shows that tunnel accidents carry significantly higher fatality rates, meaning any speed increase must be paired with smarter variable limits and safety upgrades. The Impact: Upgrading speed limits could eliminate "phantom traffic" caused by the current 100 km/h universal cap, giving drivers the flexibility to match surrounding traffic safely instead of playing a constant game of hide-and-seek with speed cameras.

Infrastructure Meets Outdated Bureaucracy

South Korea boasts some of the world's best road paving, drainage, and smart monitoring systems, yet its speed limits remain anchored in the past. When the Gyeongbu Expressway opened in 1978, a 100 km/h limit made sense given the limitations of vehicles at the time. Today, automakers like Hyundai and Kia produce high-performance cars capable of handling much higher speeds with ease. Critics argue that keeping a blanket 100 km/h or 120 km/h cap turns everyday drivers into technical lawbreakers and breeds a culture of evading speed traps rather than practicing mindful driving.

This mismatch also fuels "phantom traffic"—unexplained slowdowns caused by a ripple effect of braking. Because the current legal caps force all lanes to crawl at uniform, low speeds, passing lanes lose their functional purpose, and frustration builds among drivers. Transitioning to flexible, data-driven variable speed limits—similar to systems used on European autobahns—would allow authorities to raise caps to 150 km/h or lower them during rain and congestion, transforming speed limits from blunt instruments of punishment into tools for smooth traffic flow.

Balancing Efficiency with Critical Safety Data

Proponents of higher limits emphasize that raising the cap is not about reckless driving, but about giving motorists the freedom to choose a safe speed that matches traffic conditions and their vehicle's capabilities. However, policymakers cannot ignore hard data. According to data highlighted by Democratic Party lawmaker Hwang Hee, tunnel accidents carry a fatality rate 2.4 times higher than general road accidents, proving that higher speeds leave zero room for error where visibility is restricted.

Ultimately, a shift to 140 km/h requires more than just changing a road sign; it demands a complete overhaul of driver education, lane discipline, and hazard management. If South Korea wants its road policies to match its global standing in technology and pop culture, it must trust its drivers and infrastructure enough to build a smarter, more flexible highway system.
