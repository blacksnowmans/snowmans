South Korea’s Chip Giants Stumble as AI Profit Fears and Sticky Inflation Collide

October 15, 2026

A brutal combination of resurgent U.S. inflation indicators and abrupt decelerations among top-tier artificial intelligence startups has triggered double-digit pullbacks in South Korea’s semiconductor flagships, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

  • Macro headwinds reignite: A hotter-than-expected 0.33% month-on-month rise in the U.S. September Consumer Price Index—dominated by a 0.18 percentage point contribution from sticky housing costs—has re-empowered bond vigilantes, driving benchmark yields higher and raising borrowing costs for capital-intensive chipmakers.
  • The AI growth scare: High-flying AI developer Anthropic saw its quarter-on-quarter revenue growth collapse from 140% in the second quarter to 40% in the third quarter ($16.8 billion), fueling fears that the infrastructure spending boom is outpacing real-world monetization.
  • Intensifying model rivalry: OpenAI’s September 3 launch of its "Astra" model reversed Anthropic's enterprise momentum, touching off price reductions of over 20% and compressing margins across the model-building layer.
  • Upstream domino effect: Rising default risks among "neocloud" GPU-leasing operators threaten Nvidia orders, sparking investor fears of an eventual demand cliff for SK Hynix's mission-critical high-bandwidth memory (HBM).
  • A persistent governance discount: SK Hynix has lagged Samsung in investor sentiment following retail backlash over plans to separately list its U.S. NAND subsidiary, Solidigm—a classic Korean corporate carve-out that eroded domestic shareholder trust.

From Infrastructure Euphoria to Margin Realities

The sudden chill in Seoul’s tech sector reflects an uncomfortable shift in global market sentiment: investors are moving from celebrating AI technological milestones to scrutinizing unit economics. Anthropic’s sharp revenue growth deceleration—now tracking toward an annualized $67 billion against market consensus of $75 billion—underscores the toll taken by intense competition. Corporate spending data shows OpenAI’s Astra quickly took market share from Anthropic’s Claude models, triggering cutthroat price competition just as model developers face soaring compute and electricity bills. When foundational model developers struggle to generate sustainable profits, the downstream logic for endless capital expenditures on memory chips begins to fray.

Compounding this valuation squeeze is the macro backdrop. Rising energy costs and persistent shelter inflation in the United States have curtailed hopes for rapid global monetary easing, frustrating the Lee Jae-myung administration's domestic policy agenda of lowering interest rates to support small businesses. Higher bond yields curb risk appetites and directly deflate the multiples applied to high-beta semiconductor equities. Despite stellar recent performance—such as SK Hynix generating an operating profit of 7.44 trillion won (about $5.5 billion) on revenue of 17.6 trillion won—backward-looking balance sheets provide little shelter when forward demand from AI infrastructure clients comes into question.

Corporate Governance and the October 28 Crucible

Beyond global macro crosscurrents, SK Hynix faces an internal headwind of broken shareholder trust. While the company led the early HBM boom, the August announcement regarding a standalone initial public offering (IPO) for Solidigm—the Intel NAND business it acquired in 2021—alienated retail investors. The maneuver revived bitter memories of the controversial parent-subsidiary split listings that plagued South Korean capital markets during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure. The current administration of President Lee Jae-myung has pledged to stamp out such practices by reforming commercial law to enforce board fiduciary duties to all shareholders, but retail skepticism around SK Hynix has already left a mark.

Market observers argue that panicking during this synchronized downturn is premature. The crucial test arrives with SK Hynix’s quarterly conference call scheduled for October 28. Investors must scrutinize management’s forward guidance on next-generation HBM3E supply allocations and customer commitments. Until hard delivery contracts indicate whether the AI hardware cycle is merely maturing or truly stalling, corporate transparency and verifiable demand trends remain the only reliable anchors for navigating this correction.
