South Korea's "ADHD Phobia": Why Parents Are Overmedicating Normal Kids

October 10, 2026

  • The Trend: South Korean parents are increasingly misinterpreting normal childhood fidgeting and academic stress as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), fueled by online information and intense educational pressure.
  • The Danger: Healthy children are being given methylphenidate—a central nervous system stimulant—to boost test scores, risking severe side effects, hallucinations, and drug dependence.
  • The Expert Take: Dr. Kim Bong-nyeon, a pediatric psychiatrist at Seoul National University Hospital, warns that normal developmental energy is being criminalized as a brain disorder.
  • The Fix: Experts urge parents to ditch parenting manuals, ignore the anxiety-driven hype, and trust their own intimate knowledge of their children.

The Dark Side of Academic Pressure: Fake ADHD and Stimulant Abuse

Modern South Korean parenting is plagued by a pervasive fear that children are falling behind or hiding developmental disorders. According to Dr. Kim Bong-nyeon of Seoul National University Hospital, the hyper-connected digital environment has locked parents into an "ADHD phobia," where normal childhood behaviors are viewed through a medical lens.

Post-pandemic, children forced into sedentary online learning environments naturally struggle to focus on abstract subjects for long stretches. Yet, parents frequently panic, slapping a premature ADHD label on active, high-energy kids. Worse, in hyper-competitive school districts, students without ADHD are illegally taking prescription stimulants like methylphenidate to cram for exams. Medical experts warn that giving these controlled substances to non-ADHD brains disrupts dopamine reward circuits, carrying addiction risks comparable to narcotics.

Beyond the Pills: Attachment, Sleep, and Parental Intuition

True ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition that manifests early, usually around age seven, and persists through schooling. In contrast, sudden concentration drops in older elementary students typically stem from academic stress, anxiety, or depression—problems that medication only worsens.

Dr. Kim emphasizes that early childhood—specifically ages zero to three—relies on physical touch and absolute attachment to build healthy brain structures, not smartphone screens used as babysitters. As children grow into their school years, they need empathetic listening and consistent boundaries rather than rushed diagnoses and chemical fixes. Ultimately, parents need to reclaim their confidence: no external parenting guru or celebrity doctor knows a child better than the parent raising them.
