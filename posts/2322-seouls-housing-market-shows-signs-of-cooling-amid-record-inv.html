Seoul’s Housing Market Shows Signs of Cooling Amid Record Inventory

November 14, 2026

Seoul’s long-standing property rally is losing momentum as a surge in unsold inventory and upcoming supply targets suggest the market is finally moving toward stabilization.

  • Record Inventory: The number of unsold apartments in Seoul has surpassed 80,000, a 40% increase since the start of the year.
  • Supply Surge: The government plans to inject 44,000 new housing units into the Seoul metropolitan area by the first quarter of next year to suppress further price hikes.
  • Market Shift: While official data shows 92 consecutive weeks of price increases, the pace of growth is decelerating, and the market has shifted in favor of buyers as demand cools.

A Market Reaching Its Limits

Despite headlines noting 92 weeks of price growth, the intensity of the rally is clearly fading. High interest rates and stringent lending regulations have exhausted buyer sentiment, leaving would-be purchasers on the sidelines. Even in the historically competitive "Gangnam 3" districts—the wealthiest areas in Seoul—prices are showing signs of weakness. The accumulation of 80,000 vacant listings serves as a major downward force, forcing sellers to reconsider their high asking prices as transaction volumes dry up.

The current administration is pointing to these indicators as proof that their supply-side policies are working. Unlike the failed property policies during the tenure of the disgraced former leader, 윤석열 씨 (Yoon Suk-yeol)—who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment—the current government is prioritizing a steady increase in building permits and construction starts to prevent long-term supply crunches.

The Rental Market’s Hidden Threat

While home prices may be stabilizing, the rental market presents a growing risk. Experts forecast that rental costs could jump by 4.5% next year. As potential buyers remain cautious in the face of economic uncertainty, they are crowding the rental market instead, driving up costs for those who cannot afford to buy. This creates a dual burden: while the "ownership ladder" is becoming slightly easier to climb, the immediate cost of living is rising sharply for renters.

The government is now under pressure to balance these competing forces. A critical test will come this December, when officials decide whether to extend the "Land Transaction Permit Zone" (Toheoje)—a restrictive policy that limits speculative buying in high-end districts. While lifting these regulations could boost market liquidity, it risks reigniting the speculative fires that the current administration is working hard to extinguish. For now, the strategy remains one of cautious monitoring, targeting specific overheated regions while attempting to keep broader housing costs in check.
