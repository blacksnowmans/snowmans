Why Progressive Heavyweight Rhyu Si-min Is Being Called Back to Frontline South Korean Politics

October 16, 2026

Calls are mounting across South Korea's progressive camp for prominent political commentator and former cabinet minister Rhyu Si-min to make a formal return to active politics by joining the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK).

  • What happened: Declining support among voters in their 30s and bitter internal disputes under DPK leader Kim Min-seok have sparked calls for veteran commentator Rhyu Si-min to re-enter the political arena.
  • What it changes: A formal return by Rhyu to the ruling party could force a sweeping factional shakeup within the DPK, reinvigorating its ideological platform and replacing stagnant party insiders.
  • Who is affected: DPK party leadership, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, and the allied minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party.

A Leadership Crisis Inside the Ruling Party

The ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing a sharp political reckoning despite holding power under President Lee Jae-myung. Recent polling highlights a troubling reversal in support among voters in their 30s—traditionally a vital demographic for the party. Under current party leader Kim Min-seok, internal strife has escalated sharply over controversial personnel choices, drawing public pushback from prominent lawmakers including Chu Mi-ae and Kim Yong-min. This factional infighting threatens to sap legislative momentum from President Lee’s broader policy agenda.

In response to this drift, grassroots supporters are demanding that Rhyu Si-min—a former health minister, author, and one of the country's most recognizable progressive intellectuals—step out from behind the commentator's desk and re-enter frontline politics. While Rhyu has long insisted on remaining a media commentator, party loyalists argue that mere commentary is no longer sufficient to stabilize a ruling party that appears to be losing its strategic direction.

Why Mainstream Alignment Matters

A central debate among progressives is where Rhyu should deploy his political weight. While some suggest he align with the minor, reform-minded Rebuilding Korea Party, analysts argue that Rhyu must directly enter the mainstream DPK. Joining a minor third-party platform risks isolating his influence among niche voters. By contrast, rejoining the DPK would force a direct confrontation with entrenched party insiders. Even the public act of seeking re-entry would compel the current DPK leadership to address its authority deficit.

Ultimately, South Korea's ruling party is suffering from a shortage of heavyweights capable of keeping internal factions accountable and uniting the progressive base. Without authoritative figures inside the main party to reset its platform, influence naturally falls to career opportunists. Rhyu's potential return represents more than a personal comeback; it is a critical test of whether the DPK can overhaul its internal hierarchy and rebuild public trust before broader voter frustration takes hold.
