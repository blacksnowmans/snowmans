Inside the Push to Merge Korea’s Ruling Liberals and Double Party Membership to 3 Million

October 31, 2026

Grassroots strategists within South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) are pushing for an aggressive January merger deadline with the aligned Rebuilding Korea Party, framing rapid consolidation and a doubling of dues-paying party members as essential to protecting President Lee Jae-myung’s policy mandate.

  • The merger deadline: Party reformers are calling for a full merger between the DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party by January at the latest to ensure both factions can compete fairly in upcoming candidate primaries.
  • The electoral math: In battleground districts routinely decided by razor-thin margins of 1 to 2 percent—or as few as 500 to 1,000 votes—a split progressive ticket risks handing local and parliamentary seats directly to the conservative opposition.
  • The 3-million member goal: The party aims to expand its regular dues-paying base from 1.5 million to 3 million to buffer leadership against factional maneuvering and create a durable legislative foundation for the Lee Jae-myung administration.
  • The cultural shift: Progressive activists are demanding an end to uncritical fandom, urging members to act as vigilant "homeowners" who check administrative missteps rather than rubber-stamping government decisions.

Primary Rules Make January the Point of No Return

The urgency behind merging with the Rebuilding Korea Party—founded by former justice minister Cho Kuk—is driven strictly by administrative deadlines. Democratic Party regulations divide primary voting weight equally: 50 percent from public polling and 50 percent from registered party members who have paid monthly dues for at least six months. Because candidate selections and competitive primaries typically begin in February and March, any merger delayed past January effectively disenfranchises incoming Rebuilding Korea Party members, preventing them from building qualifying voting blocs and turning the unification into an unfair takeover.

For the ruling coalition, failure to coordinate candidate selection invites electoral disaster. Both parties draw from virtually identical voter pools. Splitting the liberal base in hyper-competitive districts has historically handed victories to the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition. DPK strategists argue that reconciling these two camps—which separated only three years ago—is the only realistic way to maintain unified legislative power behind the current government.

Replacing Blind Loyalty with 'Critical Support'

Following the constitutional impeachment and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is now serving a prison sentence—the Democratic Party regained power under President Lee Jae-myung on the back of widespread civic protests. However, party thinkers caution that replacing a discredited conservative leadership does not grant the new government a blank check. Blind adulation, they argue, deprives an administration of internal corrective mechanisms and invites policy incoherence.

Rather than quitting the party in protest during internal disputes, members are being instructed to step up financial participation. Doubling the party's dues-paying core to 3 million is presented as an insurance policy: it dilutes the leverage of internal power brokers while demanding that rank-and-file progressives provide honest, critical support to keep the Lee Jae-myung administration disciplined, responsive, and electable.
