South Korea's Supreme Court Throws Han Duck-soo's Insurrection Case Into Doubt

November 20, 2026

South Korea's Supreme Court has overturned a core part of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo's insurrection conviction and ordered a retrial on the remaining charges, a ruling analysts on the Korean current-affairs program Hong Sa-hoon Show say could drastically reduce his prison term.

  • What the court ruled: By a 9-3 majority led by Justice Kwon Young-joon, the bench ruled with an intent to acquit Han for convening a cabinet meeting before Yoon Suk-yeol's December 3 martial law declaration, while returning charges of pressuring ministers to sign documents and discussing media power cuts to the Seoul High Court, an appellate court.
  • Why the justices split: The majority applied a strict legal threshold for insurrection intent and concluded Han lacked a criminal-law duty to stop the declaration, whereas dissenting Justices Oh Kyung-mi, Shin Sook-hee, and Kim Sung-soo argued that separating Han's intent before and after the declaration defied common sense.
  • What changes for Han: Previously sentenced to 23 years in district court and 15 years on appeal, Han could see his punishment fall to roughly three years or a suspended sentence if the retrial drops insurrection, leaving only document falsification and perjury charges.
  • Who else is affected: Co-defendants including former ministers Kim Yong-hyun and Lee Sang-min—as well as the impeached and imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol and the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition—gain stronger legal arguments to contest insurrection allegations.

A non-binding remand and a custody dispute

On the November 20, 2026 broadcast, former presidential secretary Lee Kwang-chul noted that while the Supreme Court called Yoon Suk-yeol's martial law unconstitutional and unlawful, it stopped short of legally classifying it as criminal insurrection. Because the court's comments that Han's remaining charges could still stand are not legally binding, the Seoul High Court can re-evaluate the entire insurrection case from scratch.

The decision also collided with the midnight November 20 expiration of Han's detention period. Under South Korea's Criminal Procedure Act, appellate custody generally caps at eight months, and ruling Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Yong-min noted that legal practitioners are split on whether a remanded retrial restarts that clock or entitles Han to release on bail.

The broader read on martial law accountability

From a strict doctrinal standpoint, the majority's demand for hard proof of intent rather than mere presence reflects textbook criminal-law caution. Still, that standard sits uneasily alongside the reality of a constitutional crisis in which the nation's second-highest official stood at the center of an unlawful power grab.

By drawing a line between what happened before martial law was declared and what came minutes later, the ruling hands defense lawyers for other cabinet figures a ready template to argue they were passive bystanders to Yoon Suk-yeol's abrupt actions. As journalist Jang In-soo warned on the program, that reasoning risks isolating criminal blame on Yoon alone while easing legal jeopardy for the senior officials around him.
