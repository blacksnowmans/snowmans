Tectonic Shifts in AI Chips: TSMC’s Record Surge and Nvidia’s Regulatory Heat Realign Korean Tech

November 11, 2026

Record-breaking TSMC revenues and escalating US regulatory pressure on Nvidia are accelerating a global AI chip realignment, forcing South Korean memory leaders Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix to rapidly recalibrate their High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) strategies.

  • TSMC breaks monthly revenue records: Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC), the world's dominant contract chip manufacturer, reported October revenue of NT$586.9 billion (about $18 billion)—a 59.7% jump year-on-year—driven by relentless AI accelerator demand.
  • Nvidia faces mounting US congressional heat: Allegations surrounding illicit diversions of Nvidia’s restricted Blackwell chips into China—valued at roughly 86.8 billion won ($63 million)—have triggered bipartisan calls in Washington for tighter export controls on CEO Jensen Huang’s firm.
  • Big Tech pushes "anti-Nvidia" alternatives: Google rolled out four new custom AI processors this year, including 8th-generation Tensor Processing Units (TPUs), alongside aggressive pushes from AMD to offer cloud customers alternatives to Nvidia’s costly GPUs.
  • Korean memory leaders at a strategic crossroad: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, South Korea's twin semiconductor giants, face both an opportunity to diversify their HBM customer base and the threat of becoming overly dependent on TSMC’s foundry ecosystem.

Navigating Nvidia's Cracks and Big Tech's Independence

The aura of invincibility surrounding Nvidia is showing its first major political and supply chain fractures. Reports of illicit chip routing to Chinese markets have invited intense scrutiny from the US Department of Commerce and Congress, threatening stricter rules under proposed chip legislation. As Washington tightens the vise, Nvidia’s dual-track approach to retaining Chinese market access while complying with export curbs is becoming untenable. This regulatory friction comes precisely as hyperscalers like Google and AMD accelerate their own silicon initiatives. By embedding proprietary TPUs into its cloud network, Google is proving that high-performance AI deployment is possible without total reliance on Nvidia's architecture.

Strategic Imperatives for Samsung and SK Hynix

For South Korea’s memory powerhouses, this market fragmentation is a double-edged sword. On one hand, Big Tech’s shift away from a single GPU supplier broadens the market for specialized HBM chips. Samsung and SK Hynix can reduce their exposure to single-client risk as tech giants build tailored AI stacks. On the other hand, Nvidia’s proposed architectural changes—such as moving memory controllers onto the base die via its NVHBM framework—threaten to pull design authority away from memory makers and hand it to foundries. To stay competitive in the upcoming HBM4 cycle, Samsung is leaning on its fully integrated memory-plus-foundry "one-stop" model, while SK Hynix is deepening its manufacturing alliance with TSMC. How deftly both firms navigate Washington's sanctions and TSMC's expanding footprint will define South Korea's standing in the next phase of the global AI race.
