Controversial Agency Pick Sparks Ruling Party Backlash in Seoul

October 16, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s campaign to rein in South Korea’s powerful prosecution service has run into unexpected turbulence following a contentious confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong, the nominee tapped to lead the newly formed Major Crimes Investigation Agency.

  • What happened: At an October 14 parliamentary hearing, nominee Kim Ji-yong admitted he had previously recommended indicting a reform-minded prosecutor on negligence charges, directly contradicting his past public image as someone who opposed the indictment.
  • What it changes: The revelation undermines the presidential office’s claim that Kim is an uncompromised reformer, triggering alarm among ruling-bloc lawmakers who fear the new investigative body will simply reproduce the old prosecutorial mindset.
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea and President Lee, who now face accusations of managerial drift and out-of-touch personnel picks from key supporters and allied factions like the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party.
  • The political fallout: Frustration over the nomination has converged with internal party squabbles over unverified rumors, dampening grassroots enthusiasm among progressive voters.

A Flawed Nominee and Legal Double-Talk

The controversy stems from an incident during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, when prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong conducted a search of Han Dong-hoon during the 2020 "prosecutor-media collusion" probe. While Kim Ji-yong long claimed he opposed charging Jeong with abuse of authority, questioning by Democratic Party lawmaker Lee Hae-sik revealed that Kim had in fact pushed to prosecute Jeong under a different charge: occupational negligence causing injury. By simply swapping legal labels to target an investigator, Kim engaged in what lawmakers across the aisle termed an evasive prosecutorial maneuver to create a personal alibi.

Presidential public communication secretary Sung Ki-hong subsequently insisted on national radio that Kim’s performance was so clear that it was difficult to find any flaws. That defense fell flat with the public. Rather than demonstrating an appetite to dismantle entrenched legal privileges, Kim showed an obsession with narrow statutory language that disregarded institutional abuse, casting doubt on whether he possesses the ethics required to lead an agency tasked with curbing prosecutorial overreach.

Mounting Cracks Inside the Democratic Party

The hearing has laid bare tactical paralysis inside the ruling Democratic Party. Lawmakers like Yoon Gun-young openly acknowledged a feeling of futility after the session, caught between an institutional instinct to back President Lee’s executive choices and mounting warnings from lawmakers who pointed out that Kim could not even articulate a structural blueprint for the new agency. Allied voices in the Rebuilding Korea Party have warned that railroading an entrenched insider into the post will drain vital momentum from the broader reform agenda.

Compounding the problem is an unforced distraction inside party ranks. Rather than holding senior officials accountable or addressing softening public support, party figures like Representative Chung Chung-rae have amplified unverified claims that tens of thousands of members from the Shincheonji religious sect infiltrated the party rolls, with some leadership voices even floating the dangerous idea of handing internal registries over to investigators. If President Lee is to preserve the authority of his remaining term, his administration must replace technocratic evasions and paranoid intra-party feuds with genuine accountability and disciplined governance.
