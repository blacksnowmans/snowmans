South Korea Scrambles to Stop AI Cheating Days Before College Entrance Exam

November 17, 2026

South Korea has launched an inter-agency emergency response after Education Minister Choe Kyo-jin admitted that authorities cannot guarantee catching all AI-assisted cheating on the upcoming college entrance exam.

  • The Admission: Education Minister Choe Kyo-jin acknowledged technical limitations in detecting AI tools just three days before the exam, though he firmly ruled out postponing the test to avoid wider chaos.
  • Government Response: Prime Minister Han Seong-sook chaired an emergency inter-agency meeting to form a government-wide task force, which is reviewing technical countermeasures such as radio-frequency signal jamming in exam halls.
  • Legislative Push: Lawmakers from both the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the main opposition People Power Party warned of potential lawsuits from test-takers and proposed extending exam bans for cheaters to 10 years.
  • Retroactive Penalties: The ruling Democratic Party is pushing to ban offenders for 10 years or more and seeking to retroactively apply the rule to this year's exam.

High Stakes Meet Technological Deficit

In South Korea, the Suneung—the national College Scholastic Ability Test—is a high-stakes exam where a single point can determine university placement and future career paths. Minister Choe’s admission in parliament that officials "cannot block everything perfectly" immediately sparked widespread concern among students and parents. While the Ministry of Education insists that delaying the test would cause unacceptable disruption, relying on traditional visual proctoring and metal detectors has proven insufficient against modern smart devices and wireless AI tools.

To address these vulnerabilities, the government is considering physical measures such as jamming radio signals inside test centers. However, signal suppression remains in the review stage due to potential interference with nearby residential areas and official exam equipment. Without proven technical safeguards ready for immediate deployment, the burden of maintaining fairness relies almost entirely on real-time proctoring and post-exam verification.

Deterrence via Severe Penalties

With real-time detection facing technical hurdles, politicians are turning to legislative deterrence. Both ruling and opposition members on the National Assembly’s education committee warned that ambiguous oversight could lead to widespread litigation from students challenging the fairness of their results. In response, bipartisan efforts are underway to amend the law to bar individuals caught cheating from retaking the exam for up to 10 years.

The ruling Democratic Party has taken an even tougher stance, advocating for a minimum 10-year disqualification and attempting to enforce it retroactively for this year's test. However, applying new punitive measures retroactively is expected to spark legal debates over constitutional fairness. While harsher sanctions significantly raise the stakes for misconduct, they do not resolve the fundamental challenge of detecting sophisticated cheating as it happens inside the exam hall.
