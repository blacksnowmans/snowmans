Trump Claims Breakthrough at Shenzhen Summit with Xi and Putin, but North Korea Omission Sparks Alarm in Seoul

November 22, 2026

U.S. President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin held a historic trilateral summit in Shenzhen during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) meeting, agreeing in principle to prevent nuclear conflict while leaving major geopolitical flashpoints, including North Korea's nuclear program, unresolved.

  • What happened: Following the summit in Shenzhen, President Trump held a 90-minute White House press conference detailing a joint peace declaration focused on nuclear non-proliferation, UN reform, and risk management.
  • What it missed: Despite Trump’s claims of discussing Korean Peninsula security, the tripartite statement omitted any explicit mention of North Korean denuclearization, raising concerns in Seoul over potential shifts in U.S. sanctions enforcement.
  • South Korea’s reaction: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung responded by emphasizing "independent diplomacy," asserting that South Korea must lead its own destiny rather than relying on foreign powers.
  • Global fallout: European leaders pushed back against the summit's pressure for a swift end to the Ukraine war, while tech markets braced for U.S. trade pressure after Trump threatened punitive taxes on overseas data centers.

A High-Stakes Summit of Divergent Agendas

While President Trump touted "fantastic relationships" with Xi and Putin and hailed the meeting as a major success for global safety, the summit produced far more grand rhetoric than actionable policy. Trump admitted to key disagreements—such as refusing actual nuclear reductions—and offered vague positions on pressing trade, AI, and Middle East issues. Observers noted that each leader pursued conflicting goals: China used its host status in Shenzhen to showcase global influence, Russia broke out of diplomatic isolation, and the U.S. sought to project strength ahead of domestic and trade negotiations.

Seoul's Security Dilemma and Independent Diplomacy

For South Korea, the summit highlighted growing security risks. Although Trump mentioned discussing Korean Peninsula risk management, the total absence of North Korean nuclear deterrence from the official agreement triggered alarm in Seoul. Security analysts warn that if major powers manage regional risks while ignoring denuclearization, South Korea could bear the brunt of an unchecked nuclear threat—particularly if Washington unilaterally alters sanction policies.

In response, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung made it clear that Seoul will not remain a passive spectator. Emphasizing that South Korea must take the helm in shaping its own strategic fate, the Lee administration is reinforcing a stance of self-reliant diplomacy. As the three major powers pursue their own interests, South Korea faces the delicate task of maintaining proactive foreign policy while steering international diplomacy toward genuine stability on the peninsula.
