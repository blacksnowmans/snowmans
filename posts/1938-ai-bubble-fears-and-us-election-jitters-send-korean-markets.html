AI Bubble Fears and US Election Jitters Send Korean Markets Into a Tailspin

November 04, 2026

On November 3, South Korea’s financial markets suffered a massive sell-off as foreign investors retreated from semiconductor giants, driven by mounting skepticism over the global artificial intelligence (AI) boom and political uncertainty in the United States.

  • Massive Foreign Exit: Foreign investors offloaded more than $940 million (1.3 trillion KRW) in shares, dragging the KOSPI (the benchmark Korea Composite Stock Price Index) down by over 3% in a single day.
  • Tech Giants Hit Hard: Shares of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—South Korea’s two premier semiconductor manufacturers—plunged between 4% and 5% as the global "AI bubble" narrative gained steam.
  • The Oracle Trigger: A $20 billion capital-raising move by U.S.-based Oracle sparked fears that the massive investments required for AI infrastructure may not yield the expected returns, chilling the global tech value chain.
  • US Political Risk: With the U.S. midterm elections approaching, global institutional investors are aggressively shedding "risk assets," hitting the export-heavy Korean market particularly hard.

The End of the AI Honeymoon?

The primary driver of this "Black Sunday" was a sudden shift in sentiment regarding the profitability of artificial intelligence. For months, the market has been buoyed by the "AI Revolution," pushing SK Hynix to record highs due to its dominance in High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM). However, the narrative has shifted from growth potential to "investment recovery." Investors are now asking the blunt question: When will these tens of billions of dollars in AI spending actually turn a profit?

The impact was exacerbated by the fragile state of the Korean market. As a small, open economy heavily dependent on tech exports, South Korea often acts as a "canary in the coal mine" for global tech sentiment. When the Nasdaq fluctuates due to U.S. political anxiety or corporate funding news, the KOSPI frequently experiences a magnified shock. Under the current Lee Jae-myung administration, the market remains highly sensitive to these external shocks, particularly as foreign capital seeks safer harbors ahead of potential shifts in U.S. trade and tech policy.

Analysts Signal a Shift to Defense

In the wake of the crash, domestic brokerage analysts have pivoted to a starkly conservative outlook. The optimism that defined the early half of the year has been replaced by warnings of a "semiconductor winter." Experts are now advising retail investors to prioritize cash holdings rather than "buying the dip." Some extreme downside scenarios are being circulated in the financial district, suggesting that the floor for these tech leaders may be significantly lower than previously anticipated if the AI bubble continues to deflate.

A Crisis of Confidence in Corporate Policy

The downturn has also brought scrutiny to the "Shareholder Return" policies of major firms. While Samsung Electronics and other giants have attempted to bolster stock prices through dividends and buybacks, critics argue these are merely temporary fixes. In a market gripped by fears of a structural slowdown in memory demand, investors are signaling that they value technological moats and clear earnings paths over financial engineering. For now, the "wait-and-see" approach dominates, as the market looks toward the U.S. election results to gauge the next direction for global trade.

"The market isn't just reacting to a price drop; it is reacting to an absence of a visible floor. Until the uncertainty regarding AI profitability and U.S. policy clears, defensive positioning is the only rational move." — Market analyst briefing.
