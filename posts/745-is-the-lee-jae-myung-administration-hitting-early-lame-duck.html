Is the Lee Jae-myung Administration Hitting Early Lame Duck?

October 05, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung's administration is facing intense internal turmoil less than two years into its term, marked by the abrupt resignation of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik and explosive allegations of internal bullying from a prominent political journalist.
  • What it changes: The leaks and factional infighting signal a rapid erosion of executive authority, exposing deep fractures between President Lee's core loyalists (the "Seongnam faction") and the broader Democratic Party.
  • Who is affected: President Lee's grip on power is weakening fast, threatening a fracturing of the ruling liberal Democratic Party and severely damaging the administration's legislative agenda.

Cracks at the Top: Staff Resignations and Leaks

Less than two years after taking office, President Lee Jae-myung's administration is showing unmistakable signs of an early lame-duck period. Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik abruptly tendered his resignation after 15 months in office, following leaked political gossip reports claiming the president accused him of orchestrating a negative press campaign against rival factions. While political gossip sheets should always be taken with a grain of salt, Kang's sudden exit points to severe internal friction.

The departure has triggered a fierce turf war over his successor. Rumors suggest the "Seongnam faction"—officials tied to Lee’s earlier local political base—attempted to install their own candidate, only to be blocked by the ruling Democratic Party. If true, it demonstrates that President Lee's inner circle is rapidly losing its absolute grip on power as public approval ratings slide and internal loyalty frays.

The Press Corps and the Coming Democratic Split

The sense of a crumbling administration was further amplified when journalist Park Hyun-kwang appeared on a political YouTube broadcast to publicly allege that he had been subjected to institutionalized bullying inside the presidential office. The explosive claim underscores an administration whose authority has sunk low enough that reporters feel emboldened to air internal grievances publicly. Meanwhile, the ruling Democratic Party—bound together by political expediency rather than shared ideology—faces an inevitable reckoning between Lee's die-hard loyalists and traditional party members.

Just as previous administrations collapsed due to a stubborn refusal to listen to dissenting voices, critics argue President Lee is repeating the exact same playbook. With key posts sitting vacant and ruling party figures quietly hedging their bets, the administration's insular leadership style risks accelerating its own political downfall.
