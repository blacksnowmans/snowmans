President Lee Bypasses Traditional Media as Markets Tumble

October 16, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung is increasingly using direct social media broadcasts to drive his administration’s agenda, bypassing traditional news filters even as a deepening stock market crisis and controversial personnel appointments spark public anxiety.

  • Rapid Appointment: President Lee officially appointed Kim Ji-yong as the head of the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA) just five hours after receiving the personnel report, signaling an aggressive push for prosecutorial reform.
  • Market Silence: Despite the domestic stock market index crashing to the 5,500 mark, the President’s social media channels have remained silent on the economic carnage, focusing instead on internal political victories.
  • Influencer Interference: Concerns are mounting over the outsized influence of Kim Eo-jun, a controversial pro-government media personality, whose public "signals" appear to be dictating high-level government appointments.
  • Mixed Messaging: A recent SNS post by the President defending "selfless prosecutors" has confused his core supporters, who expected a more thorough purge of the judiciary following the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The Perils of "Live" Governance

The Lee Jae-myung administration has effectively turned political communication into a "live" performance. By using real-time broadcasts to interact directly with citizens—often calling out supporters by their usernames—the President has fostered a sense of intimacy that traditional media cannot match. However, this "unfiltered" approach is a double-edged sword. While it builds a loyal community of followers, it risks creating a dangerous feedback loop of confirmation bias. When the President’s team adjusts policy "thumbnails" in real-time based on live chat reactions, the line between populist entertainment and serious governance begins to blur.

The most pressing concern is the perceived privatization of state messaging. The narrative that influential outsiders like Kim Eo-jun are pulling the strings behind the scenes undermines the legitimacy of the Democratic Party of Korea’s (the current ruling party) institutional processes. If the public believes that the head of a major investigative body like the SCIA was chosen because of a podcaster’s signal rather than a rigorous vetting process, the government’s independence is compromised. Politics may be a "show" to some, but for a government still navigating the post-impeachment era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the optics of outside influence are toxic.

Economic Disconnect and the Reform Trap

There is a jarring disconnect between the administration's digital triumphalism and the reality of the Korean markets. While the President’s SNS accounts celebrate the "completion of reform" through the Kim Ji-yong appointment, the stock market’s collapse to the 5,500 level represents a catastrophic loss of wealth for the middle class. To the average worker worried about the rising cost of living, the technicalities of "separating investigation from prosecution" feel like a distant, elitist concern. By remaining silent on the market crash while posting about prosecutorial ethics, the President risks being seen as out of touch with the very "minsaeng" (people's livelihoods) he promised to protect.

Furthermore, the President’s recent rhetoric regarding "untainted prosecutors" suggests a strategic pivot that may alienate his base. After campaigning on a platform of dismantling the "prosecutorial republic" associated with the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, Lee’s softer tone is being interpreted by some as a sign of reform fatigue or a tactical retreat. If the administration continues to prioritize political theater and internal loyalty over concrete economic intervention, the social media intimacy that brought them to power could quickly turn into a digital platform for public grievance.
