Inside South Korea's Progressive Infighting: The Dinner Meeting That Stirred the Left

October 03, 2026

A late-night social media defense by South Korean President Lee Jae-myung over a private dinner with a senior party figure has exposed deep, bitter fractures within the country's progressive camp as supporters turn their scrutiny inward.

  • What happened: President Lee took to X (formerly Twitter) to push back against online rumors that he had bullied and dominated former Democratic Party leader Jung Chung-rae during a four-hour abalone-porridge dinner.
  • Who is affected: South Korea's ruling progressive bloc, including prominent figures like commentator Rhyu Si-min and senior politician Kim Min-seok, who are facing fierce internal criticism and accusations of arrogance.
  • What it changes: The public feud highlights a growing exhaustion with ideological purity tests and cancel-culture tactics among progressive voters, threatening the administration's momentum on key reforms.

The Perils of Policing Allies

The online uproar over a bowl of simple porridge shows how paranoid South Korea's political factions have become. When a president's every casual meal is scrutinized as a psychological dominance ritual over subordinates, it signals a toxic culture of suspicion. Commentators and hardcore supporters are increasingly desperate for a scapegoat whenever the administration fails to wage total war against its political opponents. But treating fellow reformers as enemies only serves to isolate the leadership.

This self-defeating impulse is compounded by prominent voices like liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min, who recently criticized the administration's handling of prosecution reform and leadership appointments as reminiscent of a monarchy. While internal critique is vital, the habit of using derogatory nicknames and branding dissenting views as treasonous leaves the progressive movement fractured. As the old political metaphor goes, politicians must stop walking with small stones in their shoes until their feet bleed just to avoid admitting discomfort.

Moving From Factions to Focus

Politicians like senior lawmaker Kim Min-seok have attempted to project relentless diligence through marathon grassroots site visits, from postal sorting centers to public sanitation posts. Yet physical stamina cannot replace a coherent political strategy. For South Korea's progressives, the path forward requires abandoning the obsessive search for internal traitors and refocusing on coalition-building. Unless the factional infighting stops, the administration risks squandering its mandate on petty grievances rather than substantive reform.
