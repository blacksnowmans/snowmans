The Hidden Cost of Temu’s Ultra-Low Prices: Regulatory Arbitrage

September 09, 2026

Temu’s rapid rise in South Korea—and globally—is not a triumph of retail innovation, but rather a masterclass in exploiting regulatory loopholes to undercut local competitors.

  • The "Regulatory Arbitrage" Play: By shipping goods directly to consumers under the guise of "personal imports," Temu avoids the tariffs, safety inspections, and warehouse taxes that local retailers like Coupang (South Korea’s answer to Amazon) must pay.
  • Algorithmic Exploitation: Temu’s pricing model forces factories into a race to the bottom, often sacrificing product safety and environmental standards to hit rock-bottom price points.
  • The Local Wall: South Korea has proven a tough market for Temu, largely due to Daiso—a ubiquitous, trusted discount chain that offers a "good enough" experience at a similar price point without the shipping risks.

The "Daiso Effect" and Consumer Trust

While Temu has gained millions of users, its actual transaction volume in South Korea is relatively low. South Korean consumers, accustomed to the convenience and reliability of Daiso, are wary of the "cheap for a reason" nature of Temu’s catalog. With roughly 50% of items from such platforms often ending up in the trash due to poor quality, Korean shoppers are increasingly prioritizing safety and local accountability over bottom-barrel prices.

The numbers reflect this skepticism. Despite billions of dollars (over 4 trillion KRW) flowing through Chinese e-commerce platforms like AliExpress and Temu combined, their impact remains limited compared to their global expansion. Consumers are essentially performing a cost-benefit analysis: they would rather pay a dollar more at a local store than gamble on a package that may arrive broken or toxic.

Global Regulatory Shifts and the Path Forward

Major economies are finally closing the loopholes Temu has thrived on. The U.S. is tightening its "de minimis" exemption (the $800 threshold for duty-free imports), and the EU is moving to eliminate similar tax breaks by 2028. South Korea’s Fair Trade Commission has already signaled its intent to act, recently fining Temu about $1 million (1.37 billion KRW) for unfair practices, but industry experts argue this is just the beginning.

The reality is simple: there is no such thing as a free lunch in retail. If a product is suspiciously cheap, someone—either the environment, the exploited factory worker, or the consumer’s own safety—is paying the difference. For a fair market to exist, platforms like Temu must be held to the same standards as local distributors. Without mandatory safety inspections and equal tax burdens, they are not disrupting the market; they are simply breaking the game.
