Internal Strife: South Korea’s Main Opposition Party Faces Leadership Crisis

October 02, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's primary liberal opposition party, is currently embroiled in an internal power struggle as party leadership attempts to silence critics, including prominent political YouTubers, amid allegations of "party privatization."

  • What happened: Kim Min-seok, a senior DPK leader, faced a legal challenge—an injunction to suspend his duties—after his camp threatened disciplinary action against "Sisa Tapa TV," a popular pro-liberal YouTube channel.
  • The provocation: The DPK leadership threatened to strip the channel’s host, Lee Jong-won, of his party membership, claiming he leaked member data and manipulated opinion polls during the party’s national convention.
  • The fallout: When it emerged that the party lacked legal grounds for such "emergency disciplinary" measures, the leadership distanced itself, claiming it was a local matter. Critics argue this was a failed attempt to suppress dissent.
  • Who is affected: The move has alienated core supporters and raised alarms about the party’s direction, with critics suggesting leadership is prioritizing personal political preservation over party unity.

The Struggle for Control and "Party Privatization"

The core of this controversy lies in Kim Min-seok’s efforts to consolidate power. Lacking a dedicated personal "fandom" of his own, Kim rose to prominence by leveraging the massive base that supports President Lee Jae-myung. Observers argue that Kim is now attempting to hijack this support to build a personal faction, often referred to as the "Pro-Kim" group, effectively turning the party into a tool for his own political survival.

By framing internal criticism as "anti-party activity," the leadership is attempting to purge dissenters. However, the legal stakes are high: if the allegations of opinion poll manipulation during the convention are investigated, it could prove fatal to Kim’s political career. The desperation to stifle the investigation suggests that the leadership fears a deeper truth may emerge.

A Crisis of Political Maturity

The tone of the current leadership has drawn intense fire, particularly their use of language that implies a hierarchy between representatives and party members. The frequent use of the term chi-ha (praising or rewarding subordinates), which is traditionally reserved for superiors addressing subordinates, has been interpreted as a sign of arrogance that has deeply offended the party’s grassroots base.

The DPK is arguably losing its focus. While the country faces urgent issues—such as local government budget shortfalls and the management of a massive "Future Response Fund"—the party leadership remains preoccupied with internal disciplinary hearings and jockeying for future nomination power.

"True leadership in a democratic party is measured by the ability to accept constructive criticism. By turning their backs on internal dialogue and treating the party as a private organization, the current leadership is failing to fulfill its role as a healthy political entity."

Ultimately, this internal battle reflects a broader rot in South Korean politics. As both the ruling and opposition parties become increasingly consumed by factional infighting and the influence of extreme internet pundits, the public’s frustration grows. For the DPK, the path to winning future elections does not lie in purging critics, but in restoring the democratic values that the party claims to represent.
