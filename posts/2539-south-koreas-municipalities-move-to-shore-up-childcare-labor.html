South Korea’s Municipalities Move to Shore Up Childcare Labor Ahead of National Integration

November 20, 2026

South Korean local governments and childcare associations nationwide are rolling out legal protections, mental health initiatives, and specialized training for early childhood staff as the country prepares for a sweeping consolidation of its preschool and daycare systems.

  • Legal relief for staffing shortages: A committee of the Busan City Council passed an ordinance establishing public funding for substitute teachers when daycare staff take sick leave or vacation, addressing chronic understaffing.
  • Closing wage and meal disparities: Daejeon’s Daedeok District announced a new teacher allowance for private and home-based centers starting next year, while the Korea Educare Association staged protests demanding budget allocations to eliminate inequities in child meal subsidies across facilities.
  • Modernizing educator and parent skills: Centers in Seoul’s Gangnam District and Jeju’s Seogwipo launched specialized workshops on parenting in the artificial intelligence era, alongside regional retreats and burnout-prevention programs in places like Mokpo and Chuncheon.
  • Preparing for systemic overhaul: Regional education offices, including Incheon’s Ganghwa Education Support Office and the Gyeonggi Women and Family Foundation, opened formal consultations to manage the upcoming merger of early education (historically under the Ministry of Education) and childcare services (under the Ministry of Health and Welfare).

Why Childcare Labor Is Moving to the Center Stage

South Korea’s early childhood crisis is often framed entirely around its record-low birth rate, but municipalities are finally acknowledging the collapse happening on the supply side. Daycare teachers have long operated under intense emotional stress, low pay, and an inability to use basic sick leave without leaving classrooms unstaffed. When private and small home-based daycare providers struggle to guarantee equal meal funding or time off, the quality of care drops precipitously. Legal mechanisms like Busan's substitute teacher pool and Daedeok's supplemental pay signal an overdue shift: treating daycare labor as vital public infrastructure rather than disposable private work.

The Looming Hurdle of Systemic Integration

These local actions arrive just as South Korea attempts yubo tonghap, the decades-delayed full unification of kindergartens and daycare centers. While merging administrative oversight under a unified standard is technically rational, the divide on the ground remains enormous. Disparities in physical infrastructure—such as the recent 2.4 billion won (about $1.7 million) energy-efficiency overhaul at Gimhae’s municipal hall daycare versus underfunded neighborhood sites—mirror deep splits in teacher certification, operational budgets, and daily workloads. Unless the central and local governments resolve baseline working conditions and funding equity first, administrative consolidation will merely paper over the burnout driving educators out of the sector.
