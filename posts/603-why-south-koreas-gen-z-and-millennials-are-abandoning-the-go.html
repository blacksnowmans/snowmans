Why South Korea's Gen Z and Millennials Are Abandoning the Government

September 10, 2026

The honeymoon period for the Lee Jae-myung administration is effectively over, as a growing sense of betrayal among young South Koreans has triggered a sharp decline in approval ratings.

  • What happened: Once-vocal supporters in their 20s and 30s have retreated into silence, withdrawing from political activism and digital community discussions.
  • The underlying cause: A deep disconnect between the government’s high-level political rhetoric—such as debates over prosecutorial reform—and the harsh economic realities facing young people.
  • Who is affected: The "2030 generation," who feel squeezed by an unforgiving housing market, failed stock investments, and labor policies that appear to favor corporations over workers.

The Erosion of Trust

The collapse in support is not a sudden reaction to a single policy, but a cumulative result of "political burnout." Many young people who volunteered, donated, and campaigned for the current government feel that their efforts were squandered on internal factional infighting and exclusionary "witch hunts" within the Democratic Party of Korea, the main liberal opposition party. Having exhausted their energy to secure a political victory, they now find themselves disillusioned as the government appears to mimic the very institutional stubbornness they fought to replace.

Furthermore, the government’s communication strategy is failing. While officials issue repetitive briefings on policy achievements, these messages never reach the daily lives of the average young person. For those struggling to pay rent or recovering from stock market losses, these briefings sound like tone-deaf noise. The "community support" base that once defended the government online has effectively gone on strike, leaving the administration isolated against rising cynicism.

Economic Despair: The Six-Pyung Reality

The most tangible source of anger is the brutal housing market. While official statistics suggest average monthly rents for a studio apartment (or wonrum) are around 650,000 won (about $480), these figures ignore reality. Once mandatory management fees are added, young residents often pay upwards of 1 million won (about $750) per month for tiny six-pyung (roughly 200-square-foot) spaces. Given that the median monthly income for those in their 20s is approximately 2.59 million won (about $1,900), the cost of housing is effectively stripping them of their financial independence.

Compounding this is a sense of betrayal regarding investment and labor. Many young people, finding the path to home ownership blocked by strict government loan regulations, turned to the stock market as a last-resort investment tool. When those markets crashed, the government's response—often seen as intervening to protect corporate interests during labor disputes—only deepened the perception that the administration is a "shield for the establishment" rather than a protector of the working class. Until the government pivots from abstract reforms to addressing the immediate, grinding costs of daily survival, the alienation of the 2030 generation will likely continue to accelerate.
