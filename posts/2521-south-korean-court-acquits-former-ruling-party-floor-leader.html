South Korean Court Acquits Former Ruling Party Floor Leader Over Martial Law Vote

November 19, 2026

A South Korean first-instance court has acquitted lawmaker Choo Kyung-ho of obstructing the National Assembly's vote to lift the December 3 martial law decree, drawing sharp criticism from panelists on the YouTube broadcast "Kim Eo-jun's Humility Is Hard News Factory" who argue the ruling reflects a broader effort to narrow insurrection trials.

  • The timeline at issue: Between 11:03 p.m. on December 3 and 12:03 a.m. on December 4, Choo—then floor leader of the People Power Party, which was the ruling party at the time and is now South Korea's main conservative opposition—shifted his party's emergency meeting site four times between the National Assembly and party headquarters around a roughly one-minute phone call with Yoon Suk Yeol at 11:22 p.m.
  • The court's reasoning: The trial court found no audio recording of the call and insufficient objective evidence to reject Choo's denial of obstruction, adding that his notice changes did not affect the outcome because other lawmakers met the quorum and passed the resolution anyway.
  • The backlash: Broadcast guests—including journalists Hong Sa-hoon and Joo Jin-woo, Democratic Party lawmaker Yoon Kun-young, and Rebuilding Korea Party Innovation Policy Research Institute head Cho Kuk—accused the court of reversing cause and effect and distorting key facts.
  • What changes: Combined with an earlier verdict involving former director Cho Tae-yong, critics contend that courts are restricting insurrection culpability to a few central figures—Yoon Suk Yeol, Kim Keon-hee, and Kim Yong-hyun—ahead of major Supreme Court rulings.

Evidentiary Standards vs. Circumstantial Reality

At the heart of the controversy is how strictly criminal courts should weigh circumstantial timelines when direct recordings are absent. Applying the criminal burden of proof, the first-instance bench concluded it was hard to see any request to block lawmakers or obstruct the vote in the 11:22 p.m. call, declining to infer guilt solely from Choo's rapid venue reversals.

Critics on the broadcast argue that treating the absence of a recording as decisive sets an unrealistic standard for private communications among top officials during a national crisis. They note that martial law was lifted only because lawmakers from the Democratic Party—now South Korea's ruling party—and the minor Rebuilding Korea Party climbed over walls to secure the chamber, meaning the court effectively used their success to clear Choo. Panelists also disputed the judgment's factual framing, alleging it inaccurately portrayed where the Rebuilding Korea Party convened and suggested the Democratic Party knew about martial law in advance while the People Power Party did not.

What the Acquittal Signals for Supreme Court Rulings

Beyond one lawmaker's legal fate, the decision highlights competing interpretations of the December 3 crisis. Viewed purely through procedural law, acquittals of individual officials can be read as judges enforcing strict proof requirements case by case. Viewed through the critics' political lens, however, pairing Choo's acquittal with the Cho Tae-yong ruling suggests a pattern of separating peripheral ministers and politicians from core leaders—potentially limiting institutional fallout even up to Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

The next test will come at the Supreme Court, where broadcast commentators expect fierce divisions between majority and dissenting opinions. The ruling also adds pressure on the Democratic Party, whose leadership faces internal criticism from observers urging clearer strategic resolve rather than hesitation over impeachment and reform measures.
