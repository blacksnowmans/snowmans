Allegations of Double Standards Resurface Over Han Dong-hoon Daughter Investigation

October 12, 2026

South Korea's investigative agencies face renewed scrutiny over allegations that prosecutors and police deliberately stonewalled an inquiry into former justice minister Han Dong-hoon’s daughter while aggressively pursuing cases against political rivals.

  • Zero searches conducted: Despite allegations of academic ghostwriting and copyright infringement involving Han’s daughter, police conducted no search-and-seizure operations, questioning Han's wife only once in writing and skipping Han and his daughter entirely.
  • Blocked international cooperation: The Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office reportedly suppressed a police request for assistance from U.S. authorities rather than forwarding it to the Ministry of Justice, raising claims of an internal cover-up.
  • Stark contrast with political opponents: Critics point out that investigators conducted more than 100 raids against former justice minister Cho Kuk's family and 131 search operations over a roughly $55 (76,000 won) corporate card charge involving first lady Kim Hye-kyung.
  • Judicial tension: The controversy surfaces as President Lee Jae-myung faces lingering judicial risks, with the ruling Democratic Party under pressure to confront the judiciary led by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

A Glaring Asymmetry in Law Enforcement

During the administration of Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was later impeached and imprisoned—Han Dong-hoon served as justice minister and wielded vast influence over law enforcement. Revelations raised during recent parliamentary audits indicate that police treated complaints against Han's family with remarkable deference. While the civic activist who filed the initial complaint was summoned six times, Han was never questioned, and his daughter faced neither in-person questioning nor searches. Furthermore, despite claims by National Investigation Headquarters chief Woo Jong-soo that exhaustive witness interviews took place, records show not a single witness was formally questioned.

The handling of overseas evidence raises even sharper questions. The probe involved serious allegations, including an on-the-record interview with a Kenyan national claiming to have ghostwritten an academic paper for pay. Yet when police sought mutual legal assistance from U.S. authorities, the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office shelved the paperwork, allegedly calling it too weak for Washington to accept. Given standard protocols that require prosecutors to transmit such international requests through the justice ministry, critics argue the prosecution effectively acted as a shield for its own former minister.

Selective Justice Undermines Institutional Trust

The controversy is less about a single high-profile student resume than about the selective application of prosecutorial power in South Korea. The relentless search warrants executed against opposition figures stand in sharp, indefensible contrast to the complete absence of physical searches in Han’s case. When a law enforcement system bends its intensity depending on who holds power, public faith in the rule of law inevitably collapses.

For the ruling Democratic Party and the Lee Jae-myung administration, these unresolved investigations highlight an urgent, unfinished agenda: dismantling the culture of impunity within the prosecutor's office. With looming court rulings concerning former first lady Kim Keon-hee and high-stakes legal battles involving the current administration, the judiciary and prosecution will find little public patience left if they continue to operate on double standards.
