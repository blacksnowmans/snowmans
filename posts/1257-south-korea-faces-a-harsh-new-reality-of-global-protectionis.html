South Korea Faces a Harsh New Reality of Global Protectionism and Transactional Diplomacy

October 18, 2026

A turbulent wave of foreign policy shocks across Washington, Brussels, and Tokyo has delivered a blunt warning to South Korea: the post-Cold War international order is giving way to unapologetic protectionism and transactional diplomacy.

  • EU EV Subsidy Walls: The European Union is advancing strict local-content rules requiring electric vehicles to contain 70% European-made parts to receive subsidies, threatening painful supply-chain disruptions for South Korean automakers and battery manufacturers.
  • Yasukuni Offering Reignites Friction: Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi sent a ceremonial masakaki offering to the Yasukuni Shrine for its autumn festival, drawing immediate protests and deep regret from Seoul's Ministry of Foreign Affairs.
  • Transactional Washington: Amid White House personnel turmoil—punctuated by the dismissal of Press Secretary Katie Zacharias after just four days over falsified credential claims—Donald Trump praised Hyundai Motor's large-scale investments in Georgia while simultaneously complaining that allies like South Korea do not contribute enough for defense.
  • Multilateral Paralysis: Outages on Ukraine's Starshield satellite network exposed the perils of relying on privatized infrastructure, while UN Security Council veto politics deadlocked the selection of the next UN Secretary-General, leaving international mediation largely toothless.

The Squeeze on Korean Industry and Diplomacy

The most immediate blow lands on South Korea's industrial core. Korean automakers and battery makers poured capital into Western markets on the premise that global decarbonization would reward technological front-runners. Instead, Brussels is following Washington's lead by weaponizing subsidies to defend domestic champions. If the EU locks in its 70% European-origin rule, Korean firms will face an agonizing choice: spend billions building out European production lines at inflated costs, or forfeit competitiveness in a crucial market.

Meanwhile, Prime Minister Takaichi's gesture toward Yasukuni—which honors wartime leaders alongside Japan's war dead—reminds Seoul of the fragility of Northeast Asian diplomacy. From the viewpoint of policymakers in Seoul, the move highlights the shortcomings of the previous approach under Yoon Suk-yeol (who was subsequently impeached and imprisoned), whose administration offered unilateral concessions to Tokyo without securing lasting reciprocal changes in Japan's historical stance.

Seoul's Turn Toward Hard-Nosed Realism

For President Lee Jae-myung's administration, these compounding pressures demand a foreign policy anchored strictly in national interest rather than ideological alignment. When Washington welcomes massive Korean corporate investments with one hand while brandishing defense-cost demands with the other, Seoul cannot afford deference. Navigating this climate requires securing critical resource supply chains, protecting domestic manufacturing fundamentals, and demanding mutual reciprocity from allies and trading partners alike.
