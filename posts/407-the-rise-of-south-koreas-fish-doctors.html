The Rise of South Korea’s "Fish Doctors"

September 08, 2026

While most associate pets with dogs or cats, South Korea is seeing a quiet boom in the "companion fish" industry, driven by an aging population and a growing desire for unconventional emotional support. As the market for aquarium fish expands to hundreds of billions of won (hundreds of millions of dollars), a rare breed of specialists—marine biologists and veterinarians—has emerged to treat these small, silent patients.

  • A niche market hits the mainstream: Fish are now the third most popular pet in South Korea, following dogs and cats, fueling a market worth hundreds of billions of won.
  • Professional care emerges: Despite the market's size, there are only 3 to 4 specialists in the country dedicated to treating aquarium fish.
  • Preventative care over profit: New guidelines, such as the National Institute of Fisheries Science's "Freshwater Ornamental Fish Disease Manual," mark a shift toward proactive health management.

The Experts Behind the Tanks

The recent surge in public interest follows a spotlight on three veteran experts: Lee Wan-ok of the Korea Freshwater Fish Conservation Association, marine biologist Hwang Seon-do, and Choi Sang-ho, who runs Seoul's only dedicated hospital for pet fish. Their stories reveal that the field is built not on grand plans, but on decades of persistence.

Choi’s career, in particular, illustrates the irony of a life path. Initially entering the field of marine science due to academic entrance exam scores rather than a grand calling, he transitioned through theology and business management before finally returning to his lifelong fascination with fish. Now, he operates a hospital that, by conventional business logic, is a money-losing venture. Yet, for pet owners across the capital, his expertise is a lifeline. In a society obsessed with high-yield investments, his commitment to treating small, non-traditional pets acts as a form of social resistance against the purely transactional nature of the modern economy.

More Than Just a Hobby

The "fish doctor" movement serves as a litmus test for South Korea's evolving empathy toward life. Historically, South Korean fisheries research focused heavily on "food fish"—production and consumption. Treating a fish as a family member rather than a meal is a relatively new cultural shift. For many urbanites, the simple act of watching fish swim at dawn provides a necessary psychological refuge from the high-pressure environment of South Korean society.

However, the industry faces structural gaps. While tens of thousands of hobbyists belong to aquarium clubs, the scarcity of professional medical care means that most sick fish are left to die due to a lack of resources. The experts argue that the solution isn't just better medicine, but a change in how we perceive the environment. By fostering interest in native species—like the "Buan-jonggae" (a loach species)—through conservation and proper care, they hope to preserve ecosystems that humans have largely ignored.

Ultimately, the work of these specialists confirms that a meaningful life is rarely a straight line. Whether it is finding a new species or simply keeping a tank balanced, these professionals have spent their lives "staying in their lane" to protect the quiet, submerged world that keeps our own planet’s ecology intact.
