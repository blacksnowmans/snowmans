South Korea’s Economic Pivot to ‘Growth First’ Fails Working Families

November 21, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has effectively abandoned his progressive agenda of universal basic support in favor of conservative, growth-first policies that are worsening income inequality and job stagnation.

  • The Policy Shift: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea has sidelined its founding commitments to economic democratization, adopting traditional trickle-down strategies centered on mega-industrial projects.
  • Worsening Indicators: Real monthly wages have dropped to 4.066 million won (about $2,950), performing worse than during the final year of Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency.
  • Vanishing Jobs: Monthly regular job growth has collapsed by nearly 60% year-over-year, while youth employment among those aged 15–34 is shrinking at double its previous rate.
  • Decoupled Growth: Key tech sectors like semiconductors are posting export gains but generating negative net job growth due to high automation.

The Return of Trickle-Down Logic

During his election campaign, President Lee Jae-myung promised a "basic society"—a social democratic platform focused on curbing inequality, bolstering economic democratization, and establishing baseline welfare guarantees. Today, that vision has been replaced by a conservative "grow the pie first, distribute later" strategy. The administration’s current stance assumes that corporate expansion will naturally lift all boats. However, with real household spending falling back to 2017 levels and bad debt surging among small business owners, working-class households are seeing none of those trickle-down benefits.

Disillusionment is mounting among the country's 14.5 million non-regular workers and self-employed individuals who form the backbone of domestic consumption. Non-regular workers now earn just 52% of regular employees' wages—a record gap. Rather than reinforcing social safety nets to cushion these vulnerable workers, the government has prioritized fiscal discipline and corporate investments, such as backing Samsung’s 113 trillion won (about $82 billion) semiconductor facility in Asan. For everyday workers facing stagnant wages and rising living costs, these mega-projects feel disconnected from daily survival.

Why the Old Industrial Playbook Is Failing

The fundamental flaw in President Lee’s strategy lies in South Korea's altered industrial structure. During the rapid industrialization of the late 20th century, every 1 billion won (about $725,000) in GDP generated roughly 78 new jobs. Today, in heavily automated, capital-intensive sectors like semiconductors and AI, that employment creation coefficient has dropped to -0.22. High-tech exports buoy macro statistics, but their revenue remains concentrated among a tiny pool of conglomerates and top-tier earners who hold low marginal propensities to consume locally.

By prioritizing corporate volume over structural wealth redistribution, the administration risks repeating past policy failures where headliner export growth masked severe domestic freezes. Without a return to direct wage protections, targeted safety nets, and distribution-oriented reforms, President Lee’s focus on top-line growth will only deepen the divide between corporate balances and everyday living standards.
