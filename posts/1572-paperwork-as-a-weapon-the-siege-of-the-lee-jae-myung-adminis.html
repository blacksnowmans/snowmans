Paperwork as a Weapon: The Siege of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 28, 2026

The current political landscape in South Korea is being defined by a staggering 150,000-page legal record and a president attempting to redefine executive communication through social media.

  • Information Overload: Prosecutors have submitted a massive 150,000-page case file regarding a Special Counsel bill on "fabricated indictments," a move critics label as a tactical attempt to paralyze the defense and the judiciary.
  • Emotional Governance: President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has released a first-anniversary speech collection that uniquely includes his personal SNS messages, aiming to dismantle traditional authoritarian imagery.
  • Supervision Row: The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, is accusing President Lee of neglecting his constitutional duties after he suggested that heads of investigative agencies need not attend every Cabinet meeting.
  • Systemic Inertia: The ongoing legal battles highlight a deep-seated "presumption of guilt" within the Korean judicial system, where the sheer volume of prosecution evidence often coerces judges into delivering guilty verdicts.

Judicial Violence and the 150,000-Page Barrier

The disclosure that 150,000 pages of investigation records have been compiled is not a sign of thoroughness, but rather a structural barrier to justice. In the South Korean legal system, this "paperwork mountain" serves as a weapon. For a judge to declare a defendant innocent, they must meticulously debunk every claim within those thousands of pages. Conversely, the path of least resistance is often to accept the prosecution’s narrative. This creates a "judicial coma" where the presumption of innocence—a pillar of the constitution—is effectively buried under ink and paper.

This tactic is particularly pointed given the current administration's push for reform. By overwhelming the courts, the prosecution (often seen as a vestige of the previous era) maintains control over the political narrative. It transforms the courtroom from a place of fact-finding into an endurance test that few defendants, even those with significant resources, can survive. This is the "judicial monster" that President Lee's supporters say must be dismantled to protect human rights.

The Trap of Presidential Flexibility

President Lee’s attempt to modernize the presidency is meeting stiff resistance from an opposition eager to use his own words against him. By including SNS posts in his official speech records, Lee is attempting to build a direct, "emotional" bond with the public, bypassing traditional media filters. This reflects a shift from the rigid, 의전 (protocol)-heavy style of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment and removal from office. Lee’s style is "soft power" applied to domestic politics, seeking to prove that a leader can be both powerful and relatable.

However, this flexibility has created a political opening for the PPP. When President Lee told the head of the Central Investigation Agency (JSCheong) that monthly attendance at Cabinet meetings was sufficient, the opposition immediately framed it as a "neglect of duty." There is a sharp irony here: the PPP is using the same "impeachment" rhetoric that led to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s downfall. In the hyper-polarized atmosphere of Seoul, a president's attempt at "humanitarian consideration" for his staff is quickly rebranded as a constitutional crisis. For President Lee, the challenge remains: how to maintain a soft, communicative image without providing the "hyenas" of the opposition the ammunition they need to paralyze his reform agenda.
