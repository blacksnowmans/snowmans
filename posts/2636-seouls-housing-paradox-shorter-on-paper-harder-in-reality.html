Seoul’s Housing Paradox: Shorter on Paper, Harder in Reality

November 23, 2026

South Korea’s latest official housing survey claims it now takes two fewer years to buy a home in Seoul, even as high-end apartment sales stall and tenants brace for a 36% jump in lease deposits next year.

  • A statistical mirage: Despite 93 straight weeks of rising Seoul apartment prices, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport reported that the capital’s price-to-income ratio fell from 14 years to 11.7 years—a distortion driven by lagging price data and survey samples skewed toward higher-income households.
  • Parental wealth drives young buyers: The number of people in their 30s owning homes worth more than 1.7 billion won (about $1.2 million) surged 70% in a year to 18,019, fueled largely by early family wealth transfers rather than earned wages.
  • Sellers pile up at the top: Active Seoul apartment listings have topped 80,000, led by a 148% jump in central Seongdong District, while luxury sellers in affluent Gangnam are slashing asking prices by up to 4.6 billion won (about $3.3 million) without finding buyers.
  • Auctions cool and regions diverge: Bids at a court auction in Songpa District’s flagship Helio City complex plunged from 87 in past cycles to just 3, while Seoul’s weekly apartment prices edged down 0.06% even as surrounding Gyeonggi Province rose 0.24%.
  • A brutal rental squeeze ahead: Under South Korea’s jeonse system—where tenants pay a massive lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent—new deposits for standard 84-square-meter apartments in outer Nowon District topped 600 million won (about $430,000), leaving 64,000 Seoul households whose 2023 leases expire next year facing an average 36% deposit hike.

Statistical relief, real-world friction

The gulf between official data and street-level reality explains why younger and non-homeowning Koreans met the 11.7-year figure with disbelief. When government metrics understate how fast home prices and mortgage burdens outpace salaries, they erode trust in state statistics without lowering the real barrier to entry. In Seoul today, buying a home on wage income alone has effectively slipped out of reach.

A market splitting from the top down

Beneath the headline figures, Seoul’s prolonged rally is showing classic signs of exhaustion. Buyers are stepping back as inventory builds, forcing steep discounts in Gangnam while outer neighborhoods post a late, uneven catch-up—a sequence that often signals a broader market correction ahead.

Yet a cooling sales market offers little comfort to renters. Hesitant would-be buyers are staying in the rental pool, driving jeonse deposits higher just as rules on jeonse loans tighten. With forecasts pointing to an 8% rise in capital-region jeonse prices next year compared with 3% to 5% growth in sale prices, ordinary households face an acute cash squeeze even if apartment valuations soften.
