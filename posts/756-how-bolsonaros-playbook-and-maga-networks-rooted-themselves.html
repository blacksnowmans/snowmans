How Bolsonaro's Playbook and MAGA Networks Rooted Themselves in South Korea

October 06, 2026

Global right-wing populist movements are mirroring each other across continents, as imported American and Brazilian political playbooks—steeped in evangelical nationalism and manufactured voter fraud narratives—take root in South Korea's streets and religious groups.

    Global Parallels: South Korea's fringe right-wing religious groups are deploying the exact same playbook used by supporters of former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro and the U.S. MAGA movement, weaponizing religion to brand political opponents as absolute evil.
  • Religious Extremism: Figures like South Korean conservative pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon mirror the strategies of Brazilian "political pastor" Silas Malafaia, using pulpits to organize anti-democratic actions and push election denialism.
  • Imported Networks: Events like "BuildUp Korea" and the influence of American networks like CPAC and strategist Steve Bannon have helped transplant foreign culture-war tactics, anti-China sentiment, and xenophobia into local politics.
  • Street-Level Fallout: International polarization has trickled down to South Korean tourist hubs like Myeongdong and Hongdae, where young activists target minority groups under the guise of patriotism.

The Dangerous Merge of Faith and Far-Right Politics

The convergence of American evangelicalism with foreign political movements has proven exceptionally potent at short-circuiting democratic norms. In Brazil, Bolsonaro's rise relied heavily on shifting the country's traditional Catholic landscape toward an aggressive, politicized Protestantism. South Korea has seen a remarkably similar trajectory, where specific religious figures have hijacked political agendas to amplify social divisions, hiding partisan ambition behind the shield of religious freedom.

This cross-border exchange is hardly accidental. Strategic nodes—such as the Conservative Political Action Conference (CPAC) and local forums—serve as conduits for pushing disinformation, notably debunked election fraud claims. When religious authorities convince their followers that a political loss is a cosmic battle against evil, compromise becomes impossible, and democratic institutions are systematically undermined from within.

A Warning for South Korea's Democratic Future

While South Korea has managed to avoid the literal storming of legislative halls seen in Brazil and the U.S., the underlying currents of hatred and anti-intellectualism remain dangerously active. Xenophobic street rhetoric and the weaponization of manufactured outrage are not organic domestic developments; they are the direct products of an imported global manual designed to paralyze public reason.

Defending democracy against these transnational networks requires more than political shifts—it demands a collective refusal to let bigotry and religious demagoguery dictate public life. When outrage replaces policy, everyone loses. The antidote to a globalized wave of hatred is a clear-eyed, civic-minded refusal to be manipulated.
