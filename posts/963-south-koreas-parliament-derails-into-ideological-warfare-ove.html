South Korea's Parliament Derails Into Ideological Warfare Over Foreign Letters and Family Scandals

October 11, 2026

South Korean politics descended into a hyper-polarized free-for-all on October 10 as parliamentary audits collided with a flurry of personal and diplomatic controversies, pushing public fatigue to new heights.

    What happened: The ruling Democratic Party attacked conservative opposition politicians over family privilege allegations and a controversial letter to Ukraine's leader, while the opposition hit back over a U.S. visa delay for a senior ruling party lawmaker. What it changes: The fierce partisan bickering has completely overshadowed ongoing parliamentary audits, driving public approval for the ruling party down to new lows. Who is affected: Everyday citizens watching essential economic and livelihood legislation stall while politicians trade insults.

Partisan Brawl Erupts Over Family Scrutiny and Foreign Policy

The political friction was underscored by a bitter clash between the ruling Democratic Party and the conservative opposition People Power Party. The ruling camp launched a heavy offensive against People Power Party lawmaker Han Dong-hoon, demanding investigations into alleged resume falsification and past police cover-ups involving his children. Han dismissed the accusations as "baseless mudslinging" and accused the ruling party of trying to manufacture political narratives to seize the upper hand.

Foreign policy also became a battleground when conservative lawmaker Na Kyung-won sent a letter of gratitude to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. The Democratic Party slammed the move as reckless freelance diplomacy that undermined the current government's stance, comparing it to historical betrayals. In response, conservatives fired back, branding the administration as overly sympathetic to North Korea.

Falling Approval Ratings and Lost Priorities

Behind the political noise, the ruling Democratic Party is facing a troubling slide in public support. Plummeting approval numbers, rising housing costs, and relentless verbal sparring in parliament have left voters weary. While party insiders have scrambled to defend leadership policies and dismiss criticism, veteran politicians have begun issuing warnings that the party is losing touch with public sentiment.

Ultimately, this non-stop cycle of negative campaigning benefits neither side. As rival camps trade explosive labels like "traitors" and "pro-North Korea" over foreign letters and visa delays, pressing economic and livelihood issues are shoved aside. For a public exhausted by constant political theater, the true casualty is faith in democratic governance itself.
