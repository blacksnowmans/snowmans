Global Chaos: Trump’s Pressure on Ukraine and the Shifting Geopolitical Order

October 12, 2026

The international order is fraying as the U.S. shifts toward an aggressive, "America-first" stance, forcing South Korea to navigate an increasingly volatile landscape of shifting alliances and trade protectionism.

  • U.S.-Ukraine Crisis: President Trump has publicly called for the ouster of Ukrainian President Zelenskyy and threatened to cut intelligence sharing if Ukraine continues targeting Russian oil facilities.
  • Trade Barriers: The EU and China have reached a limited agreement to restrict hybrid vehicle exports, creating new obstacles for South Korean automakers like Hyundai and Kia.
  • Diplomatic Pivot: To mitigate these risks, the administration of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is aggressively pursuing strategic partnerships, including a new Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) with Egypt.

The White House’s Volatile Turn

The U.S. is signaling a radical departure from its traditional role as a guarantor of global stability. By demanding regime change in Kyiv and threatening to abandon support for Ukraine’s defense, the White House is prioritizing narrow political optics over long-standing geopolitical commitments. This behavior is mirrored domestically, where the U.S. Department of Justice has launched antitrust investigations into media outlets critical of the administration, raising alarms about the erosion of democratic norms within the world’s leading superpower.

Furthermore, President Trump’s recent friction with the International Criminal Court (ICC)—triggered by his failure to win the Nobel Peace Prize—demonstrates a disregard for international institutions. When a superpower treats global judicial bodies as tools for personal grievance, it forces allies to reconsider their reliance on U.S.-led frameworks.

South Korea’s Strategic Realignment

For South Korea, the message is clear: the era of relying solely on "romanticized" alliances is over. As protectionism rises in the EU and the U.S. turns inward, the Lee Jae-myung administration is attempting to diversify its economic dependencies. The push for a CEPA with Egypt represents a pragmatic effort to secure new markets and supply chains that are less susceptible to the volatility of U.S.-China-EU trade wars.

Domestically, the government faces heavy criticism from the conservative People Power Party (the main opposition party), which argues that Seoul’s foreign policy is misaligned with U.S. interests. However, the government maintains that its primary duty is to ensure national survival through "realist diplomacy." As Kim Seung-yeon, Chairman of Hanwha Group (a major South Korean conglomerate), recently noted, "optimism has no place" in the current climate. Whether Seoul can successfully navigate these "Great Power" headwinds will depend on its ability to maintain a cold-eyed focus on national interest rather than getting bogged down in ideological debates.
