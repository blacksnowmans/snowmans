South Korea Postpones Cryptocurrency Taxation Until 2029

November 12, 2026

South Korean lawmakers have tentatively agreed to delay the implementation of a capital gains tax on virtual assets by another two years, pushing the start date to January 2029.

  • The tax subcommittee of the Strategy and Finance Committee reached a bipartisan consensus on November 11 to adopt the government's proposal for a further delay.
  • This marks the fourth deferral of the tax since its first proposed start date in 2022, highlighting the government’s struggle to balance fiscal principles with political popularity.
  • While the timeline was moved, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s ruling party, and the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, failed to reach an agreement on technical specifics like loss carryover deductions.
  • The measure is expected to pass the final vote in the National Assembly, South Korea's unicameral legislature, as part of the upcoming year-end budget deliberations.

The Politics of Investor Approval

Korean politics is currently focused on courting the "2030 generation"—voters in their 20s and 30s who form the backbone of the domestic retail crypto market. For the Lee Jae-myung administration and the DPK, enforcing a 20% tax on crypto gains now carries significant electoral risk. By siding with the PPP to delay the tax again, the government has chosen short-term political stability over the immediate collection of tax revenue. Both parties effectively admitted that the backlash from millions of retail investors is a weight they are not yet willing to carry.

The delay also acknowledges a harsh technical reality: South Korea’s tax infrastructure is not yet ready. While domestic exchanges have improved their reporting, tracking assets moved to overseas platforms or decentralized "cold" wallets remains a massive hurdle for the National Tax Service. Lawmakers argued that taxing domestic trades while being unable to track offshore profits would unfairly penalize compliant investors, potentially driving even more capital out of the country.

A Pattern of Policy Uncertainty

The most contentious unresolved issue remains the "loss carryover deduction." This mechanism would allow investors to subtract previous years' losses from their current taxable gains—a standard practice in the stock market. Without it, an investor could be forced to pay heavy taxes on a single profitable year even if they are still "in the red" overall. Because the DPK and PPP could not agree on how many years of losses to recognize or how to verify them, the debate has simply been postponed along with the tax itself.

By kicking the can down the road for a fourth time, the National Assembly has weakened the credibility of the country’s tax code. Each delay reinforces the perception that tax deadlines are negotiable if enough public pressure is applied. For a market that thrives on predictability, this "stop-and-go" legislation creates a different kind of risk. The next two years will be a test for the Lee Jae-myung government to see if it can finally build the legal framework—including a comprehensive "Basic Act on Virtual Assets"—to move beyond these temporary reprieves and establish a permanent, fair system.

Key Takeaway: While crypto investors have won a temporary reprieve, the lack of agreement on loss deductions means the fundamental "fairness" of the future tax system remains in limbo.
