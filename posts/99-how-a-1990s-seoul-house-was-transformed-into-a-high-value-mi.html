How a 1990s Seoul House Was Transformed into a High-Value Mixed-Use Property

September 27, 2026

South Korea’s booming real estate market has put older properties under the spotlight, forcing owners to choose between expensive demolition or smart remodeling. A recent project in Seoul led by building remodeler Shin Bit-na demonstrates how a 120-pyeong (about 4,000 sq ft), 1990s detached home can be completely overhauled into a modern residential and commercial asset.

  • The Property: A rundown single-family home built in the 1990s, featuring heavy red brick walls and chronic basement dampness.
  • The Strategy: Removing exterior barriers for parking, installing heavy-duty waterproofing, and reconfiguring layouts to allow the owner to live and work in the same building without crossing paths.
  • The Budget: Approximately 400 million won (about $300,000) for a total structural and interior upgrade.
  • The Impact: Turns a liability-prone old house into a high-yield, functional asset by navigating strict South Korean building codes and parking regulations.

Overcoming Structural Nightmares: Parking and Flooding

One of the biggest hurdles in remodeling older South Korean homes is complying with strict municipal parking laws and the country's notorious basement flood risks. In this project, high red-brick walls that choked the narrow alleyway were completely demolished, turning what was once dead space into a clean, reinforced concrete parking area with a sleek, polished finish. This single move instantly boosted the property's curb appeal and asset value.

Meanwhile, the basement presented a classic headache: sitting roughly 1.2 meters below road level, it was a magnet for monsoon rains and mold. Rather than applying superficial paint, the project team installed a dedicated water barrier, carved precise drainage channels linked to a relocated sump pit, and added advanced ventilation. By clearing out the bulk of the old gravel sub-floor and upgrading insulation, they also shaved down floor thickness to reclaim precious ceiling height—proving that technical execution matters far more than cosmetic touch-ups.

Engineering Privacy and Maximizing Value

The true genius of the renovation lies in its spatial choreography. Multi-family and mixed-use buildings in dense urban areas often suffer from friction between residents and commercial tenants. By designing dual arched front entrances and distinct internal corridors, the project achieved a triple-split traffic flow. The owner can access their upper-floor residence, walk into a newly converted commercial office, or access the basement rental unit without ever crossing paths with anyone else.

Ultimately, this project serves as a masterclass for real estate investors eyeing South Korea's aging urban housing stock. Blindly slapping fresh wallpaper on a decaying building invites hefty municipal fines and structural failure. But when owners invest in foundational fixes—like professional waterproofing, smart zoning, and clever storage solutions—even a dated 1990s concrete box can be reborn as a highly competitive, income-generating asset.
