South Korea's Kospi Crash Sparks 'Nokia Risk' Warnings and Hits President Lee's Ratings

October 18, 2026

A severe plunge in South Korea's benchmark Kospi index—tumbling below the 5,500 mark from an earlier high above 8,100—has triggered a collapse in President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings and laid bare the structural vulnerabilities of an economy overly reliant on memory semiconductors.

  • The crash: The Kospi fell 36% in the period following the June 2026 local elections, even as Taiwan's tech-heavy benchmark rose 3.4% under identical global macroeconomic conditions.
  • The structural flaw: While financial regulators blamed external shocks—such as U.S. Federal Reserve rate projections, artificial intelligence market jitters, and rising oil prices—critics warn of a "Korean Nokia risk," where national economic health is tied too tightly to cyclical memory chip earnings.
  • The domestic fallout: Retail investors—known locally as "ants"—have borne the brunt of the downturn as foreign funds exited with profits, leaving household balance sheets damaged and domestic consumer sentiment frozen.
  • The political toll: Economic distress and the stock market collapse rose to the second-highest grievance in public opinion polling, dragging President Lee's approval rating from near 60% down into the 30% range.

A Single-Engine Economy Meets Global Headwinds

South Korea's current market shock bears uncomfortable similarities to Finland’s historic overreliance on Nokia. For years, strong memory chip cycles masked deeper industrial weaknesses, giving policymakers an illusion of broad economic resilience. Unlike Taiwan, which insulated its economy through TSMC and a diversified, irreplaceable foundry ecosystem, South Korea remains dangerously exposed to cyclical swings in memory prices. When demand softened, the lack of alternatives left the wider market defenseless.

Market observers have directed much of their ire at the regulatory response. The Financial Services Commission (FSC), South Korea's top financial regulator, repeatedly pointed to external factors during emergency market reviews. Yet blaming external winds rings hollow when domestic structural defenses were neglected. By relying on temporary stopgaps rather than overhauling capital market rules and pushing industrial diversification, policymakers missed the window for structural reform.

Grassroots Betrayal and Reform Gridlock

The market downturn has quickly evolved into a legitimacy crisis for President Lee and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Lee took office carrying immense expectations following the governance crisis and subsequent impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving a prison sentence. Lee built his political brand on the populist pledge of eokgang-buyak—curbing the excessive influence of the powerful to protect the weak. Yet retail investors who backed that platform now complain the administration offered mere complacency, brushing off early slides as a "temporary search for equilibrium."

The fallout has alienated key intellectual allies and grassroots supporters. Prominent progressive economists who once shaped the administration's platform, including Professor Choi Bae-geun, have voiced public anguish over the government's trajectory. Meanwhile, the administration's defensive stance toward internal critics has deepened disillusionment. For millions of retail investors watching their savings shrink, empty self-praise from economic officials has only accelerated a bitter collapse in public trust.
