Ruling by Fear Versus Governing by Trust: A Lesson for South Korea’s Leadership

October 22, 2026

Governing through legalistic intimidation and fear inevitably erodes a state's legitimacy into public ridicule and cynicism, leaving the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung facing the critical task of rebuilding authentic emotional trust with the electorate.

  • The cautionary collapse of coercion: The tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol demonstrated how heavy-handed rule—relying on prosecutorial authority and extraordinary decrees—backfires, culminating in impeachment, imprisonment, and complete public alienation.
  • The Aristotelian alternative: Classical political philosophy argues that durable authority rests not on Machiavellian fear, but on a "politics of friendship" defined by mutual goodwill, wishing the other well, and explicit reciprocal recognition.
  • The emotional landscape of power: When leaders slide from the protective "valley of goodwill" onto the exposed "ridge of parting," even minor policy errors trigger catastrophic domestic backlash.
  • Strategic dilemma for the ruling camp: President Lee and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea must avoid hollow triangulation between rival voting blocs and instead rebuild foundational trust with their core base.

The Perilous Slide from Fear to Cynicism

A sovereign's authority cannot survive purely on institutional violence and punitive legal machinery. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, the state leaned heavily on prosecutorial instruments to enforce compliance. Rather than inspiring deference, this strategy triggered public satire and internet mockery. Once a population stops fearing state coercion, it strips away the regime's solemnity through ridicule.

Even more fatal than satire is cold cynicism. Satire still presumes that power might respond or reform; cynicism severs all emotional connection between citizens and the state. When the public views every official initiative merely as a self-serving elite power grab, governance grinds to a halt. The dramatic downfall and detention of Mr. Yoon highlight how quickly fear-based dominance self-destructs once civic goodwill evaporates.

Navigating the 'Ridge of Parting' Under Lee Jae-myung

This dynamic presents an urgent structural test for South Korea's current leader, President Lee Jae-myung. In political analysis, public support can be mapped as a topological shift: in the deep "valley of affection," citizens forgive tactical errors out of shared affinity; on the precarious "ridge of parting," public confidence flattens, and the slightest misstep sends a presidency tumbling downward.

The trap for the Lee administration lies in attempting artificial mediation between hardened core loyalists and volatile swing voters. Ambiguous compromises frequently leave core supporters feeling betrayed while failing to win over skeptical outsiders. Rather than acting as a detached arbiter trying to please incompatible factions, a ruling administration must cultivate explicit, verifiable goodwill with its foundational base. Sustainable democracy requires moving past the instinct to control, anchoring leadership instead in shared civic respect.
