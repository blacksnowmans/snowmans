Xi Jinping’s Diplomatic Pivot: Redefining China’s Global Reach

November 03, 2026

Chinese leader Xi Jinping is aggressively reshaping China's foreign policy by cementing influence in the Middle East while simultaneously insulating his regime from Western pressure over the war in Ukraine.

  • Deepening Middle East Ties: Beijing hosted the Sultan of Oman for the first time in 48 years of diplomatic relations, signaling a major push to secure energy interests and challenge U.S. regional dominance.
  • Cold Shoulder to Europe: In meetings with German leadership, Xi explicitly rejected requests to engage in peace negotiations for the Russia-Ukraine war, prioritizing national interest over Western expectations.
  • Tightening Internal Control: Xi has doubled down on the necessity of Communist Party oversight, dismissing international criticism that party interference stifles economic growth.

The Pivot Toward the Middle East

The recent state visit by the Sultan of Oman marks a strategic turning point. By positioning China as a "peace mediator" in the Middle East—including the dispatch of special envoys to manage Iran-U.S. tensions—Beijing is attempting to fill the vacuum left by shifting U.S. priorities. This is not mere diplomacy; it is a calculated effort to secure energy and logistics infrastructure under the Belt and Road Initiative, China’s massive global infrastructure development strategy. By framing itself as a neutral alternative to Western military intervention, China is actively building a coalition of "Global South" partners to counter American influence.

Defiance of Western Norms

The contrast in Xi’s diplomatic tone is stark. While he courts Middle Eastern energy powers, he remains dismissive of European efforts to involve China in ending the conflict in Ukraine. During recent summits, Beijing made it clear it has no interest in mediating a conflict that would force it to take sides against Russia or alienate Washington. This refusal serves a dual purpose: it avoids direct entanglement in the European theater while reinforcing the narrative that China will not be dictated to by Western powers.

This approach is underpinned by a rigid commitment to state-led economics. Despite international warnings that excessive party control is hampering China's recovery, the leadership has reasserted that the Communist Party’s dominance is the bedrock of its power. For global investors, this signals that China is willing to trade economic openness for internal political stability, a move that likely points to a more unpredictable and isolated Chinese market in the coming years.

For South Korea, under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, these developments present a complex challenge. As the U.S.-China rivalry intensifies and expands into the energy-rich Middle East, Seoul must navigate a narrow path. The era of the previous administration—characterized by the now-imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol—saw a more polarized approach. The current government faces the urgent task of rebuilding diplomatic autonomy and negotiation leverage, ensuring that South Korea’s export-driven economy is not caught in the crossfire of this expanding global power struggle.
