The "Cartel" Within: Unpacking Allegations Against Han Dong-hoon and the Prosecution

October 09, 2026

  • The Core Issue: Allegations of institutionalized corruption and "self-protection" within South Korea’s powerful prosecution service have reignited demands for sweeping judicial reform.
  • What Happened: Recent reports highlight a history of systemic misconduct, including the cover-up of sexual assault by a former elite prosecutor related to Han Dong-hoon, and the questionable indictment of a prosecutor who once attempted to search Han's phone.
  • The Impact: The revelations have fueled public outrage, as they paint a picture of a "legal nobility" that operates above the law while suppressing internal dissent.
  • Political Context: With the administration of President Lee Jae-myung seeking to establish a new anti-corruption agency (the Major Crimes Investigation Agency), critics are questioning whether the new government is merely recycling old-school, establishment-friendly figures rather than driving genuine reform.

A History of "Self-Protection"

For years, the South Korean prosecution has been accused of acting as a closed cartel. A prime example is the case of Jeong Jin-woong, a prosecutor who was indicted for "assault" while attempting to execute a search warrant on the phone of Han Dong-hoon, a former high-ranking official. Despite Jeong’s efforts to prevent the potential destruction of evidence—a standard part of his duty—he was prosecuted by his own colleagues. The Supreme Court eventually acquitted Jeong, confirming his actions were a legitimate exercise of his official duties. This case remains a bitter reminder of how the prosecution system has historically been weaponized to protect its own elite members.

The rot appears to go deeper than mere procedural bias. Further scrutiny has fallen on the family connections surrounding Han Dong-hoon. In 2015, a prosecutor named Jin Dong-kyun—who is Han's brother-in-law—was accused of serial sexual assault against junior female colleagues. Instead of facing criminal charges, the case was hushed up, and he was allowed to quietly resign, only to land a high-paying executive role at a major conglomerate. When the case finally reached a court of law years later, Jin was sentenced to prison, yet he shockingly attempted to challenge the constitutionality of sexual assault laws, claiming his actions were mere "accidental touches."

Reform Under Fire

The public anger surrounding these disclosures is exacerbated by the current political transition. As the Lee Jae-myung administration pushes to expand the investigative powers of the new Major Crimes Investigation Agency, critics are sounding the alarm. The appointment of figures perceived as "opportunists" or holdovers from the old guard to lead these new institutions suggests that, despite a change in government, the structural culture of the prosecution remains largely intact.

Ultimately, the controversy is not just about individual crimes, but about the "prosecutorial privilege" that allows the powerful to dodge accountability. For the current government, the challenge is clear: if they fail to dismantle this deeply ingrained cartel and continue to appoint figures who mirror the biases of the past, they risk losing the very public support that brought them to power. The expectation for a transparent and fair legal system remains the single most important litmus test for the success of this administration.
