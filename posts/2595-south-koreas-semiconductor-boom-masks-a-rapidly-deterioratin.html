South Korea’s Semiconductor Boom Masks a Rapidly Deteriorating Domestic Economy

November 21, 2026

A staggering 259.4% surge in semiconductor exports driven by global artificial intelligence demand is masking severe distress in South Korea's domestic economy under President Lee Jae-myung.

  • Export illusion: Microchip exports skyrocketed 259.4% year-on-year, generating windfall profits for tech giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix without boosting broader employment.
  • Collapsing job growth: Monthly job additions dropped below 100,000 under the current Lee administration, compared to 280,000 under the former Moon Jae-in administration.
  • Shrinking paychecks: Average real wages fell slightly compared to Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, while monthly wages for temporary and daily workers dropped by nearly 150,000 won (about $110).
  • Youth job crisis: Youth employment (ages 15–34) is shrinking by 113,000 jobs per month, pushing household real spending back to 2017 levels.

The Limits of 'Trickle-Down' Microchip Growth

Prominent economists like Professor Choi Bae-geun point out that the Lee Jae-myung administration is relying heavily on headline trade figures to claim economic success. However, the surge in microchip exports is largely an external windfall created by massive AI infrastructure investments from U.S. tech firms, rather than effective domestic policy. Because IT manufacturing employs just 1.9% of South Korea's workforce—compared to 10.6% in Taiwan—record chip profits do not translate into broad-based income growth or job creation for average citizens.

Instead of addressing structural inequality, the administration’s reliance on top-line export numbers has allowed the domestic market to deteriorate. Small business debt defaults are surging, and household debt has reached 171.14% of disposable income—the seventh highest in the OECD—severely choking consumer demand across local communities.

Worsening Inequality and Disappearing Jobs

The income gap between major corporations and everyday workers continues to widen. The ratio of non-regular to regular worker wages has dropped from 54.0% during Yoon Suk-yeol's term to 52.1% today, while small and medium-sized enterprise (SME) wages relative to major conglomerates slipped from 62.9% to 60.8%. Over 14 million people—including 8.67 million non-regular workers and 5.8 million self-employed business owners—face increasingly fragile living standards.

With monthly permanent job creation dropping to 135,000 from 323,500 during Yoon Suk-yeol's term, and SME debt defaults rising above 11 trillion won (about $8.2 billion), South Korea's domestic foundation is buckling under debt and stagnant growth. Without targeted income redistribution and debt relief, the country risks lingering in a deep domestic slump while relying entirely on a single export sector.
