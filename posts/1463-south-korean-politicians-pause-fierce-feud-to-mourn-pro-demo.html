South Korean Politicians Pause Fierce Feud to Mourn Pro-Democracy Icon Choi Hyung-woo

October 26, 2026

The death of former Minister of Home Affairs Choi Hyung-woo, a giant of South Korea’s pro-democracy movement, has brought a rare, temporary truce to the country's deeply divided political landscape.

  • What happened: Choi Hyung-woo, widely remembered as the "right-hand man" to former President Kim Young-sam, passed away on October 25 at the age of 91.
  • Bipartisan unity: In an extraordinary gesture, Kim Min-seok, a senior leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, and Jang Dong-hyeok of the main opposition People Power Party agreed to serve as co-chairs of the funeral committee.
  • Historical legacy: Choi was a central figure in resisting South Korea's military dictatorships during the 1970s and 1980s, helping launch the Council for the Promotion of Democracy and driving the 1987 June Democratic Struggle that established direct presidential elections.
  • Final rites: After days of joint mourning attended by political figures across the ideological spectrum, Choi will be buried at the April 19th National Cemetery on October 27.

A Momentary Silence in a Bitter Political War

Choi’s passing arrived during an era of intense hostility in South Korean politics. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party remain locked in daily battles over parliamentary audits and judicial investigations—a polarization lingering from the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Yet, for a brief moment, politicians from both camps set aside their political daggers to pay their respects at Choi's memorial altar.

Seeing party leaders who routinely exchange harsh rhetoric stand shoulder-to-shoulder in mourning clothes offered a striking image. Conservative figures, including Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, joined progressive leaders in acknowledging that Choi’s personal sacrifices under authoritarian regimes laid the foundation for modern Korean democracy.

A Warning from Political Elders

Beyond honoring Choi, the funeral became an arena for reflection among political veterans. Many elders visiting the memorial voiced frustration over the current state of South Korea's National Assembly, lamenting that ruling and opposition parties now view one another as "mortal enemies." They recalled how, during the pro-democracy struggle, opposing factions fought bitterly over principles but still maintained channels of personal dialogue and compromise.

While the decision to co-chair Choi’s funeral demonstrates that historical reverence endures, the question remains whether this brief civility will last. Paying true honor to Choi’s legacy will require more than symbolic unity at a memorial—it demands restoring functional parliamentary dialogue once lawmakers return to the National Assembly.
