How a Young Couple Beat Seoul's Housing Crunch with a 40-Year-Old Villa

September 02, 2026

  • The Deal: A couple in Seoul bypassed expensive modern apartments to buy a 40-year-old, brick multi-family villa for 150 million won (about $110,000) and spent 50 million won (about $37,000) on renovations, total-ing just 200 million won (about $147,000).
  • The Renovation: Despite warnings from real estate commentators to avoid old brick buildings with poor resale value, the couple gutted the interior, removing a water tank to add storage and pushing the ceiling height up to 3.8 meters (12.5 feet).
  • The Shift: The project highlights a growing debate in South Korea over whether housing should be judged purely as a liquid financial asset or as a space for personal well-being.

Challenging the Apartment Cult

In South Korea, brand-new high-rise apartments are often treated as the default gold standard for housing and status. Real estate commentators frequently advise buyers to steer clear of older brick multi-family homes, warning of low liquidity and high depreciation. But this couple's project challenges the obsession with newly built complexes.

By stripping away standard low ceilings to reveal an airy 3.8-meter vertical space, the couple turned a supposedly flawed relic into an expansive, custom home. Their choice argues that architectural character and ceiling height can trump the uniform layouts of modern developments, proving that a modest budget can yield a deeply personalized living environment when decoupled from market-driven expectations.

Redefining Housing Value

The prevailing Korean housing market pushes buyers toward fierce competition for new apartment pre-sales, often locking younger generations into heavy mortgage debt for standardized cookie-cutter units. This renovation highlights a different path: prioritizing daily quality of life over future resale value.

While skeptics might view an old, customized villa as a risky financial move, the project reframes a tight budget not as a limitation, but as creative freedom. It suggests that true luxury in a hyper-dense city like Seoul isn't a corporate brand logo on a massive apartment complex gate—it is the autonomy to shape one's own living space.
