Inside the Cult-Smear Scandal Shaking South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 06, 2026

Representative Kim Min-seok of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is facing severe backlash from party loyalists after issuing a non-apology for spreading unverified claims that political rivals were allied with Shincheonji, a controversial fringe religious group.

  • What happened: During internal election feuds, Rep. Kim Min-seok amplified ungrounded rumors alleging that supporters of rival candidate Jung Chung-rae and local members under Rep. Kwon Hyeong-yeop were part of a secret pact with the Shincheonji cult.
  • What the data shows: Internal party reviews and joint investigative probes found no evidence of a secret factional pact with Shincheonji, leaving thousands of grassroots party loyalists falsely branded as cult infiltrators.
  • What it changes: Kim’s ambiguous public statement—saying he felt "both sorry and thankful"—has fueled fury among rank-and-file members who view it as evasive rhetoric designed to dodge official discipline.
  • Who is affected: Long-time DPK members whose reputations were tarnished, alongside party leadership now accused of sweeping senior-level misconduct under the rug in the name of party unity.

Factional Mudslinging and the Human Cost

The weaponization of religious cult rumors is a toxic maneuver in South Korean politics. Shincheonji, a secretive religious movement frequently embroiled in political controversies, was used as a wedge issue during internal DPK rivalries. Smear campaigns targeted the constituency of DPK lawmaker Kwon Hyeong-yeop in Suncheon, spreading false claims that 6,000 Shincheonji members had surreptitiously joined the party to sway internal votes. While internal estimates suggest 30,000 to 50,000 people with cult ties exist among the DPK’s 1.55 million total members, investigators found zero evidence that candidate Jung Chung-rae or local chapters organized a covert alliance with the group.

The collateral damage has fallen heavily on grassroots party loyalists. Grassroots organizers—including four-decade party veterans in their eighties—were suddenly denounced online as cultists and political traitors. Critics note that Kim's smear tactics mirror past factional mudslinging from his student activist days in the 1980s and his controversial political maneuvers during the 2002 presidential election. Rather than taking full responsibility, Kim offered a convoluted television statement expressing a mix of gratitude and apology, leaving victims without formal exoneration.

Leadership Inaction and the Challenge to Party Morality

Rather than enforcing strict accountability through the party’s ethics committee, DPK leadership—including floor leader Park Chan-dae and senior lawmakers like Kim Young-bae and Han Jeoung-ae—moved to defuse the crisis at general assemblies. Senior figures urged lawmakers to move past the feud and focus on broader institutional reforms regarding religious interference, effectively preempting formal inquiries into Kim’s actions.

This elite-led compromise has deepened resentment among the rank-and-file. By allowing high-ranking politicians to broadcast false allegations and retreat behind empty rhetoric, the DPK risks undermining its own moral authority and commitment to party democracy. For South Korea's ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, brushing aside internal character assassination sends a troubling signal that factional expediency matters more than truth and accountability.
