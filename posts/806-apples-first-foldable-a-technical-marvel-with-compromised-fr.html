Apple’s First Foldable: A Technical Marvel with Compromised Freedom

September 18, 2026

Apple’s entry into the foldable market, the iPhone Duo, has arrived with a premium price tag of about $2,400 (3.29 million KRW) and a bold claim from CEO John Ternus that it is the "most innovative change since the original iPhone." While the device showcases impressive hardware engineering, early reviews suggest that Apple’s rigid design philosophy may be hindering the very user experience it claims to perfect.

  • Hardware vs. Software: The Duo uses a unique "D-shaped" asymmetric layout that forces system icons to the right side, prioritizing a clean aesthetic over accessibility for left-handed users.
  • Restricted Multitasking: Despite the large 7.6-inch screen, Apple limits the dock to four icons and forces a 50:50 split-screen view, limiting the multitasking freedom found in rival devices like Samsung’s Galaxy Z Fold series.
  • Underwhelming Pen Support: After years of opposing styluses, Apple has added support for the basic USB-C Pencil, but it lacks pressure sensitivity, haptics, and other pro features, with software support delayed until later this year.
  • Premium Price, Mid-Tier Camera: To achieve its 5.2mm thickness, the Duo sacrifices advanced camera features like telephoto zoom, a baffling trade-off for a device priced significantly higher than the flagship iPhone 18 Pro.

The Price of Perfectionism

The iPhone Duo represents a classic Apple paradox: exquisite build quality crippled by "walled garden" constraints. By forcing the dock and navigation to the right side to maintain a perfect rectangular content area, Apple has inadvertently created a barrier for left-handed users. While this maintains the brand's signature aesthetic symmetry, it feels like a triumph of form over function. When a $2,400 device prevents you from placing a fifth app in your dock simply because it deviates from Apple’s long-standing "four-icon" tradition, the software feels like a cage rather than an ecosystem.

Hardware Innovation vs. Practical Utility

The device’s physical design, featuring a nano-texture screen and an incredibly subtle crease, proves that Apple’s engineering team is at the top of its game. Yet, these technical achievements are undercut by strange omissions. The decision to make the Duo an eSIM-only device has sparked local frustration in South Korea, where mobile carriers charge fees for each eSIM activation—an unnecessary friction point for a "pro" device. Furthermore, the lack of a high-end stylus experience or a competitive camera module suggests that in the rush to make the phone thin and foldable, Apple neglected the practical utility power users actually demand.

Ultimately, the iPhone Duo is a beautiful, expensive piece of hardware that asks users to settle for less control in exchange for "Apple-approved" simplicity. Whether the market accepts these limitations as the "new standard" or rebels against the lack of professional-grade freedom remains to be seen.
