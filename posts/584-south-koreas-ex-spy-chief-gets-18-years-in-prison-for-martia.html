South Korea's Ex-Spy Chief Gets 18 Years in Prison for Martial Law Plot

October 02, 2026

  • A South Korean court has sentenced former Defense Security Commander Yeo In-hyung to 18 years in prison for his role in the abortive Dec. 3 martial law attempt.
    •
  • Other top military brass received heavy sentences ranging from 9 to 15 years, falling short of the special counsel's demands for up to 30 years.
  • During the trial, defendants blamed staff misunderstandings and denied orders to arrest lawmakers, drawing widespread public criticism.
  • The rulings highlight the judiciary's reckoning with military leaders who abandoned political neutrality to serve authoritarian political ambitions.

South Korean courts have delivered a stark message to the military figures behind the chaotic Dec. 3 martial law attempt, sentencing former Defense Security Commander Yeo In-hyung to 18 years behind bars. Yeo, who directed key security apparatuses during the short-lived emergency decree, was convicted of playing a central role in subverting constitutional order. Yet, rather than accepting responsibility, the defendants spent their day in court hiding behind far-fetched excuses, blaming subordinates for what they characterized as mere operational misunderstandings.

The Seoul Central District Court also handed down sentences to other high-ranking officials involved in the conspiracy: former Capital Defense Commander Lee Jin-woo received 15 years, former Army Chief of Staff Park An-su got 10 years, former Special Warfare Commander Kwak Jong-keun received 9 years, and former Intelligence Commander Moon Sang-ho was sentenced to 12 years. Prosecutors had originally sought up to 30 years for the ringleaders. While Kwak received a relatively lighter sentence due to early cooperation and admissions, leaders like Yeo continued to offer implausible denials—even claiming that orders to track and detain major politicians were just routine investigative work.

Excuses and Evasion in the Courtroom

The trial exposed a deeply troubling pattern of military commanders attempting to wash their hands of a failed coup. Yeo claimed in court that he never explicitly ordered the arrest of lawmakers, attributing the mobilization of troops and tracking of politicians to a communications snafu with the police. This version of events directly contradicts testimony from subordinate commanders who detailed direct orders to prep detention facilities and execute raids. When confronted with evidence of targeting opposition politicians like Democratic Party leader Lee Jae-myung and ruling party figures, the defendants fell back on transparent semantics, arguing that they were simply moving too cautiously to enforce the martial law decree before it was voted down.

This post-hoc rationalization insults the public memory of a night when elite military units were weaponized against the nation's democratic institutions. The spectacle of generals invoking religious texts and shifting blame onto junior officers underscores a complete failure of leadership. South Korea's armed forces rely entirely on public trust in their constitutional duty; when top commanders treat national defense assets as personal private armies for political patrons, the resulting breach of trust runs deep. As these cases move to appeal, the public demand for unsparing accountability remains absolute.
