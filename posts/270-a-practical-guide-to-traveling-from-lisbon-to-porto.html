A Practical Guide to Traveling from Lisbon to Porto

September 07, 2026

Traveling between Portugal’s two largest cities is best approached not as a high-speed commute, but as a slow-paced immersion into the country’s history and local rhythm.

  • The Experience: While the Intercidades (IC) train service is older and slower than modern high-speed alternatives, it offers an authentic view of the Portuguese landscape.
  • Navigating Transit: Understanding the "Andante" card system—the local public transit pass—can save travelers from unnecessary fees and confusion.
  • Strategic Stays: Choosing an apartment outside the immediate city center often provides better value and a more genuine look at local life.
  • The Cost of Tourism: Porto is grappling with "overtourism," making it essential for travelers to respect local housing and neighborhood dynamics.

Embracing the Pace of Portuguese Rail

The journey from Lisbon’s Santa Apolónia Station to Porto on the IC 621 train takes roughly three hours and 13 minutes. While the rolling stock is aging—reminiscent of South Korea’s older Mugunghwa-ho trains—the experience highlights the "flow" of travel rather than the efficiency of speed. For travelers, the key is to manage expectations; treating the train's minor inconveniences as part of the historical experience shifts the mindset from frustration to observation.

Station food is often unfairly maligned as overpriced, but it remains one of the best ways to get a quick, accurate taste of a city. At Santa Apolónia, a light meal of an egg tart, apple pie, and coffee costs about $8.50 (7.70 EUR). It is an ideal way to bridge the gap between departure and arrival.

Mastering Porto’s Transit and Culture

Porto’s public transport revolves around the Andante card. The biggest mistake for visitors is failing to understand the zonal (Z) system, which can lead to paying double for transfers. A Z2 ticket, for example, allows for transfers within one hour. Smart travelers calculate whether a 3-day tourist pass (about $18) is truly cost-effective for their specific itinerary before purchasing.

Beyond logistics, Porto’s transit is an architectural experience. São Bento Station, famous for its azulejo (traditional blue and white ceramic tile) murals, serves as a starting point that feels more like an art gallery than a transit hub. The city’s infrastructure, particularly the Dom Luís I Bridge, is a testament to 19th-century engineering that remains the city's vital artery today.

As you explore, remember that Porto is currently facing significant housing pressures due to rising tourism. Choosing to stay in local apartments away from tourist-clogged centers and respecting the neighborhoods you visit is key to sustainable travel. Whether you are watching the sunset at Jardim do Morro or marveling at the 16,000 tiles of the Capela das Almas, the goal is to observe the city’s pulse, not just to consume it as a photo opportunity.
