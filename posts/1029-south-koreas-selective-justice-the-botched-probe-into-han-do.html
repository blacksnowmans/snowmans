South Korea's Selective Justice: The Botched Probe into Han Dong-hoon's Family

October 12, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean police closed an 20-month investigation into academic resume padding allegations against the daughter of Han Dong-hoon—a lawmaker with the conservative People Power Party and former justice minister—without a single search warrant or witness interview.
  • The double standard: By sharp contrast, police executed 131 search warrants during a six-month investigation into Kim Hye-kyung, wife of current liberal President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Institutional fallout: Lawmakers grilled police chiefs at a parliamentary audit over the inactive probe, foreign evidence requests that were quietly dropped, and false briefings given to the press by senior law enforcement.
  • Who is affected: Public trust in South Korea's rule of law and investigative agencies, which face growing accusations of bowing to political power rather than applying equal justice.

An Investigation Carried Out in Whispers

In South Korea, aggressive evidence collection is the baseline for high-profile political investigations. Yet for nearly two years, allegations that Han Dong-hoon's daughter used fabricated extracurriculars and ghostwritten essays for college applications sat untouched in a police drawer. While accusers were summoned six times for questioning, the target of the investigation was given the courtesy of a single written questionnaire. Zero search warrants were issued across 20 months.

When pressed at a National Assembly audit, the acting National Police Agency chief admitted the handling was unusual. Compounding the damage, the National Office of Investigation head falsely claimed to reporters that numerous witnesses had been interviewed, when the actual number was zero. For critics, this wasn't merely incompetence—it looked like a coordinated cover-up designed to protect a powerful political figure from scrutiny.

When the Rule of Law Bends for the Powerful

The core frustration among ordinary citizens is not just the handling of one politician's family, but the glaring asymmetry in how state power is applied. South Koreans are acutely sensitive to systemic double standards. When an investigation into a political opponent can trigger over a hundred search warrants while an inquiry involving a conservative heavyweight results in absolute inaction, the word "fairness" loses all meaning.

Lawmakers have demanded an internal audit and a full reinvestigation. Without a thorough reckoning—and a willingness to apply the law equally regardless of political affiliation—South Korea's investigative agencies will continue to hemorrhage public trust. Justice is supposed to be blind, but recent history suggests it frequently peeks to see who is standing in front of the scales.
