MP Lee Jun-seok’s DIY Anti-Cheating App Challenges South Korea’s Education Bureaucracy

November 18, 2026

Lee Jun-seok, a prominent lawmaker from the New Reform Party, has personally coded a mobile application designed to detect AI-powered cheating devices during the upcoming Suneung, South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam.

  • Lee, a Harvard-educated MP from the centrist New Reform Party, developed the software during a flight to London to address growing fears that current security measures cannot detect miniature AI earpieces.
  • The app scans for Bluetooth signals in real-time, offering a low-cost way for exam proctors to identify unauthorized transmissions using their existing smartphones.
  • The initiative follows a controversial admission by the Education Minister, who suggested that ministry officials were "too old" to catch modern AI-driven cheating tools.
  • While the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, is pushing for legislation to impose five-year prison sentences on cheaters, Lee argues that technological prevention is more effective than post-exam punishment.

Bureaucratic Inertia vs. Digital Agility

The Ministry of Education’s response to the threat of AI cheating has been widely criticized as defeatist. By admitting that proctors are essentially too old to understand the gadgets being used to cheat, the ministry has signaled a massive vulnerability in the Suneung. This exam is the ultimate arbiter of social mobility in Korea, and any hint of unfairness can cause national outrage. Lee Jun-seok’s decision to code the solution himself is a calculated political move to highlight incompetence within the Lee Jae-myung administration’s education wing, shifting the narrative from abstract policy debate to practical, technical intervention.

The technical logic behind the app is sound. While some had suggested using thermal cameras to find heat-emitting earpieces, the New Reform Party concluded that body heat would cause too many false positives. Bluetooth signal detection is a more precise "digital fingerprint." By providing the code openly, Lee is forcing the government to choose: either adopt a functional tool created by a political rival or continue to rely on manual checks that they have already admitted are inadequate against 21st-century technology.

A Regulatory Deadlock

Despite the app's potential, the Ministry remains hesitant to deploy it, citing administrative regulations that strictly prohibit exam supervisors from carrying smartphones during the test. This highlights a recurring theme in Korean governance where rigid rules often lag years behind technological reality. While Chun Ha-ram, another member of the New Reform Party, went as far as suggesting the exam be delayed until security is guaranteed, the proposal was dismissed by the ruling Democratic Party as an unnecessary cause for public anxiety.

The People Power Party has focused its efforts on "deterrence through fear," proposing heavy criminal penalties for those caught using AI to cheat. However, as Lee’s move demonstrates, harsh laws are useless if the state lacks the technical capacity to catch the offender in the first place. If the government refuses to validate or adopt these technological safeguards, it risks a total collapse of public trust in the country's most important academic institution. In an era where technology moves faster than policy, the "airplane coding" incident may be remembered as the moment when the limits of traditional Korean bureaucracy were finally laid bare.
