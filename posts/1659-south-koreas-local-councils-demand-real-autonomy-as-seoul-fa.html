South Korea’s Local Councils Demand Real Autonomy as Seoul Fast-Tracks Regional Megaprojects

October 29, 2026

South Korea's local legislatures are staging a coordinated nationwide push to secure fiscal and personnel independence from the central government, even as the Lee Jae-myung administration moves to accelerate long-delayed regional development initiatives by over a decade.

  • Push for a Local Council Act: Lawmakers from provincial and municipal assemblies across the country converged on the National Assembly to demand legislation granting them independent staffing and budgetary powers before the end of the year.
  • Saemangeum target moved to 2035: Prime Minister Han Seong-sook announced that the massive Saemangeum coastal reclamation project in North Jeolla Province will transition to a state-driven development model, advancing its completion deadline by 15 years from 2050 to 2035.
  • Sejong administrative capital review: Lawmakers are preparing for a pivotal November 11 parliamentary review of the Administrative Capital Special Act, aimed at cementing Sejong City as the nation's true governing center.
  • Municipal gridlock stalls local bills: Despite calls for greater authority, several local assemblies—including districts in Incheon and Daegu—remain paralyzed by partisan squabbling over council speakerships and disciplinary actions.

Autonomy Demands Clash With Local Council Gridlock

South Korea has maintained a formal system of local self-governance for more than three decades, but regional councils have long operated as administrative lightweights. Without the statutory power to manage their own budgets or appoint their own staff independent of local mayors and governors, councilors argue they are executing half-baked oversight. With the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) aligning behind President Lee Jae-myung’s balanced regional development agenda, local politicians are leveraging national party networks to force parliamentary action on a dedicated Local Council Act.

Yet the push for greater responsibility is undermined by scenes of dysfunction at the grassroots level. While local government chiefs travel to Seoul to lobby for critical infrastructure subsidies, district assemblies in Daegu and Incheon have ground to a halt over leadership posts. This partisan posturing leaves urgent livelihood ordinances unattended, giving critics ammunition to question whether local councils are mature enough to wield expanded powers.

A Decisive Shift Toward State-Led Regional Development

The decision to advance the Saemangeum project's timeline to 2035 marks a sharp break from past policy. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, the ambitious coastal reclamation zone suffered severe budget cuts in the wake of the mishandled 2023 World Scout Jamboree, fueling resentment in southwestern provinces. By ditching reliance on sluggish private investment in favor of direct public funding, the Lee administration is attempting to reposition the area as a state-backed hub for physical artificial intelligence and renewable energy.

Delivering on these promises will require structural backing from the National Assembly. Critical milestones—such as legalizing Sejong City’s status as the full administrative capital and enacting relief measures for past state violence in Yeosu and Suncheon—depend on cooperation with the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. Whether this regional push yields lasting economic decentralization or remains an exercise in political signaling hinges on whether parliament can pass these core frameworks before year-end.
