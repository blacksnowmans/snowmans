Lawmaker Roh Jong-myon's Defensive Explanations Miss the Mark

September 02, 2026

South Korean lawmaker Roh Jong-myon is facing sharp criticism for issuing defensive, self-pitying statements instead of taking accountability for a recent political controversy.

    Roh Jong-myon, a former broadcast journalist turned politician, sparked backlash over messaging linked to an "Ildae" or public interest group controversy involving high-profile political cases. Instead of owning the public fallout, Roh released an explanation blaming poor "political instincts" and arguing that his intentions were misunderstood. Critics argue the response shows a dangerous lack of self-awareness, treating politics as an exercise in defending one's intentions rather than owning real-world results. The episode highlights a recurring frustration with politicians who retreat behind supporters and paint themselves as victims when faced with public criticism.

When Explanations Sound Like Excuses

In politics, voters do not care about noble intentions or misunderstood motives—they judge results. Roh's recent statement attempts to reframe the controversy by claiming his original goals were different and that critics are simply being overly harsh. But as a former investigative anchor, Roh should know better than most that the message received by the public is the only message that matters. Blaming the audience for misinterpreting a signal is a fast track to political irrelevance.

Shifting the blame to external critics or chalking up the mess to a "lack of political sense" is a weak cop-out. It treats public oversight as an annoyance rather than a core part of democratic accountability. When a politician spends more time defending their wounded pride than addressing why their actions caused alarm, they signal to voters that they are more interested in self-justification than public service.

The Trap of Self-Narrative

Roh’s background as a hard-hitting journalist makes his current defensive posture all the more disappointing. Politicians with media backgrounds often fall into the trap of constructing an elaborate internal narrative where they are always the righteous truth-seeker, and anyone who disagrees is an adversary. By framing himself as the victim of excessive criticism, Roh is echoing the exact defensive habits of the powerful figures he once spent his career scrutinizing.

Ultimately, the public does not want to read an essay about a politician's feelings. We want to see leaders who can take a hit, acknowledge when their messaging misses the mark, and redirect their energy toward actual legislative work that affects everyday life. Until politicians stop treating criticism as a personal attack and start treating it as feedback, episodes like this will continue to erode public trust.
