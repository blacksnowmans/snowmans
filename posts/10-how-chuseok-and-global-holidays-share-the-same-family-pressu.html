How Chuseok and Global Holidays Share the Same Family Pressures

September 24, 2026

  • What happened: A recent panel discussion featuring guests from Iran, Pakistan, and Canada explored how major family holidays around the world compare to South Korea's Chuseok (the autumn harvest festival).
  • What it changes: It challenges the common belief that Korean holiday stress—such as forced family gatherings, heavy domestic labor, and awkward probing questions—is a uniquely Korean problem.
  • Who is affected: Anyone navigating the fine line between family obligation and personal burnout during major national or religious holidays.

Beyond South Korea: Family Feasts and Burnout Are Global

Whether it is Nowruz in Iran, Eid in Pakistan, or Christmas in Canada, major holidays universally serve as high-stakes reunions that balance deep communal warmth with inevitable family friction. While Korean media often focuses on the heavy domestic burdens and modern alienation tied to Chuseok and the Lunar New Year, international comparisons show that the core tension of bringing extended families together is a shared human experience.

Each culture has developed its own rituals to handle this balancing act. In Iran, families celebrate Nowruz in spring with symbolic table settings and crisp coin gifts for children. In Pakistan, communities observe Eid with communal prayers and days of shared feasting. Meanwhile, Canadians increasingly opt for practical adjustments—such as renting cabins for Christmas rather than staying cooped up at home—to protect family bonds without driving individuals to exhaustion.

Rethinking Holiday Tradition in Modern Society

The takeaway for modern societies moving past rigid patriarchal traditions is not to abandon family ties altogether, but to redesign how holidays are spent. As younger generations push back against mandatory labor and nagging relatives, the challenge is keeping the warmth of community alive without turning family gatherings into a source of dread.
