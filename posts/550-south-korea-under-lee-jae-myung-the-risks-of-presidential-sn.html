South Korea Under Lee Jae-myung: The Risks of Presidential SNS Politics and Stalled Reforms

October 01, 2026

  • President Lee Jae-myung's active use of social media and controversial personnel appointments are drawing heavy criticism for undermining administrative consistency and alienating the wider public.
  • Critics argue that planned prosecutorial reforms under the new investigative agency (the tentatively named Serious Crime Investigation Agency) remain half-baked, with former opponents of reform reportedly slated for top posts.
  • Economic tensions are rising as effective corporate tax rates for major conglomerates (chaebols) continue to fall below those of small and medium-sized enterprises, deepening wealth inequality.
  • The administration faces mounting pressure to pivot away from partisan factionalism and digital skirmishes, focusing instead on structural tax reform and institutional accountability.

South Korea's public discourse under President Lee Jae-myung is caught in a familiar yet increasingly tense paradox: the push for direct, unfiltered communication with citizens via social media is clashing with the gravity of actual governance. While digital engagement was billed as a modernizing tool for democracy, it has frequently functioned as an unchecked megaphone, creating policy confusion and deepening polarization.

Compounding these political missteps are stalled institutional reforms. Long-standing promises to strip prosecutors of sweeping investigative powers have hit roadblocks, with critics pointing to contradictory high-level appointments that signal continuity with the old guard rather than systemic change. Meanwhile, economic disparities remain unaddressed, as tax policies continue to favor large conglomerates over ordinary citizens and smaller firms.

The Trap of Algorithmic Echo Chambers

A president commands a state of 52 million people, making every digital remark a matter of national weight. Yet, impulsive commentary on social media and feuds with public figures degrade the office, reducing presidential authority to the level of online squabbling. By retreating into an algorithmic bubble dictated by hardline supporters, the administration risks losing touch with the broader electorate.

True communication requires institutional discipline and formal channels—not late-night posts that bypass administrative checks and rattle foreign and domestic markets alike. Moving forward, the Lee administration must decide whether its priority is pandering to a narrow base of online supporters or delivering disciplined, responsible governance that serves all South Koreans.
