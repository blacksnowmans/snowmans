South Korea’s Ruling Party Fast-Tracks Housing and "Livelihood Bills" to Stabilize Domestic Economy

October 31, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea, the nation’s ruling party, has launched a year-end legislative blitz to fast-track ten "livelihood bills" and a massive housing plan to supply more than 76,000 new homes in the Seoul metropolitan area.

  • Housing Supply Acceleration: The party aims to pass the Urban Renovation Act by the end of the year to support the construction of 76,700 housing units in the capital region, focusing on shortening construction timelines.
  • "10 Livelihood Bills" Package: This legislative push targets "everyday safety" and economic stability, covering dating violence, financial fraud prevention, and disaster management.
  • Farmland Reform: A proposed special act seeks to legalize customary farmland leases, resolving a long-standing legal mismatch that has left many elderly farmers in rural areas vulnerable to penalties.
  • Local Budget Crisis: Lawmakers are demanding a total overhaul of the local tax system to address a projected $1.44 billion (2 trillion KRW) shortfall in local government budgets for next year.

Pivoting from Audits to Tangible Results

Following the conclusion of recent parliamentary audits, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is shifting its focus toward the Lee Jae-myung administration’s policy agenda. By prioritizing the supply of 76,700 homes, the DPK is attempting to signal to the market that the era of "announcement-only" housing policy is over. The party is particularly focused on qualitative improvements; for example, a new proposal offers developers a floor-area ratio increase of up to 120% if they commit to expanding green space by at least 10%. This suggests a move away from the dense, "concrete jungle" developments of the past toward more livable urban environments.

The DPK is also tackling deep-seated rural issues that have been ignored for decades. By pushing the Special Act on Farmland, the ruling party is acknowledging that the constitutional principle of "land to the tiller" has become increasingly difficult to maintain in an aging society. Currently, many elderly farmers who can no longer work their land are technically in violation of the law when they lease it out. Legalizing these "customary" leases would bring these farmers back into the fold of government support programs and provide legal clarity for younger tenants. This pragmatic shift demonstrates a willingness to prioritize economic reality over rigid ideological adherence.

Addressing the Fiscal Legacy of the Past

A significant portion of the current legislative push is dedicated to fixing what the DPK calls the "fiscal wreckage" of the previous era. Lawmakers, including Representative Choo Mi-ae, are sounding the alarm over a projected $1.44 billion decrease in available local government funding for next year. The DPK blames this shortfall on the aggressive corporate and wealthy-tax cuts implemented during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, which they argue have left local municipalities unable to fund basic social welfare and infrastructure projects.

As the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, proposes a "3+3" consultative body to discuss property tax deregulation and lending limits, the DPK faces a balancing act. While the ruling party is open to negotiation, it remains wary that the PPP’s focus on deregulation could reignite real estate speculation. Instead, the DPK is doubling down on its "livelihood" branding, focusing on micro-targeted policies like expanding the high-speed KTX-Eum rail service and strengthening legal protections for victims of dating violence—issues that have high public resonance but have historically lacked robust legal frameworks.
