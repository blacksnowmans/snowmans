Why Global Realignments in Brazil and the Middle East Endorse Seoul’s Pragmatic Turn

October 26, 2026

As shifting alliances in South America and the Middle East reveal the fading reliability of traditional security umbrellas, South Korea is reinforcing its push for pragmatic diplomacy and self-reliant defense under President Lee Jae-myung.

  • Saudi Arabia turns to Iran: Unchecked Houthi drone strikes on Aramco refineries and Riyadh’s airport exposed the limits of U.S. protection, forcing Saudi Arabia into direct deal-making with its longtime adversary, Tehran.
  • Lula’s fragile comeback: Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s narrow electoral triumph in Brazil underlines the return of pragmatic "South-South" diplomacy, even as political polarization and voter apathy test his veteran leadership.
  • Israel's structural deadlock: Israeli elections remain caught in a bitter stalemate between Benjamin Netanyahu and military-led opposition slates, with 15 Arab-party swing seats left systematically sidelined.
  • Seoul’s foreign policy pivot: Korean policymakers are citing these global realignments as proof that total strategic reliance on a single foreign power—a policy favored during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration—is a dangerous artifact of the past.

The Collapse of Absolute Security Guarantees

The geopolitical shockwaves rippling through the Middle East offer a stark lesson for middle-tier powers. Despite spending billions on modern military hardware, Saudi Arabia found itself unable to stop inexpensive drone and missile barrages from Yemen's Houthi movement. With Washington no longer willing to underwrite regional security unconditionally, Riyadh has been forced to pursue a pragmatic bargain with Iran to secure its borders. The lesson is brutally simple: in a multipolar world, foreign security guarantees are neither absolute nor permanent.

Seoul's Pragmatic Realism in a Multipolar World

For South Korea, these distant crises reinforce the rationale behind President Lee Jae-myung’s foreign policy framework. By prioritizing self-reliant national defense and flexible engagement with major powers, the current administration aims to avoid the vulnerabilities currently troubling Riyadh. This approach contrasts sharply with the posture under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment—whose strict ideological alignment left little room for strategic maneuvering.

Whether in South America or the Persian Gulf, global events prove that rigid camp-building is giving way to cold realpolitik. As Lula's career long demonstrated that successful governance requires negotiation over dogma, South Korea's survival in an unstable Asia-Pacific hinges on maintaining agile, interest-driven diplomacy rather than passive reliance on external allies.
