Big Tech's AI Slowdown Pushes Out Smaller Rivals

September 21, 2026

  • What happened: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a three-step framework to slow down artificial intelligence development and establish global governance, backed by prominent figures like Elon Musk and OpenAI.
  • What it changes: Major tech companies could use safety regulations and massive computing costs to erect entry barriers, squeezing out smaller startups and open-source developers.
  • Who is affected: AI startups, open-source communities, and everyday users who face an increasingly consolidated digital landscape controlled by a handful of tech giants.

Safety Speech or Monopoly Strategy?

When the leaders of artificial intelligence empires talk about saving humanity from existential risk, it pays to read the fine print. Dario Amodei, CEO of artificial intelligence safety and research firm Anthropic, recently proposed slowing down development to let third-party evaluators inspect systems and allow democratic governments to set safety baselines. Tech heavyweights like Elon Musk quickly chimed in with agreement. But as these firms sink billions of dollars into massive infrastructure—like clusters of up to 400,000 graphics processing units—calls for caution start to look a lot like a defensive moat.

Imposing stringent safety audits and high compliance costs creates a formidable barrier to entry. For venture-backed startups and open-source groups, these regulatory hurdles can be fatal. In practice, the safety debate functions as a strategic lever for industry leaders to lock out up-and-coming rivals and cement their own market dominance. Meanwhile, insiders are sounding alarms about the true state of internal research. Former Anthropic engineer Jacob Coxton publicly warned that companies are racing blindly toward self-improving superintelligence while gambling with public safety, exposing a glaring contradiction between public PR campaigns and internal acceleration.

Rapid Breakthroughs and the Illusion of Control

Outside the boardroom politics, technical progress marches forward at an astonishing pace. Google’s research labs recently mapped a male fruit fly's brain and successfully integrated that neural network architecture into reinforcement learning environments, testing everything from video games to cryptocurrency trading and humanoid robot control. Similarly, a swarm of AI agents recently collaborated to crack the Navier-Stokes equations, a fluid dynamics math problem that had resisted full solutions for 90 years. While impressive, these feats spark fierce debates over whether the models are doing genuine, novel research or simply regurgitating patterns from vast training data.

As autonomous digital agents gain root-level control over consumer operating systems and mobile devices, the boundary between user and tool is dissolving. Big tech companies are no longer just building software applications; they are building autonomous systems that operate across computers and networks with minimal human oversight. The rush toward artificial general intelligence is reshaping the global tech order, marked by aggressive data harvesting and geopolitical rivalry—such as recent reports of Chinese models secretly siphoning responses from Western systems. Users are left on the sidelines of a high-stakes power struggle, enjoying incremental conveniences while handing over deep personal data and control to opaque systems.
