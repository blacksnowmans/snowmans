Don’t Lose Your Deposit: A Guide to Reading Korean Property Records

September 08, 2026

In South Korea's high-stakes real estate market, failing to properly analyze a property’s registration document—the deunggi-bu deungbon—can lead to losing hundreds of thousands of dollars in a heartbeat.

  • What happened: Many buyers and tenants treat property records as a formality, ignoring critical errors that can lead to seizure, legal disputes, or hidden debt.
  • The risk: Simple oversights, such as missing "land rights" or ignoring previous tax seizures, can leave a buyer paying for a house they don't fully own.
  • The takeaway: The responsibility for verifying a property’s legal standing rests entirely on the buyer or tenant; the system will not protect you if you fail to check the details yourself.

The Hidden Traps in Property Records

When buying an apartment or studio, you are not just purchasing a building—you must also acquire the daejigwon, or the rights to the land beneath it. In some cases, developers fail to pay for the land correctly, resulting in units being registered without these rights. If you unknowingly buy a "landless" apartment at full market price (e.g., $500,000, or about 700 million won), you may later be hit with an additional bill of $140,000 (about 200 million won) to secure the land title.

Furthermore, never rely solely on the registration document. It is essential to cross-reference it with the geonchukmul daejang (the official architectural ledger). If the two documents disagree—such as discrepancies in floor area—the architectural ledger takes precedence. Relying on the wrong document could leave you liable for an illegal construction fine or result in paying a premium for square footage that doesn't legally exist.

Watching the "Economic Traffic Lights"

The Gap-gu section of the registration document serves as the history of a property's ownership. Look beyond the current owner. If you see repeated records of seizures (amnyu) and subsequent cancellations, it is a glaring red flag indicating the landlord is in financial distress. A landlord who struggles with tax payments, such as the frequent tax-related seizures occasionally flagged by local authorities, is a high risk for failing to return your security deposit.

Equally critical is the Eul-gu section, which details debt. A standard rule of thumb is to avoid properties where the sum of existing mortgage loans and your security deposit exceeds 80% of the property's total value. If the document shows "housing lease registration" (jutaek imchakwon), it is a warning that a previous tenant was evicted without receiving their deposit—a property to be avoided at all costs.

Ultimately, checking these records is not an act of distrust toward the landlord or the real estate agent; it is basic self-defense. For a cost of about $1 (1,000 won), you can access the information necessary to protect your life savings. In the Korean property market, the only person looking out for your interests is you.
