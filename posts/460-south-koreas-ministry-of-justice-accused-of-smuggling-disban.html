South Korea’s Ministry of Justice Accused of Smuggling Disbanded Prosecutors Back Through the Back Door

September 08, 2026

  • South Korea's Ministry of Justice has unveiled a reorganization plan for the newly established Prosecution Service that critics argue preserves the old investigative apparatus under a different name.
  • Despite a landmark legislative push to completely separate investigation and indictment powers, the new blueprint retains roughly 7,000 investigators within the prosecution and requests hundreds more prosecutors.
  • The move has sparked fierce backlash from reform advocates who fear the revival of the powerful prosecutorial cartel through administrative enforcement decrees.

South Korea thought it had permanently split the monolithic powers of its prosecution service when it passed historic legislation separating investigation from indictment. But a newly proposed organizational decree from the Ministry of Justice suggests the old guard is attempting to resurrect its empire through administrative fine print. Rather than downsizing to match its stripped-down mandate of handling only trials and indictments, the agency has crafted a restructuring plan that critics have dubbed a "Trojan horse."

The numbers tell a suspicious story. While the ministry claims a 21.4% reduction in overall headcount for the new Prosecution Service—bringing total staff to 8,412—the vast majority of those reductions come from 1,761 personnel moving to the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. In reality, the core investigative engine remains almost entirely intact, leaving about 7,000 investigators inside the prosecution's offices. Stranger still, the ministry is asking for 281 *more* prosecutors, a bizarre request for an institution that is supposedly shedding its investigative duties.

A Trojan Horse of Bureaucratic Control and Cartel Survival

The restructuring plan goes beyond mere headcount preservation by actively carving out new spaces to meddle in investigations. The ministry plans to install a "judicial control department" in local prosecution offices to review dropped cases from other agencies. For an institution historically criticized for selective justice and looking the other way for its own, granting prosecutors the power to police independent investigative bodies is a stunning piece of audaciously inverted logic. Instead of being audited, the auditors want to become the ultimate supervisors of everyone else.

Old Habits Die Hard in South Korea's Justice System

This institutional sleight-of-hand is mirrored at the top. The nomination of lawyer Kim Ji-young—a former senior prosecutor who fiercely opposed stripping the prosecution of its supplementary investigative powers—to head the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency threatens to create an uncomfortably cozy "two roofs, one family" dynamic. With former prosecutors poised to run both the indictment agency and the independent investigative agency, the separation of powers risks becoming a polite fiction.

Global standards in civil law countries generally restrict prosecutors from commanding massive direct-investigation wings, favoring a system where prosecutors objectively supervise police. South Korea’s recent reforms were meant to drag its uniquely omnipotent prosecution service down from its untouchable perch to meet these international norms. The Constitutional Court has already affirmed that the legislature has every right to trim the prosecution's wings. Yet, as this latest decree shows, powerful bureaucracies rarely surrender their grip on power without a fight, preferring to weaponize administrative decrees to bypass the clear intent of the legislature.
