South Korea's Political Polarization Hits the Family Dinner Table as Economic Pain Deepens

September 29, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's recent Chuseok holiday family gatherings were marked by widespread political avoidance and bitter disputes, reflecting a society where 83% of people view generational and political conflict as severe, according to a recent Hankook Research survey.
  • The flashpoints: President Lee Jae-myung faced heavy criticism after his official X (formerly Twitter) account retweeted posts containing abusive language, alongside mounting frustration over a slowing reform agenda and soaring housing costs.
  • Who is affected: Ordinary citizens trapped in a worsening rental crisis, young adults making up nearly half of all personal bankruptcy filings due to high-risk leveraged investments, and a fractured political base.
  • The core takeaway: While the administration preaches caution to avoid a political backlash against reforms, its detachment from compounding economic misery and reliance on partisan rhetoric risk permanently alienating public trust.

Family Feuds and the Trap of Partisan Echo Chambers

In South Korea, traditional holiday gatherings are increasingly defined by self-censorship and anxiety. Rather than fostering connection, family dinners have become battlegrounds shaped by fragmented media consumption and deep-seated economic inequality. Discussions around public figures and political strategy frequently dissolve into mutual distrust, mirroring a broader national slide into hyper-polarization where an individual's entire character is judged by the social media channels they follow.

Compounding this social fatigue is a growing disconnect within the ruling camp. Commentators have noted that supporters are split between blind loyalists, conditional reformers, and disillusioned voters who have pulled back from active support. Yet, rather than addressing these internal fractures, top leadership has struggled with messaging. President Lee's recent social media controversy—where his account amplified hostile rhetoric before being deleted—and his subsequent defense framing supporters as "warriors of light" highlighted an administration too comfortable leaning into toxic online tribalism instead of rising above it.

Reform Rhetoric Versus a Crushing Economic Reality

The administration's political positioning has grown increasingly contradictory. During a recent overseas trip to Europe, President Lee warned against overzealous reforms, invoking the excesses of the French Revolution to caution that pushing too hard invites a conservative backlash. Yet, this calls-for-moderation stance contrasts sharply with aggressive policies elsewhere, such as a sweeping mandatory inspection of agricultural land aimed at rooting out speculation—a move pursued despite fierce public resistance.

This uneven application of political will is hitting ordinary people hard, particularly as everyday economic pressures spiral out of control. Seoul's rental market is in deep distress, with traditional lump-sum *jeonse* leases disappearing and monthly rents surging by up to 200%. Meanwhile, South Korea's younger generation is bearing the brunt of a financial squeeze: adults in their 20s and 30s now account for 40% to 50% of personal bankruptcy filings, often after turning to high-risk leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs) in desperation.

When leadership responds to systemic financial distress and youth bankruptcy with public indifference, it crosses the line from political misstep into institutional failure. Vague warnings about moderation cannot replace basic economic competence. Unless the government pivots from defensive rhetoric to tangible relief for struggling households, the widening gap between elite political survival and everyday South Korean survival will prove politically fatal.
