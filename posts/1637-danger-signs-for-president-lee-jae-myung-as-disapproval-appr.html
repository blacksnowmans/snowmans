Danger Signs for President Lee Jae-myung as Disapproval Approaches 60%

October 28, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s administration has entered perilous territory as his approval ratings sink below 40% while negative sentiment approaches 60% across multiple polling outlets.

  • The 60-to-35 trap: Recent tracking surveys show Lee's disapproval climbing to between 57.7% and 62%, while his approval has dropped to the 35% to 41.5% range, wiping out post-inauguration highs that once cleared 65%.
  • Ruling party bleed: Support for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) fell beneath 40% to 39.7%, driven by a steep 13.8-percentage-point defection among self-identified progressives.
  • Minor progressives surge: The Rebuilding Korea Party has seen its support among progressive voters more than double to 22.4%, capturing alienated center-left voters seeking an alternative.
  • Leadership under fire: DPK leader Kim Min-seok faces mounting pressure as his personal disapproval rating climbed to 63.4%, with a majority of progressives now evaluating his performance negatively.

The Triple Breakdown Fueling Discontent

The swift erosion of Lee’s political capital stems from simultaneous friction across policy, ideology, and personal trust. Practical measures—including revisions to the Commercial Act aimed at lifting market valuations and youth-targeted budget allocations—have struggled to deliver immediate, felt benefits to ordinary households. At the same time, traditional progressive voters increasingly feel the administration is drifting away from longstanding party commitments to welfare, equality, and fairness.

Crucially, Lee lacks the emotional cushion enjoyed by previous liberal leaders like the late President Roh Moo-hyun, whose personal favorability consistently ran 15 points higher than his job approval. Without that emotional baseline of goodwill, skepticism over governance easily hardens into personal rejection. If voters decide the president’s political style is rigid or insincere, even well-designed policy proposals struggle to regain public traction.

The "Lifeboat" Dilemma on the Left

The sudden migration of voters to the Rebuilding Korea Party signals division rather than expansion. Initially seen as an aggressive "icebreaker" tackling judicial and prosecutorial reform, the party is increasingly functioning as a political "lifeboat" for disaffected liberals abandoning the governing DPK. This creates a deepening strategic contradiction: veteran party loyalists favor eventually merging with the DPK to consolidate influence, while newly arriving defectors adamantly oppose reuniting with a governing ship they view as foundering.

The political warning lights are flashing yellow. A locked-in dynamic of 60% disapproval against 35% approval mirrors the exact structural pattern that crippled Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's administration in its opening months before his eventual impeachment. In South Korea’s presidential system, legislative power tracks presidential approval closely. Unless President Lee and DPK leadership can reconcile with their alienated base, the ruling party risks a permanent fracture that leaves its reform agenda stalled.
