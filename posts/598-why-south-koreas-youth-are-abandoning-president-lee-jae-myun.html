Why South Korea's Youth Are Abandoning President Lee Jae-myung

September 10, 2026

South Korea’s ruling administration is facing a sharp political warning as its approval ratings slide for the eighth consecutive week, driven by a growing sense of betrayal among young voters facing crushing living costs and stagnant economic mobility.

  • What happened: President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings have dropped for eight straight weeks, accompanied by a noticeable silence from the young, online core supporters who once defended the administration.
  • What it changes: The disconnect exposes deep frustration among casual political observers over tanking stock portfolios, grueling housing costs, and muddled labor policies.
  • Who is affected: Young adults in their 20s and late 20s—whose median monthly take-home pay of about $1,900 (2.59 million won) is heavily swallowed by surging rents and hidden maintenance fees—alongside cash-strapped parents.

The Economic Squeeze: Rent, Stocks, and Broken Promises

According to a panel of political commentators and local figures, the administration’s sliding support is not merely a matter of partisan opposition, but a fundamental crisis of expectations. For many young South Koreans who poured their savings into the stock market following the government's push away from real estate speculation, deep market losses have turned political enthusiasm into bitter disillusionment. When a young worker's portfolio is in the red, grand political discourse takes a back seat to basic financial survival.

Housing remains an even more brutal reality. While average Seoul one-room rents sit around $470 to $480 (640,000 to 650,000 won)—and climb past $700 in key districts like Gangnam, Mapo, and Yongsan—that figure hides a darker trick: landlords routinely slash base rent while inflating monthly maintenance fees to $110–$150, pushing actual fixed housing costs past $730 a month. Public housing options offer little relief, often requiring massive deposits and loan interest that bring total monthly expenses right back to commercial rates. For a demographic earning a net median income of about $1,900 a month, dedicating nearly half their paycheck merely to keep a roof over their heads leaves no room for future planning or family formation.

Policy Gaps and the Erosion of Trust

The alienation goes beyond economics into policy execution. When the administration intervened in a high-profile bonus dispute at Samsung Electronics, pro-labor youth who had expected steadfast support for workers felt a jarring sense of betrayal as the government stepped in to protect corporate stability. Similarly, large-scale public housing pledges, such as proposals for 15,000 new units in Yongsan backed by the Rebuilding Korea Party (a minor progressive opposition party), are increasingly viewed by alienated young adults as out-of-reach lottery tickets that do nothing for their immediate survival.

Commentators note that the government's daily barrage of triumphant performance briefings is entirely disconnected from ground-level realities. As long as youth politicians are bogged down in factional turf wars and policymakers ignore hidden housing scams and moldy basement apartments, clever rhetoric will not win back public trust. A government cannot run on grand visions while its foundational supporters are drowning in everyday expenses.
