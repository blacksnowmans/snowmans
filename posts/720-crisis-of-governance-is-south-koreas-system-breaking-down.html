Crisis of Governance: Is South Korea’s System Breaking Down?

October 05, 2026

South Korea is currently grappling with a convergence of crises—ranging from runaway housing prices to compromised national security—that suggests a fundamental breakdown in public administration and state control.

  • Property Market Collapse: Housing prices have risen for 86 consecutive weeks, signaling that government policies have failed to provide the promised stability for citizens.
  • Security Gaps: Recent incursions by North Korean drones and mishandled landmine incidents have exposed a lack of decisive leadership within the Ministry of National Defense.
  • Erosion of Public Order: Law enforcement is increasingly viewed as passive, failing to address disruptive political protests in public spaces like Olympic Park or the harassment of private citizens at their homes.
  • Systemic Inaction: Critics argue that the current administration is masking its inability to govern by utilizing aggressive political rhetoric rather than implementing practical, data-driven solutions.

The Failure of Housing and Security Policy

The current administration, which rose to power by criticizing the housing policies of its predecessor, is now facing a more severe crisis of its own. With 86 weeks of uninterrupted price growth, the housing market is no longer just an economic concern; it is a structural failure that widens the wealth gap and leaves younger generations without a path to homeownership. Rather than addressing this with effective reform, the government is accused of doubling down on rhetoric and relying on partisan supporters to dismiss legitimate criticism.

Parallel to the domestic housing crisis is a growing alarm over national security. The Ministry of National Defense has faced backlash for labeling hostile incursions as "unfortunate accidents" rather than clear threats. This hesitation, coupled with an apparent lack of a strong directive from the Commander-in-Chief, has created a perception of power vacuums. When a state cannot protect its borders or the daily peace of its citizens, it loses the fundamental trust required to govern effectively.

The Erosion of Public Order and Accountability

Perhaps most concerning to the public is the perceived paralysis of law enforcement. In spaces ranging from major landmarks like Olympic Park to the private residences of public figures, political protesters are increasingly violating the rights of others with little to no police intervention. By hiding behind the concept of "freedom of assembly," these groups are often infringing upon the daily lives of citizens, while authorities remain notably silent.

This passivity is not merely a lack of policing; it is a symptom of a deeper administrative failure. When public order is sacrificed to appease political factions or to avoid confrontation, the rule of law is hollowed out. For the average Korean, the "system" is beginning to feel less like a guarantor of stability and more like an observer of its own decline. Moving forward, the government must prioritize transparency, admit to policy failures, and re-establish the basic functions of the state to regain the public's confidence.
