President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Offensive Sparks Concern Over Governance

October 10, 2026

  • What happened: President Lee Jae-myung has increasingly used his personal social media accounts to launch direct, aggressive attacks against critics and perceived political opponents, abandoning the nuanced communication typical of his office.
  • Why it matters: Critics, including journalist Jang In-soo, argue this shift indicates a move toward binary, “us-vs-them” politics that discourages internal strategic advice and undermines the dignity of the presidency.
  • What’s next: The administration faces growing internal friction as key political figures and allies begin to publicly push back against the President’s combative tone and his administration's failure to deliver on core campaign promises.

The Shift Toward "Emotional" Governance

The days when observers had to carefully decode the President’s statements are over. Under President Lee Jae-myung—leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's primary liberal party—the messaging has become starkly blunt. Rather than relying on refined statements drafted with his staff, Lee has increasingly used his personal social media to vent frustrations directly to his core supporters. This "Twitter-first" approach is being widely criticized as an undignified display of emotion that prioritizes personal grievances over stable governance.

Journalist Jang In-soo has characterized this behavior as an addiction to social media, noting that the President’s shift toward polarizing rhetoric leaves no room for political flexibility. When the head of state treats policy debate as a personal battleground, the collateral damage is borne by the public. By labeling those who demand accountability for broken promises as "extremists," the President is inadvertently admitting that his own agenda has lost touch with its original reformist goals.

Missing Promises and Historical Truths

The disconnect between campaign pledges and current reality is most visible in the administration’s handling of judicial reforms. Despite making "Prosecution Reform"—specifically the separation of investigation and prosecution powers—a flagship agenda item, the DPK administration has allowed the prosecution to maintain its grip on key bureaucratic positions. Furthermore, the Office of Investigation for Corruption among High-ranking Officials (CIO), which was intended to act as a powerful check on power, now faces internal pressure from the DPK to restrict its own scope, leading to concerns that the party aims to neutralize any oversight that hinders its own influence.

In a separate but revealing development, newly unearthed diplomatic archives from Hungary have provided a definitive blow to long-standing "conspiracy theories" regarding the 1980 Gwangju Democratization Movement. The documents confirm that North Korean leadership was caught off guard by the uprising, proving that the oft-cited claim of "North Korean infiltration" in Gwangju is factually baseless.

Ultimately, these events highlight a dangerous trend: the prioritization of political framing over empirical truth and campaign obligations. As the administration continues to use the legacy of the late President Roh Moo-hyun as a shield for its current struggles, it risks alienating the very supporters who once believed in its promise of systemic change. For the public, the challenge remains clear: to move beyond the cathartic buzz of social media attacks and demand tangible results and honest accountability from those in power.
