Ruling Party and Lee Administration Clash Over Property Tax Relief

November 09, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the Lee Jae-myung presidential office have clashed sharply over property tax policy, deadlocking on whether absentee single-home owners should receive the same tax breaks as owner-occupiers.

  • The parliamentary push: Democratic Party lawmakers representing Seoul districts demanded a flat 1.4 billion won (about $1.05 million) deduction on the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax for all single-home owners, regardless of actual residency.
  • The government’s veto: Cheong Wa Dae rejected the proposal during a high-level coordination meeting on November 8, insisting on a tiered system that grants 1.4 billion won to owner-occupiers but caps non-resident owners at 1.2 billion won (about $900,000).
  • Who is affected: Hundreds of thousands of single-home owners in the Seoul metropolitan area—especially elderly retirees facing steep assessments and "gap investors" who bought rental-backed apartments without moving in.
  • The political stakes: Ruling party legislators fear a suburban middle-class backlash at the ballot box, while the Lee administration refuses to gut anti-speculation guardrails for short-term political gains.

Principles vs. Populism Ahead of the Vote

The rift exposes deep anxieties inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea ahead of the upcoming legislative elections. Following years of severe asset inequality and market instability during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, voters in the capital region have grown intensely hostile to property taxes. Lawmakers from high-priced Seoul districts argue that distinguishing between resident and non-resident owners creates bureaucratic confusion and unfairly burdens retirees who own a single home but cannot reside in it. Desperate to stem voter attrition, the party wants an immediate, across-the-board deduction ceiling.

For President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, however, that concession crosses a red line. The core tenet of the Lee government’s housing agenda is shielding owner-occupiers while cracking down on speculative, non-residential ownership. Cheong Wa Dae officials warn that extending identical tax relief to absentee owners would effectively subsidize leveraged "gap investment" and gut the progressive character of the holding tax. In their view, lifting the non-resident baseline from the previous 900 million won (about $675,000) to 1.2 billion won is already generous enough.

Mixed Signals Cloud the Real Estate Outlook

The open discord between the ruling party and the government sends a confusing signal to an already jittery property market. When tax thresholds jump from 900 million won to 1.2 billion won, and then to 1.4 billion won in legislative bargaining within months, tax predictability disappears. Buyers and sellers freeze their decisions, waiting to see which side blinks first.

Moreover, conflicting signals undermine policy credibility. While Democratic Party politicians promise sweeping tax relief to placate homeowners, financial authorities continue to enforce tighter loan restrictions to curb speculation. By treating the tax code as an electoral bargaining chip, political leaders risk fueling speculative expectations just when housing stabilization requires consistent, principled policy enforcement.
