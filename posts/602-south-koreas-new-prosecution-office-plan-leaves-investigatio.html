South Korea's New Prosecution Office Plan Leaves Investigation Power Intact

September 10, 2026

  • South Korea's Ministry of Justice has unveiled a reorganization plan to split investigation and indictment powers, creating a new "Prosecution Office" focused solely on trials.
  • Despite stripping the agency of direct investigative powers, the plan keeps 7,000 investigators and nearly all 2,292 prosecutors, cutting only 238 actual positions.
  • Critics argue the massive retained workforce and new oversight units will allow prosecutors to meddle in investigations and revive the old prosecutorial power structure.

South Korea’s latest push to reform its powerful prosecution service risks becoming an empty shell. On paper, the Ministry of Justice is dividing investigative and indicting powers to curb decades of unchecked authority. But a close look at the staffing numbers reveals that the sprawling infrastructure of the old prosecution service remains virtually untouched. When an agency sheds its investigative duties yet retains thousands of investigators and prosecutors, it raises an obvious question: what is the reform actually changing?

The core problem lies in the institutional loopholes designed into the new structure. Rather than slimming down to match a trial-only mandate, the plan introduces specialized "judicial control" and "major crime" units within the new office. These departments are positioned to cooperate with and oversee other investigative bodies, such as the newly planned Major Crime Investigation Agency and the police. By keeping a massive workforce and creating administrative workarounds, the ministry is effectively preserving a standing army that can step back into direct investigations whenever political winds change.

The Danger of Recreating the Old Legal Cartel

Beyond the numbers, the personnel choices threaten to undermine the entire reform. Lawmakers and commentators have raised urgent warnings about appointing former prosecutors to head the new independent investigative bodies. If prosecutors-turned-reformers run both the investigative agencies and the new Prosecution Office, they will simply recreate the old informal networks under new names. True reform requires a complete "de-prosecutorialization" of personnel, ensuring that those leading the new era do not rely on the institutional habits of the past.

Ultimately, splitting institutions means little if the culture remains the same. Unless the Ministry of Justice strips away these ambiguous oversight units and slashes the bloated staff numbers to match actual trial needs, this reorganization will fail. Instead of dismantling an overreaching power, South Korea may simply end up with two separate, collaborating entities carrying on the exact same political and investigative habits.
