South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Dilemma Over Impeaching Chief Justice

October 21, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party is grappling with a high-stakes demand from its progressive allies to impeach the nation's Chief Justice, potentially deepening a political divide as the government’s approval ratings slide.

  • The Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, is publicly pressuring the ruling Democratic Party to co-sponsor an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.
  • President Lee Jae-myung has ordered a "housing supply blitz" to bypass administrative red tape and called for urgent legislative action to impose heavier fines on companies responsible for fatal industrial accidents.
  • A major legislative battle has emerged over the "Super-regional Special Zone Act," as the ruling party attempts to balance regional development with controversial exemptions to the standard 52-hour work week.
  • New polling from Yeon-ron-jo-sa-gong-jeong shows the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), leading the ruling Democratic Party by 14 percentage points (47.3% to 33.3%).

Judicial Impeachment and the Risk of Backlash

The push to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae represents a dangerous gamble for the Democratic Party. While the Rebuilding Korea Party argues that removing the Chief Justice is essential for "judicial justice," ruling party leaders are hesitant. They fear that aggressive use of impeachment powers could alienate moderate voters and revive the "legislative tyranny" narrative that has previously hurt them. Memories of the legal and social upheaval during the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol remain fresh in the public mind, and a failed attempt in the Constitutional Court would hand the conservative opposition a massive political victory.

The PPP has characterized the move as an attempt to "tame the judiciary" and an attack on the separation of powers. This tension was on full display during the ongoing National Assembly audits, where lawmakers clashed over the independence of the courts. With the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) currently reviewing multiple complaints against the Chief Justice, the judiciary finds itself at the center of a worsening political firestorm that threatens to overshadow urgent legislative business.

Policy Pivots Amid Falling Approval

President Lee Jae-myung is attempting to reclaim the narrative by focusing on "livelihood issues" (min-saeng). His directive to speed up the housing supply—shortening the gap between land designation and actual construction—is a response to persistent public anxiety over real estate. Furthermore, his vocal frustration regarding stalled legislation for industrial safety fines highlights the friction between his administration’s pro-labor goals and a National Assembly where the average bill takes 644 days to pass. By criticizing the "sluggish" pace of the legislature, the President is trying to position the executive branch as the primary driver of progress.

However, the ruling party’s "two-track" approach to labor laws suggests internal confusion. By simultaneously proposing bills that both include and exclude exceptions to the 52-hour work week in special economic zones, the Democratic Party is trying to please both labor unions and regional business interests. With the conservative PPP using its recent polling lead to obstruct government initiatives, the Lee administration faces an uphill battle in translating its policy goals into tangible results for a public that is increasingly looking toward the opposition.
