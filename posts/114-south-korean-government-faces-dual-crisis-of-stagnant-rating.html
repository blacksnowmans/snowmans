South Korean Government Faces Dual Crisis of Stagnant Ratings and Internal Infighting

September 29, 2026

  • President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dipped below the critical 40% threshold, with his ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) suffering a corresponding decline in popularity.
  • Public sentiment, gathered during the Chuseok holiday, reflects deep frustration over high consumer prices and a perception that the administration is consumed by internal power struggles rather than economic relief.
  • Key leadership figures, including DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok, are facing record-low approval ratings, signaling a growing disconnect between party elites and the electorate.

The post-holiday political atmosphere in Seoul has shifted from routine parliamentary friction to a full-blown existential crisis for the ruling block. After touring local markets and speaking with voters, lawmakers returned with a clear message: the public is exhausted. While the DPK has historically relied on a strong base, recent polling indicates that voters no longer distinguish between the President’s failures and the party’s performance, viewing the entire administration as a single, struggling entity.

The core of the frustration lies in the disconnect between the government's rhetoric and the reality of the "shopping basket" economy. With inflation tracking at roughly 3.1%, citizens are calling for tangible solutions to the cost-of-living crisis rather than the performative, vitriolic squabbling that has come to define the DPK’s recent internal discourse.

A Leadership in Disarray

The party’s internal dynamics have worsened, with DPK leaders often acting as a political liability rather than a shield for the President. Chairman Kim Min-seok’s current job approval rating—with 51% of respondents disapproving of his performance—highlights a failure to consolidate party support. Instead of acting as a "big tent," the leadership has been accused of fostering an environment where internal dissent is treated as betrayal. This has alienated moderate voters and frustrated supporters who expected a competent reformist agenda.

The government’s attempt to rally its base through social media—essentially asking supporters to engage in online information warfare against "manipulated minorities"—has backfired. Rather than uniting the country, the move is perceived as an effort to mobilize "us-versus-them" tactics. This strategy, combined with the administrative paralysis at the Blue House due to a high-level vacancy in the Chief of Staff and Policy Chief positions, has left the administration looking rudderless.

External Pressures and Diplomatic Missteps

The administration is also struggling on the international stage. A recent incident involving Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, who publicly disclosed sensitive intelligence regarding North Korean military personnel, has blindsided the Seoul government. The incident highlights the risks of Korea’s involvement in the Ukraine conflict, with critics arguing the government’s lack of strategic ambiguity has left it vulnerable to being used for the political objectives of foreign leaders.

Whether it is the failure to manage diplomatic leaks or the inability to address domestic judicial reforms—such as the polarizing debate over whether to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—the administration is currently losing the initiative. Unless the DPK shifts its focus from internal "loyalty contests" to addressing the material needs of the electorate, the coming months are likely to bring further erosion of their political capital.
