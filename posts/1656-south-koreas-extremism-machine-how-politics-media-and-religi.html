South Korea’s Extremism Machine: How Politics, Media, and Religion Built an Outrage Economy

October 29, 2026

South Korea’s deepening culture of hate and political polarization is not a series of isolated incidents, but the product of a self-sustaining extremism ecosystem driven by political opportunism, religious nationalism, and algorithmic profit models.

  • What happened: Far-right rhetoric, once relegated to the fringes of Korean society, has successfully infiltrated mainstream politics, legacy media, and religious institutions, mirroring global populist trends.
  • Who is affected: Marginalized groups—including migrant workers, religious minorities, and political opponents—face escalating everyday violence and scapegoating.
  • What it changes: Democratic norms, judicial oversight, and basic mutual respect are steadily eroding as extremism is normalized as a profitable and politically useful tool.
  • The political shift: With former conservative President Yoon Suk Yeol impeached and imprisoned, and the progressive Democratic Party of Korea now in power under President Lee Jae-myung, right-wing factions have increasingly leaned into anti-establishment grievance politics.

The Anatomy of an Outrage Ecosystem

Far-right movements in South Korea rarely operate in a vacuum; they thrive by borrowing playbooks from global populist networks, such as those seen in Donald Trump’s orbit and Jair Bolsonaro’s supporters in Brazil. Observers note that these movements rely heavily on a "politics of exclusion," constantly manufacturing new targets of hate—from undocumented immigrants to minority communities—to forge internal solidarity among anxious citizens.

This dynamic feeds directly into the domestic media landscape. Korean media outlets, under relentless pressure to generate clicks and ad revenue in a crowded digital market, routinely amplify fringe, inflammatory figures under the guise of "balanced reporting." The result is a toxic loop where outrageous rhetoric drives views, views drive revenue, and algorithms push vulnerable users further down rabbit holes of radicalization.

When Faith and Bigotry Merge

A uniquely potent driver of South Korea's right-wing radicalization is its intersection with conservative Christian nationalism. Rooted in historical grievances and a sense of lost status, certain religious groups have fused theological purity tests with hardline political agendas. By framing political disagreements as "spiritual warfare" between absolute good and absolute evil, these factions make compromise impossible and cast democratic institutions as enemies of faith.

Mainstream political parties, particularly the main conservative opposition People Power Party, have repeatedly courted these radical factions for votes and organizational muscle. When political leaders validate extremist narratives rather than drawing a firm line against bigotry, they signal to the public that the rule of law is negotiable. Rebuilding South Korea's democratic health will require more than a change in administration; it demands that media, politicians, and civil society stop treating societal hate as a harmless business model.
