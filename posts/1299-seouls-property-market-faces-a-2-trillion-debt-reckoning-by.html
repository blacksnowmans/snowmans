Seoul’s Property Market Faces a $2 Trillion Debt Reckoning by 2027

October 19, 2026

Seoul’s housing market is barreling toward a severe correction by 2027, when an estimated 2,822 trillion won (about $2.1 trillion) in residential mortgages face mandatory interest rate resets at significantly higher borrowing costs.

  • A massive debt reset is looming: Five-year hybrid fixed-rate loans taken out during the 2021–2022 buying frenzy will expire in waves starting in late 2026, forcing borrowers from sub-3% rates into loans above 4%.
  • Extreme exposure to floating rates: Over 57% of South Korea’s outstanding household loans and nearly 65% of new mortgages carry variable rates, leaving households far more exposed to rate shocks than peers in the United States or Europe.
  • Policy-fueled leverage: South Korea's total household debt has crossed 2,020 trillion won (about $1.5 trillion), driven in part by relaxed lending programs during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol that temporarily cushioned the market after the 2022 credit freeze.
  • Drying liquidity: About 70% of recent Seoul home purchases were funded by cashing out equity gains, a liquidity channel that has collapsed alongside turbulent global stock markets.

The Illusion of the 88-Week Rally

Seoul apartments recently logged an unprecedented 88-week continuous price rally, gaining 17.9% cumulatively. Yet this growth masks severe imbalances. While prices in affordable northern districts like Seongbuk surged nearly 15%, wealthy southern enclaves like Gangnam have seen weekly declines. A study by the Korea Research Institute for Human Settlements shows that benchmark interest rates account for 60.7% of domestic housing price changes, while physical housing supply explains just 8.5%. Simply put, cheap credit drove the boom, not genuine real estate fundamentals.

South Korea already saw how fast sentiment can evaporate. In late 2022, following debt defaults linked to the Legoland theme park development in Gangwon Province, 10-year treasury yields topped 4% for just seven weeks. In response, Seoul apartment sales plunged to an all-time low of 558 transactions in October 2022, and nationwide transaction prices dropped 22% for the year. That downturn was swiftly arrested by artificial stimulus, including state-backed 50-year mortgages that merely postponed the inevitable adjustment.

Why Supply Shortages Cannot Offset Macro Headwinds

Real estate bulls argue that a looming supply cliff will prop up valuations. Seoul is projected to add only 15,000 new apartments in 2028, down from 37,000 in 2025, while urban redevelopment will displace roughly 45,000 households that year. While this shortfall will spike rental prices in the short run, it cannot manufacture purchasing power out of thin air. With bank delinquency rates already hitting a decade high of 0.63%, households constrained by strict debt-service ratio caps simply cannot borrow more to chase escalating prices.

Now under President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea faces a critical transition away from debt-fueled asset inflation toward macroprudential deleveraging. For prospective buyers, treating Seoul real estate as an invincible safe haven ignores basic financial gravity. As multi-trillion-dollar debt resets approach, the cost of borrowing will determine market pricing far more than any local housing shortage.
