Why Chinese E-Commerce Giants Are Losing Their Grip on Korea

October 14, 2026

Chinese e-commerce platforms AliExpress and Temu are facing a deepening crisis in South Korea as aggressive marketing fails to overcome mounting concerns over product safety, deceptive pricing, and poor service.

  • The Marketing Mirage: Despite spending over $90 million (100 billion KRW) on aggressive campaigns, these platforms are now seeing their growth stall as customer trust evaporates.
  • Financial Strain: AliExpress Korea’s advertising expenses recently exceeded its revenue, creating a loss-making structure where the company spends more to acquire users than it earns from them.
  • Safety Scandals: Reports of heavy metals in low-cost jewelry and products failing local safety standards (KC certification) have caused widespread public backlash.
  • Consumer Exodus: Average spending per user has plummeted, as many Koreans now view these apps as sites for "one-off" cheap purchases rather than reliable shopping channels.

The Limits of "Cheap" Strategies

When these platforms first entered the market, they were met with skepticism. However, they initially gained traction by slashing delivery times from weeks to just three days by setting up local warehouses. For a brief period, they appeared to be legitimate rivals to established local giants like Coupang—South Korea’s dominant e-commerce leader—and Naver, the country’s top search engine and platform operator.

The honeymoon phase was short-lived. Behind the rapid growth metrics lay a fragile business model. Data shows that users are increasingly acting as "cherry pickers," buying only heavily discounted bait products while avoiding actual necessities. A direct price comparison for everyday goods—like instant noodles or toothpaste—reveals that these platforms are often more expensive than local competitors, exposing their "thousand-won" marketing as a tactical illusion.

Regulatory Heat and Eroding Trust

The situation has moved beyond mere consumer frustration to formal government scrutiny. The Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC), the nation’s antitrust regulator, has begun cracking down on these platforms for unfair contract terms, misleading discount timers, and deceptive promotional language.

Furthermore, the platforms’ handling of consumer complaints—such as refusing refunds or forcing credits onto users—has alienated the South Korean market. In a country with high expectations for both product quality and customer service, the platforms' "growth at all costs" mentality has backfired. By prioritizing scale over safety and transparency, these companies have transformed themselves from market disruptors into symbols of broken trust. As the KFTC enforces stricter oversight, the days of these platforms operating in a regulatory blind spot are officially over.
