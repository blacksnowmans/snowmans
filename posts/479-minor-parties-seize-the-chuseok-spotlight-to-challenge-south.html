Minor Parties Seize the Chuseok Spotlight to Challenge South Korea's Two-Party Monopoly

September 30, 2026

    Leaders from three South Korean minor progressive parties appeared together on a special political broadcast ahead of the Chuseok autumn harvest festival to diagnose public sentiment.
  • Shin Jungsik of the Rebuilding Korea Party, Kim Jong-hun of the Progressive Party, and Han Chang-min of the Social Democratic Party criticized the major parties for prioritizing partisan warfare over working-class struggles.
  • The discussions focused on pressing issues including runaway economic inequality, deteriorating conditions for small business owners, and widespread fatigue with the ruling bloc.
  • The appearance highlights how minor parties are attempting to bypass traditional media gatekeepers to frame themselves as the true voice of marginalized citizens.

Challenging the Dual Monopoly

South Korean politics is notoriously dominated by two main camps: the conservative People Power Party and the liberal Democratic Party. For voters exhausted by polarized gridlock and endless investigations, minor parties offer an alternative, albeit one fighting on a heavily tilted playing field. This recent broadcast gave floor leaders from smaller factions a rare platform to speak directly about structural inequalities without the filter of mainstream party spin.

Each leader brought a distinct perspective rooted in their background. Shin Jungsik of the Rebuilding Korea Party framed his political survival around a relentless fight against economic disparity, positioning his party on the front lines of progressive reform. Kim Jong-hun of the Progressive Party drew on his roots in labor activism to argue that mainstream politics consistently erases working-class realities. Meanwhile, Han Chang-min of the Social Democratic Party announced he is stepping down as party leader to serve as a floor leader, prioritizing practical legislative results over holding onto symbolic titles.

The Chuseok Verdict on the Current Administration

Timing the broadcast just before the Chuseok holiday—a period when families gather and discuss politics over dinner—was strategic. The leaders reported that public sentiment gathered from traditional markets and workplaces was grim. Citizens are grappling with soaring living costs and stagnant prospects, while small business owners face mounting bankruptcy pressures.

According to the opposition figures, public frustration has moved past mere economic anxiety and hardened into a desire to hold the administration accountable. While major opposition parties sometimes hesitate or seek compromise, these minor factions are banking on a sharper, more confrontational message. Whether this rare alignment among smaller parties can break through the concrete walls of Yeouido and translate into tangible legislative pressure remains the central question for South Korea's political landscape.
