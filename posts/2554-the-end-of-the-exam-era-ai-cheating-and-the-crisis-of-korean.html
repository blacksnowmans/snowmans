The End of the Exam Era: AI Cheating and the Crisis of Korean Intelligence

November 20, 2026

The proliferation of real-time AI cheating in both national exams and corporate hiring is threatening to dismantle the meritocratic foundation of South Korean society.

  • Record Cheating: Authorities detected 98 cases of smart-device-assisted cheating during the most recent Suneung, the high-stakes national college entrance exam that determines a student's university and career path.
  • Commercialized Fraud: At least eight international firms have launched Korean-language services offering "AI cheating solutions" that provide real-time, GPT-driven answers for corporate aptitude tests and video interviews.
  • Intellectual Decay: Reports from the OECD and MIT warn that "productive struggle"—the difficult cognitive process required for deep learning—is being replaced by instant algorithmic answers, eroding long-term reasoning skills.
  • Administrative Pressure: The government of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party are under increasing pressure to overhaul an educational evaluation system that remains optimized for a pre-AI world.

The Death of the 'Productive Struggle'

For decades, the Suneung has been viewed as the ultimate test of endurance and memorization. However, the system is now facing a "terminal diagnosis." Critics argue the exam has devolved into a "technical skill" competition where students are trained like machines to identify patterns and eliminate wrong answers at high speed. As AI becomes capable of solving these problems instantly, the very nature of South Korean education is being hollowed out. The "productive struggle" described by researchers—the mental friction required to truly master a subject—is vanishing. When an algorithm provides a polished solution in seconds, the mental circuitry required for complex problem-solving fails to develop. There is a growing fear that within two decades, the country may lack a generation with the native intelligence required to even maintain the AI systems they currently use as crutches.

Corporate Vulnerability and the Fairness Myth

The crisis has moved beyond the classroom and into the job market, where the stakes are equally high. Global firms have launched specialized platforms that capture a candidate’s screen during remote exams and use AI to feed them answers via hidden pop-up windows. These services, which cost between 100,000 and 200,000 won (about $70 to $140), have turned corporate hiring into a pay-to-win game. Even video interviews are no longer secure; AI can now generate real-time scripts for candidates to read, and some applicants have even been caught using AI-integrated glasses. While Samsung, the country’s largest conglomerate, has implemented specific guidelines against such wearables, most other companies lack clear protocols.

In response to this systemic threat, the Ministry of Justice has announced a massive audit of the last five years of national bar exam essay responses to look for AI-generated patterns. However, these are retroactive, analog solutions to a digital-native problem. The real challenge for the current administration will be shifting the national evaluation paradigm away from "closed-book" memorization toward methods that prize critical inquiry and human-AI collaboration. Without a fundamental shift, the "fairness" that South Korean society prizes above all else will become a relic of the past.
