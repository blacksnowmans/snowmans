South Korea's Main Opposition Fractures Over Conservative Rebuilding and Street Protests

November 11, 2026

South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has plunged into bitter factional warfare over its future leadership even as it prepares to escalate street protests against the administration.

  • An influential pundit’s ultimatum: Veteran conservative commentator Cho Gap-je publicly declared that revitalizing the conservative movement requires keeping former leader Han Dong-hoon while purging party leader Jang Dong-hyeok, warning that clinging to conspiracy theories will guarantee election defeat.
  • Vicious internal infighting: Party leader Jang openly fired back at pro-Han lawmakers, telling them to "shut their light mouths" if they will not fight alongside leadership, amid party clashes over legislative agenda items like overseas electronic voting.
  • Drawing battle lines with the government: Floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik met with Choi In-ho, senior presidential secretary for political affairs, demanding dialogue while flatly refusing cooperation on key budget funds and special counsel legislation.
  • Taking the fight outside parliament: The People Power Party announced nationwide street rallies to demand a presidential veto on special counsel bills, kicking off demonstrations in Busan on November 14.

A Party at War With Itself

The bitter exchange between party elders, party leadership, and reform-minded factions highlights how little progress the People Power Party has made in healing its internal wounds. Cho Gap-je’s intervention struck a nerve precisely because it pointed out a recurring trap: turning inward and doubling down on conspiratorial, hard-right rhetoric. With key conservative figures—including Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, reformist lawmaker Lee Jun-seok, Han Dong-hoon, and Jang Dong-hyeok—jockeying for positioning, talk of a broader conservative realignment remains hollow. Without confronting internal accountability for past crises, such as the December 3 martial law debacle, calls for unity amount to little more than a scramble for factional dominance.

Maximalist Opposition Without a Vision

The party's two-track strategy—professing an openness to dialogue while taking to the streets—looks less like a coherent plan and more like political drift. Floor leader Jeong’s warning that respecting the opposition means respecting half the electorate is a standard parliamentary playbook, but pairing budget obstructionism with street rallies risks alienating moderate voters who are weary of perpetual confrontation.

By resorting to nationwide rallies in strongholds like Busan instead of building effective policy alternatives in parliament, the People Power Party appears stuck in protest mode. Unless conservative leadership offers a constructive vision that moves beyond relitigating old battles, its bid to re-establish itself as a viable governing alternative will continue to fall flat.
