South Korea's Ruling Party Hits Post-Transition Low as Internal Feuds Split Voters

November 16, 2026

According to a November 2026 survey by South Korean pollster Realmeter, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has fallen to its lowest approval rating since the change of government, even as President Lee Jae-myung's personal approval ticked slightly upward.

  • A growing rival: Realmeter's weekly poll for the second week of November showed the Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller reform-oriented party, climbing from 6.9% to 8.7% and closing in on 10% as frustrated voters look elsewhere.
  • An ethics referral triggers turmoil: On November 15, DPK leader Kim Min-seok referred Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee to the party's ethics tribunal over leaked remarks claiming President Lee had opposed impeaching Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was removed from office and is now imprisoned—in order to attract centrist voters.
  • Factional warfare goes public: Choi apologized and blamed a memory slip, yet demanded answers over Kim's earlier allegations of a secret alliance involving party dissidents and the Shincheonji religious group, sparking meeting boycotts and expulsion calls between rival factions, Yonhap News TV, YTN, and the Kyunghyang Shinmun reported.
  • Friction with the judiciary: Meanwhile, tensions with Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae have sharpened, as liberal commentator Kim Ou-joon and Rebuilding Korea Party lawmakers accuse Cho of delaying a recusal case involving former Minister Choi Sang-mok—charges Cho and some legal experts reject as unconstitutional interference.

Core Supporters Question the Party's Direction

The divergence between President Lee's slight rebound and the DPK's slide suggests that voter anger is aimed squarely at how the party manages itself, rather than at the presidential office alone. On his widely watched YouTube broadcast, Kim Ou-joon—alongside some second-term DPK lawmakers—attributed the slump to core backers disillusioned over stalled prosecution reform, government appointments, and a "pragmatism" line perceived as pushing loyalists aside.

From the outside, the handling of the Choi Min-hee dispute looks like a self-inflicted crisis. Even senior DPK figures argued that leadership could have settled the matter politically after Choi's apology instead of escalating it into a formal disciplinary battle. Both major camps share this weakness: South Korea's main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP) under leader Jang Dong-hyeok, is likewise mired in lawsuits and disputes over lawmaker expulsions, deepening public fatigue.

Judicial Stand-offs Open Space for a Rival

This internal paralysis carries a clear political cost for the DPK by ceding the reform spotlight to the Rebuilding Korea Party. While the DPK chose in late October to pursue a parliamentary inquiry into issues including the Supreme Court's 2025 election law ruling on Lee and 2026 justice nominations, the Rebuilding Korea Party unveiled a second impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho. Cho testified in October that legislative involvement in judicial nominations is unconstitutional, and legal observers remain split on the court's actions. Yet as long as the DPK stays consumed by infighting, its smaller rival appears poised to absorb restless reform-minded voters.
