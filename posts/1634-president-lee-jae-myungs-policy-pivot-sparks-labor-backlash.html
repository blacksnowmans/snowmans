President Lee Jae-myung’s Policy Pivot Sparks Labor Backlash and Diplomatic Friction

October 28, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is facing significant internal and external pressure as its push for semiconductor industry exemptions clashes with core labor promises and controversial remarks regarding the economic value of foreign workers.

  • Labor Policy Conflict: The administration is seeking 52-hour work week exemptions for researchers in semiconductor "mega clusters," a move that critics say betrays President Lee’s campaign pledge to implement a 4.5-day work week.
  • Foreign Worker Controversy: President Lee sparked a backlash after suggesting foreign workers do not "greatly" contribute to the domestic economy due to remittances; however, data shows they spend approximately 43% of their wages within South Korea.
  • Opposition "Fake News": Representative Joo Jin-woo of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, faces potential legal penalties for spreading false claims regarding a luxury golf outing involving Kim Keon-hee, the wife of the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • Judicial Scrutiny: A judge presiding over a case involving Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has been accused of "distorting the law" after an AI-assisted review of the ruling allegedly found nine pages of logical errors.

A Progressive President’s Industrial Pragmatism

The pivot on labor hours highlights a growing tension between President Lee’s progressive identity and the pragmatic demands of the global technology race. Lee, who often references his past as a child factory worker to bolster his pro-labor credentials, is now championing "White Collar Exemptions" to help Korean firms compete with Chinese rivals. This shift has placed Labor Minister Kim Young-hoon, a veteran labor activist, in a difficult position. If the administration bypasses social consensus to force these exemptions through executive decrees—a tactic Lee previously criticized during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—it risks a permanent rift with its own political base.

Furthermore, the administration’s struggle with "ping-pong administration" is stalling regional projects. In Gwangju, the relocation of a dual-use military and civilian airport is stuck in bureaucratic limbo. Neither the Ministry of Foreign Affairs nor the Ministry of National Defense has taken the lead in necessary negotiations with U.S. Forces Korea regarding military facilities. This lack of a "top-down" approach from the Blue House suggests that despite the administration's strong rhetoric, internal coordination on complex local-international issues remains a bottleneck.

Media Friction and Judicial Integrity

The political atmosphere remains thick with distrust, extending to the judiciary and the press. The reporting of a judge for "law distortion" reflects a growing public demand for accountability within the courts, moving away from the tradition of treating judicial rulings as beyond reproach. Simultaneously, the administration’s relationship with the media is fraying. President Lee recently took the unusual step of publicly calling out the Hankyoreh, a major daily newspaper, for what he deemed a misleading headline regarding his foreign worker remarks.

This sensitivity to narrative control is mirrored by concerns at KBS, the national public broadcaster. Reports that KBS President Park Sang-beom frequently replaced his mobile phone during critical political shifts—including the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—have fueled suspicions of evidence destruction. For international investors and observers, these developments suggest that while the leadership has changed, the underlying institutional conflicts between the presidency, the judiciary, and the media continue to define the Korean political landscape.
