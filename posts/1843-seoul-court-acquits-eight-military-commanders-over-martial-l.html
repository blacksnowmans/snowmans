Seoul Court Acquits Eight Military Commanders Over Martial Law Deployments

November 02, 2026

A Seoul court has acquitted all eight military officers and field commanders charged with insurrection for their roles in the short-lived martial law attempt, accepting their argument that they were merely following orders without recognizing that the decree was unconstitutional.

  • What happened: The Seoul Central District Court cleared eight former military brass—including participants in an ad-hoc coordination session at a Lotteria fast-food outlet and commanders who sent armed troops to surround the National Assembly, the National Election Commission, and independent broadcast outlet News Factory.
  • The legal logic: Presiding Judge Jeong Su-young ruled that prosecutors failed to prove the defendants harbored a specific "intent to subvert the Constitution," deciding they lacked both prior knowledge and adequate time to evaluate whether superior orders were illegal.
  • The ripple effect: Legal analysts and civic critics warn the ruling establishes a hazardous precedent by reviving the "Nuremberg defense"—allowing high-ranking generals and colonels to escape criminal liability by claiming ignorance of basic constitutional law.
  • The political stakes: The verdict complicates the ongoing prosecution of key figures behind the coup attempt, including Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment and ouster, while intensifying progressive demands for sweeping judicial reform.

A Dangerous Precedent for Military Obedience

The court's acceptance of the commanders' plea strains credulity. These were not low-ranking conscripts thrust into chaos; they were senior officers, including brigade and battalion commanders, who held overnight planning sessions before deploying armed troops to paralyze South Korea's parliament and election watchdog. To argue that top-tier military planners could not discern the illegality of storming democratic institutions gives legal sanction to an engineered "ignorance strategy." Under this standard, any future military subversion can be shielded from domestic treason laws simply by claiming reflexive chain-of-command compliance.

The acquittals also cloud the broader legal accountability for the martial law bid. If the tactical commanders executing the orders bear zero criminal culpability, establishing the institutional boundaries of insurrection becomes far harder. The ruling leaves the public with a baffling paradox: a militarized assault on constitutional bodies occurred, yet the officers who directed the boots on the ground are deemed entirely blameless.

Judicial Friction in the Post-Yoon Era

Politically, the decision marks a sharp escalation in tensions between the courts and the ruling progressive bloc under President Lee Jae-myung. Critics point to the conservative tenure of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, alleging that the judiciary is actively insulating the conservative military establishment and retreating to defensive formalism to avoid rocking the boat.

With public outrage mounting, the Democratic Party of Korea is likely to treat this verdict as a catalyst for direct intervention. Expect lawmakers to push for parliamentary investigations and potentially weigh impeachment proceedings against judges viewed as shielding insurrectionists. Far from closing the chapter on the martial law crisis, the court's clean sweep of acquittals has shifted the battleground into an outright conflict between parliament and the bench.
