How Different Cultures Say Hello, Share Space, and Hide Behind Silence

October 01, 2026

  • Panels from the US, Turkey, Senegal, and Rwanda compared everyday social habits, revealing how deeply language reflects a society's boundaries and attitudes toward strangers.
  • Turkish and American speakers highlighted the hidden friction of learning Korean: mastering tricky nasal syllable endings and decoding indirect, round-about greetings.
  • Global participants debated the fine line between Korean "jeong" (affection) and cultural overreach—such as intrusive personal questions and elevator silence—compared with foreign hospitality norms like Senegal's "Teranga" or American small talk.

Language is rarely just a tool for exchanging information; it is a physical and psychological blueprint of how a society manages personal space and collective anxiety. When people from diverse cultures sit down to compare how they greet strangers, ask after the sick, or ride an elevator, the quirky peculiarities of each society float to the surface.

For non-Koreans stepping into local life, mastering the language means more than memorizing vocabulary—it requires adopting an entirely different set of physical habits and social armor. From the subtle art of "noonchi" (reading the room) to the aggressive warmth of older generations asking personal questions, everyday interactions in Korea often feel like a complex codebook to outsiders.

Ultimately, these cultural clashes show that no single way of relating to others is objectively correct. Whether it is the efficient directness found in Rwanda or the suffocating yet comforting closeness of a tight-knit community, every language preserves the survival strategies of the people who speak it.
