South Korea's Indie Animation Defies the Odds with 'Gills'

October 04, 2026

Veteran animation director Ahn Jae-yoon is returning with Gills, a striking new indie feature that sets aside gritty local poverty in favor of an alien European backdrop, backed by an all-star voice cast including top actors Jung Hae-in and Kang Ha-neul.

  • What happened:
    •  Director Ahn Jae-yoon—head of the studio Meditation with a Pencil—has adapted Gu Byeong-mo’s acclaimed novel Gills into an animated feature, setting the story in Paris, Venice, and Italian towns rather than the slums of Korea.
  • Star-studded backing:
    •  A-list actors Jung Hae-in and Kang Ha-neul volunteered to voice the characters, drawn by Ahn's artistic integrity and a shared desire to support South Korea's struggling indie animation sector.
  • What it changes:
    •  By shifting the setting abroad, the film avoids poverty-porn clichés and zeroes in on universal themes of isolation, transforming a character's physical wound into a metaphor for survival.
  • Who is affected:
    •  Independent animators facing a harsh domestic market, art-house cinema audiences, and viewers looking for introspective storytelling over blockbuster spectacles.

A Rare Voice in a Neglected Industry

South Korea's content boom has earned the prefix "K-" for everything from dramas to pop music, but animation remains a notable exception. Without the industrial scaffolding of Japan’s anime industry, Korean animators like Ahn fight an uphill battle. Working through his studio, Ahn has spent decades balancing commercial projects with literary adaptations like The Shower, striving to build a homegrown animation history from the ground up.

What makes Gills remarkable is not just its aesthetic ambition—moving its narrative to the melancholic streets of Paris to heighten the protagonist's profound loneliness—but the industry solidarity behind it. When top actors like Jung Hae-in and Kang Ha-neul step into an indie recording booth for scale wages, it highlights a desperate need to shield independent creators from total obscurity. Ahn's work reminds us that animation in Korea is driven by stubborn passion rather than institutional support.

More Than Just a Movie

In a crowded landscape dominated by Hollywood blockbusters and festival darlings, Gills demands a different kind of viewing. Ahn has stated his wish that audiences leave the theater not to debate film theory, but to write their own stories. The film treats trauma and scars not as things to be surgically removed, but as the very "gills" that allow us to navigate a harsh world.

As the film hits select South Korean art-house theaters—from Seoul's IndieSpace and Artnine to regional screens like Incheon's Cinema Space Juan—it stands as a quiet triumph for an overlooked medium. It is an invitation to look past the box-office metrics and recognize the profound, lonely labor it takes to keep South Korea's animated dreams breathing.
