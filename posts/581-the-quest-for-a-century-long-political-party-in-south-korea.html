The Quest for a "Century-Long" Political Party in South Korea

September 10, 2026

South Korean political commentators and activists are currently debating what it takes to build a "century-long" political party, arguing that the nation’s political landscape requires a fundamental shift from self-serving power struggles to an altruistic focus on citizen needs.

  • What happened: The "Century Committee," a forum led by prominent liberal commentator Kim Ou-soo, recently hosted discussions on political sustainability, joined by veteran novelist Cho Jung-rae and several scholars.
  • The core issue: Critics argue that mainstream media and political incumbents remain entrenched in manipulative practices—such as inflating survey data—to protect their own interests.
  • The shift: There is a growing movement of "highly awakened" citizens who are self-organizing to hold power accountable, moving beyond traditional partisan fan-club behavior.
  • The goal: The forum proposed that for a party to survive 100 years, it must evolve into a "philosophical umbrella" that protects human life in an era of rapid AI and economic instability.

Media Manipulation and the Struggle for Reality

The credibility of South Korean media came under fire during the session. The panel highlighted a recent headline from the Chosun Ilbo, South Korea’s most influential conservative newspaper, which claimed that a majority of police officers supported the prosecution’s retention of investigative powers. However, subsequent investigation revealed the study was based on a sample size of only eight people. Critics at the forum used this as a stark example of how media outlets manipulate public opinion, effectively laundering the views of a tiny minority to appear as the consensus of a 130,000-strong police force.

Meanwhile, acclaimed author Cho Jung-rae (known for his epic historical novels like Taebaek Mountains) introduced his latest work, Frost Flower. Cho, 84, revealed that he wrote the book despite suffering from a major abdominal surgery that required him to endure a 30-centimeter incision, underscoring his belief that literature must expose how the collusion between capital and legal authorities destroys the lives of ordinary people. His work serves as a literary protest against a society where love and human dignity are commodified by unchecked greed.

Can Politics Become Altruistic?

The discussion shifted to the sustainability of political parties in an era dominated by rapid technological change. Scholars like Park Koo-yong noted that while science is advancing at an exponential rate, South Korea’s democratic systems have actually regressed over the past decade, trapped in outdated power games. They argued that a party capable of lasting a century cannot be a mere interest group chasing the next election; it must be a force that provides solutions for the future, including the ethical challenges posed by AI and structural inequality.

The panel concluded that the "century-long" party is not created by political engineering, but by "altruistic courage." If political parties continue to prioritize their own seat counts and influence, they will inevitably face public backlash. The consensus was clear: in a time when trust in traditional politicians is at an all-time low, the burden of governance is shifting toward highly active, self-organizing citizens who demand that their leaders act as true public servants rather than agents of the status quo.
