Inside South Korea’s Divorce Consulting Boom: Money, Silence, and Modern Marriages

October 10, 2026

South Korean divorce lawyer Yang Narae's blunt breakdowns of marital breakdowns on YouTube offer a stark look at how modern relationships fracture over two persistent friction points: money and communication.

    What happened: High-profile legal commentary on YouTube channels hosted by South Korean comedians Song Eun-i and Kim Ssook has spotlighted the gritty realities of marital disputes, from hidden debt to digital infidelities. What it changes: Couples are increasingly relying on banking apps to audit partner finances before marriage, treating matrimony as a calculated economic merger rather than just a romantic bond. Who is affected: Young South Koreans preparing for marriage, long-term spouses dealing with silent alienation, and pet owners navigating custody grey areas.

The Economics of Marriage and the Death of Conversation

South Korean courts view marriage as an economic partnership, but transparency remains elusive. Yang warns that hiding debts before walking down the aisle is a ticking time bomb. With prospective spouses even plugging partner salaries and apartment prices into ChatGPT to vet compatibility, financial pragmatism has reached a sterile extreme. Yet, legal protections for everyday budgeting within a marriage are weak, meaning voluntary cooperation is the only shield against distrust.

Beyond finances, the silence between long-term partners often proves fatal. South Korean divorce inquiries spike predictably after major holidays like Chuseok (Korean Thanksgiving), when couples are forced into intense proximity, revealing simmering resentments. Under South Korea's strict "fault-based divorce" system, simply finding a partner unresponsive or emotionally dead is rarely enough to legally dissolve a marriage if the other party objects—leaving couples trapped in cold, communicative voids.

Where the Law Draws the Line on Infidelity and Custody

Infidelity remains the ultimate trust-destroyer, though a massive gap exists between emotional betrayal and legal proof. Daily chat messages or evening phone calls with a colleague do not legally constitute an affair unless explicit romantic intent or physical meetings are proven. Meanwhile, petty digital behaviors—like a wife liking an ex-partner's dog account—highlight a dangerous disrespect for boundaries, even if they stay on the safe side of the law.

When relationships finally collapse, disputes bleed into modern lifestyle assets, such as pets. In South Korean law, pets are still classified as property, meaning ownership often defaults to whoever paid the adoption fees or holds the official registration—though practical enforcement remains nearly impossible if an ex-partner vanishes with the animal. Ultimately, as Yang's counselling sessions show, surviving modern relationships requires cutting off the environments where temptation breeds long before lawyers ever have to get involved.
