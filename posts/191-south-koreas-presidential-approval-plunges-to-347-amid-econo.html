South Korea's Presidential Approval Plunges to 34.7% Amid Economic Pain and Internal Feuding

September 05, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is facing a sharp political crisis as its approval rating sinks to 34.7%, driven by mounting economic anxiety among voters in their 30s and 40s and growing backlash over ruling party rule changes.

  • Plunging Support: President Lee’s approval rating has dropped to 34.7%, with negative evaluations reaching 61.9% in the administration's second year.
  • Generational Backlash: Disapproval among voters in their 30s and 40s has spiked to 70% and 66.6% respectively, fueled by soaring housing costs, volatile stock markets, and stagnant household budgets.
  • Internal Controversy: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's primary liberal political party, faces criticism for rewriting party rules and by-laws to tighten control over candidate nominations and sideline internal dissent.
  • Political Fallout: Analysts and critics view the numbers as a direct rebuke of the administration's failure to address everyday economic struggles and a loss of procedural legitimacy.

The Root of the Backlash: Livelihoods and Legitimacy

The core of the administration's current vulnerability lies in the daily financial strain weighing down South Korea's economic backbone. Voters in their 30s and 40s—traditionally the most economically active demographic—are feeling the squeeze of an unattainable real estate market and volatile asset values. When a government fails to secure basic economic stability, its moral authority evaporates quickly. An administration that once promised to serve as an instrument of history is now being judged on whether ordinary citizens can afford next month's rent.

Compounding these economic woes is a self-inflicted political crisis within the Democratic Party. Leadership has moved aggressively to consolidate control over candidate selections by altering internal regulations and utilizing ethics watchdogs against internal critics. This heavy-handed approach has alienated core supporters and raised serious questions about the party's commitment to internal democracy. Observers note that when a government isolates itself and demands absolute compliance rather than genuine communication, it risks rapid political isolation.

A Narrow Window for Reform

An approval rating in the mid-30s during a president's second year serves as a flashing red warning light, historically mirroring the deep troubles faced by past administrations. Rebuilding broken trust requires far more than superficial public relations campaigns or cosmetic policy shifts. As economic pressures mount and public disillusionment deepens, the administration faces a critical golden time to abandon top-down authoritarian tendencies, restore internal party pluralism, and refocus its energy entirely on the bruised livelihoods of everyday South Koreans.
