South Korea’s Ruling Party in Turmoil as Infighting and Conspiracy Claims Erupt

November 11, 2026

A bitter feud has exploded within South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party, as veteran politician Song Young-gil launched a dramatic attack alleging that a fringe religious group systematically infiltrated party primaries, threatening to plunge the progressive camp into an existential crisis.

  • What happened: Former Democratic Party leader Song Young-gil abruptly escalated accusations that members of the Shincheonji religious sect manipulated party membership rolls to distort primary election results, clashing with the leadership of party chief Kim Min-seok.
  • What it changes: The public spectacle has fractured the ruling party’s unity, invited attacks from the conservative opposition, and sparked legal challenges from rank-and-file members questioning the legitimacy of party leadership.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung’s administration faces collateral damage as ruling party infighting drags down approval ratings, while ordinary party members and voters in key strongholds like Honam express deep alienation.

The Politics of Desperation and the "Shincheonji" Conspiracy

Song’s aggressive turn—pivoting from a moderate stance to declaring "a decisive war against illegal political interference under the guise of religion"—comes on the heels of devastating polling numbers. In recent surveys, Song registered a dismal 3.1% support rating in automated response polls for future presidential contenders, leaving his political career hanging by a thread. Facing near-total irrelevance after past legal troubles and the arbitrary dissolution of his minor splinter party, Song appears to be wielding the "Shincheonji frame" as a high-stakes noise marketing strategy to claw back the spotlight.

Yet, the allegations rely heavily on hearsay and unverified tips rather than hard evidence. Crucially, critics point out that cross-referencing private party membership lists with outside religious rosters risks severe violations of South Korea's Personal Information Protection Act and the Political Parties Act. By baselessly casting tens of thousands of ordinary members under a cloud of suspicion, the party risks painting itself as compromised from within—handing a precious weapon to the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, which is already demanding a special counsel investigation.

Grassroots Revolt and a Dangerously Distracted Government

The fallout has triggered an unprecedented grassroots backlash. Over 60% of Democratic Party supporters in recent surveys are demanding that party leader Kim Min-seok issue a formal apology and take responsibility for the chaos. Infuriated members, particularly from the party's heartland in the Honam region, feel insulted by implications that regional voting patterns were the result of a religious cult's infiltration rather than genuine democratic participation. In response, angry party members have launched injunction lawsuits to suspend Kim’s leadership, while grassroots coalitions push for the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae to force through long-delayed judicial reforms.

For President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, this self-inflicted civil war couldn't come at a worse time. With presidential approval ratings slipping amid broader economic headwinds, the ruling party should be rallying behind the executive agenda. Instead, veteran figures are treating internal factional warfare as a zero-sum game, prioritizing personal survival over public service. Unless the Democratic Party leadership drops its obsession with political maneuvering and disciplines those trading in ungrounded conspiracy theories, the ruling camp risks squandering its mandate from the very voters who put them in power.
