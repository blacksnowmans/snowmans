South Korea’s Markets Rebound as AI Chip Prices Double

November 02, 2026

South Korea’s benchmark index and its semiconductor leaders defied heavy foreign selling on Monday, buoyed by a domestic institutional "buy-the-dip" rally and surging export prices for AI-ready chips.

  • Market Resilience: The KOSPI, South Korea’s main stock index, surged 2.24% as domestic institutions snapped up undervalued shares in electronics and automotive sectors, offsetting a massive sell-off by foreign investors.
  • Export Quality over Quantity: October semiconductor exports reached $56.4 billion; while the total volume of chips shipped fell 3.1% year-on-year, the export value nearly doubled due to the high price of advanced AI memory.
  • Regulatory "Naming and Shaming": The Korea Exchange flagged 151 companies—including giants like E-mart and Lotte Shopping—for failing to provide "Value-up" plans to address their chronically low stock valuations.
  • The 2026 Super Cycle: Analysts anticipate a major supply bottleneck in 2026 as global AI infrastructure demand outpaces the production capacity of high-end memory equipment.

The Paradox of Foreign Selling

For years, the conventional wisdom in Seoul has been that the market only rises when foreigners buy. Monday’s session broke that rule. Despite a "selling bomb" from global funds, domestic securities firms and private equity funds stepped in as a bulwark. This shift suggests that local professional investors now view the recent dip as a valuation floor, regardless of the mechanical rebalancing often performed by global passive funds.

Foreign outflows are frequently driven by technical portfolio limits rather than a loss of faith in Korean fundamentals. When a specific stock’s value rises too quickly within a global index, funds are often forced to trim their holdings to stay within mandate. Savvy domestic players are capitalizing on this technicality, especially as the Democratic Party government under President Lee Jae-myung continues to push for corporate governance reforms to protect minority shareholders.

Semiconductors: Pricing Power is the New Growth

The latest data from the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy reveals a critical shift in the semiconductor landscape. While the market worried about a "peak-out" in chip demand, the actual numbers show that Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (the world’s two largest memory makers) have seized unprecedented pricing power. They are producing fewer chips but charging twice as much for them, thanks to the explosion of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) and enterprise-grade solid-state drives (eSSDs).

This pricing surge is a direct result of the AI arms race between global tech giants. While Google struggles with "AI cannibalization" of its search ads, Meta has seen a 26% jump in ad revenue by integrating AI into its recommendation engines. This success provides Meta with the capital—and the motivation—to keep buying Korean chips at any price. Analysts now point to 2026 as the likely peak of this cycle, as the lead times for specialized manufacturing equipment from firms like ASML and Applied Materials mean that new supply cannot hit the market until then.

The Infrastructure Bottleneck

However, a new risk is emerging from the physical world: power and water. In the United States, local opposition to AI data centers is growing due to soaring electricity costs and water usage. With more than half of U.S. states seeing political candidates campaign on halting data center construction, the pace of AI expansion may slow. For Korea, this infrastructure bottleneck in the West may actually prolong the current high-price environment by preventing a sudden glut of new data center capacity.

Meanwhile, the "Value-up" initiative remains a double-edged sword for the market. By publicly shaming 151 firms with low Price-to-Book (PBR) ratios—some trading as low as 0.15 times their book value—regulators are forcing a choice: reform or face permanent irrelevance. For investors, this list serves as a roadmap of "traps" to avoid or "gems" that may finally be forced to reward shareholders under government pressure.
