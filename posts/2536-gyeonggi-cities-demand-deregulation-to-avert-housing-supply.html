Gyeonggi Cities Demand Deregulation to Avert Housing Supply Crisis

November 20, 2026

Mayors across Gyeonggi Province, the populous region surrounding Seoul, are calling on the central government to slash administrative red tape to prevent a looming housing supply collapse in the capital area.

  • Accelerating 104,000 Homes: 24 local leaders met with Prime Minister Han Seong-suk to coordinate the timely delivery of over 100,000 housing units, warning that procedural delays are the biggest threat to supply.
  • Cutting Lead Times: Cities like Suwon and Bucheon requested "integrated reviews"—a system to merge overlapping bureaucratic checks—aiming to shave up to four years off the typical decade-long redevelopment process.
  • Infrastructure Demands: Gimpo and other northern cities emphasized "transport first, move-in later," demanding that projects like the extension of Seoul Subway Line 5 be finalized before new residents arrive.
  • Market Polarization: While major builders like Hanwha E&C are landing massive contracts, such as a $770 million (1.07 trillion won) project in Gwangmyeong, rising costs are leaving less profitable sites without any bidders.

The Procedural Bottleneck

The plea from Gyeonggi mayors highlights a desperate reality: urban decay in Seoul’s satellite cities is outstripping the government’s ability to approve new construction. Currently, a single redevelopment project can be stalled for years by separate reviews for environmental impact, education, and transportation. By the time a project clears these hurdles, the original financial estimates are often obsolete. The mayors are urging the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—to move beyond merely announcing supply targets and instead dismantle the "administrative wall" that keeps those homes on paper.

Specific pain points include the Sanbon New Town in Gunpo, one of the original planned cities from the 1990s. Local leaders there are demanding higher floor area ratios (FAR)—which allow for taller buildings—arguing that without them, the "contribution fees" required from current residents will be so high that the projects will fail. This sentiment is echoed across the province, where mayors warn that promised housing will remain a "mirage" unless the central government grants local authorities more power to bypass rigid zoning laws.

The Construction Cost Paradox

Even if the government streamlines the law—as it recently did by lowering the resident consent threshold for apartment remodeling from 75% to 70%—a new crisis is emerging: the "construction cost shock." While Hanwha E&C celebrated a record-breaking year with a cumulative $1.8 billion (2.5 trillion won) in urban orders, many other sites are being abandoned. In Songpa, a prime district in southern Seoul, a major redevelopment bid recently failed because no construction companies were willing to risk the rising costs of labor and materials.

For the average resident or international investor, the takeaway is that deregulation is no longer a silver bullet. The market is splitting in two: "premium" sites where builders are willing to fight for contracts, and "marginal" sites in the outer suburbs where even government support cannot bridge the gap between soaring costs and what residents can afford to pay. As the People Power Party, the main conservative opposition, continues to critique the government’s handling of the economy, the success of these Gyeonggi projects will likely determine the political and economic temperature of the capital region heading into next year.
