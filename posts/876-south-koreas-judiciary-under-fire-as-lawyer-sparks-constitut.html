South Korea's Judiciary Under Fire as Lawyer Sparks Constitutional Row Over Supreme Court Appointments

October 09, 2026

  • South Korean lawyer Kim Kyung-ho dropped a bombshell at a parliamentary audit of the Supreme Court, accusing Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae of undermining the constitution by claiming absolute control over Supreme Court appointments.
  • The clash exposes a bitter power struggle over the interpretation of constitutional checks and balances, pitting the judiciary's traditional autonomy against democratic accountability.
  • Critics argue that treating the Chief Justice's power to nominate justices as absolute—while refusing to resubmit candidates rejected by the executive—amounts to a dereliction of constitutional duty and an abuse of privilege.

South Korea's legal and political establishment was rocked late at night during a parliamentary audit when Kim Kyung-ho, a lawyer and honorary professor, challenged the leadership of the judiciary. At the center of the storm is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, who defended his refusal to renominate rejected candidates by asserting that neither the president nor the parliament has any constitutional right to interfere with his nomination power. The confrontation has laid bare deep-seated tensions over the separation of powers and the legacy of authoritarian constitutional drafting.

The core of the dispute lies in a fundamental disagreement over democratic legitimacy and the nature of the Chief Justice's role. While the president, currently from the Democratic Party, and the National Assembly derive their authority directly from universal suffrage under the 1987 democratic constitution, the Chief Justice's legitimacy is strictly indirect. Critics argue that treating judicial appointments as an untouchable, absolute prerogative echoes the authoritarian era of the Yushin Constitution, when dictatorial regimes used the judiciary as a tool for control rather than an independent arbiter of justice.

Challenging Judicial Elitism and Accountability

The controversy goes far beyond a procedural disagreement over personnel files; it strikes at the heart of public trust in South Korea's legal system. Chief Justice Cho's defense relies on drawing a parallel to the president's constitutional veto power over legislation. However, legal scholars and critics point out a false equivalence: while a presidential veto protects the core of democratic representation against legislative overreach, appointing Supreme Court justices is an administrative collaborative process requiring alignment among the Chief Justice, the president, and parliament.

For an outside observer, the episode highlights the insular culture of South Korea's legal elite, where institutional self-preservation often eclipses constitutional fidelity. As calls for comprehensive judicial reform grow louder—fueled by public frustration over perceived impunity and special privileges within the court system—this late-night confrontation signals that the judiciary's self-governing sanctuary will face intense, sustained political pressure.
