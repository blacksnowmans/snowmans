South Korea's Judiciary Under Fire: Prosecutors Seek 20 Years for Ex-Minister Over Martial Law

October 02, 2026

  • Prosecutors have demanded a 20-year prison sentence for Choo Kyung-ho, former finance minister and ruling party floor leader, for his role in facilitating the aborted December 2024 martial law attempt.
  • Meanwhile, former Prime Minister Han Duk-soo was acquitted on election law charges regarding a suspicious cash donation in Gwangju, fueling public anger over a perceived double standard in the courts.
  • Critics accuse the Supreme Court leadership of trying to oust independent judges through arbitrary performance reviews, deepening a crisis of public trust in South Korea's legal institutions.

A Judiciary Plagued by Political Bias and Executive Overreach

South Korea's legal system is facing intense public scrutiny following a wave of controversial rulings and internal disciplinary moves. In the martial law probe, prosecutors asked for two decades behind bars for Choo Kyung-ho, accusing him of using his parliamentary influence to block ruling party lawmakers from attending the crucial vote to lift the military decree. Despite the severe charges, Choo dismissed the trial as a political conspiracy, highlighting the defiant posture of figures linked to the short-lived emergency rule.

Conversely, former Prime Minister Han Duk-soo walked free from charges related to a $1,100 (1.5 million won) donation made during a visit to Gwangju while heavily tipped to run for president. The court ruled Han did not officially qualify as a "prospective candidate" at the time, a technicality that stunned observers who viewed the payout as a clear pre-election publicity stunt. This ruling, coupled with alleged administrative retaliation against a dissenting judge who previously ordered a prosecutor out of his courtroom, paints a picture of a judiciary increasingly bending to political winds under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

Political Noise and Radical Proposals for Reform

Away from the courtroom, political bickering and conspiracy theories continue to dominate the headlines. Political broker Myung Tae-kyun submitted a convoluted defense regarding unacknowledged opinion polls tied to Seoul Mayor Oh Sehun, while ruling party figures have traded insults over historical memory and cultural projects. This endless cycle of mudslinging has left everyday citizens battling profound political fatigue and a sense of powerlessness.

Amid this systemic disillusionment, some commentators are floating radical overhauls to South Korea's representative democracy—including a proposal to select lawmakers by random lottery rather than partisan elections to truly capture the public voice. While such ideas highlight the depth of public frustration with professional politicians, the immediate battle remains centered on defending judicial independence and demanding accountability from those in power.
