Why South Korea's Opposition Party Is Bleeding Support

September 21, 2026

  • Prominent political journalist Kang Ji-ho blamed South Korea's main opposition Democratic Party for its sliding approval ratings, pointing to internal purges rather than public policy.
  • A faction within the Democratic Party has pushed an "anti-Lee" framework to sideline party traditionalists under the banner of broadening political appeal.
  • Critics argue this strategy has alienated core supporters while failing to attract moderate voters, trapping the party in an echo chamber.
  • The commentary highlights the disconnect between professional politicians focused on inside-the-beltway disputes and everyday citizens worried about the economy and living costs.

Inside the Democratic Party's Purge Strategy

South Korean politics is currently consumed by an internal factional war within the Democratic Party. According to journalist Kang Ji-ho, a newly empowered faction has weaponized an "anti-Lee" narrative—ostensibly meant to widen the party's appeal to moderates—as a pretext to marginalize long-standing, loyal party members. By labeling critics as disloyal, party leaders have effectively silenced internal dissent and punished those who built the movement from the ground up.

This insular approach has exacted a heavy toll. While political operators obsess over micro-targeting and algorithmic echo chambers, public approval for party leadership has stagnated or declined. Core supporters, once fiercely protective of the party, have grown cold as leadership dismisses valid criticisms as factional betrayal. Instead of listening to grassroots voters struggling with housing and living expenses, party managers are fixated on policing loyalty and sidelining opposing voices.

The Broken Bond Between Politicians and the Public

The growing rift extends beyond party headquarters to the relationship between politicians and independent media. Broadcasters and independent commentators who once sustained politicians during election cycles are increasingly treated with hostility the moment they offer constructive criticism. Politicians who relied on grassroots funding and sympathetic media platforms often pivot to self-preservation, abandoning their supporters once they secure office.

This dynamic has left everyday citizens feeling deeply betrayed, fostering a widespread political cynicism. When media outlets and political parties insulate themselves from reality, they risk losing the very foundation of public trust. The takeaway is stark: sustainable political power cannot be maintained through manufactured loyalty or elite maneuvering. Without a return to honest engagement with the public and a tolerance for hard truths, political movements inevitably isolate themselves from the electorate they claim to serve.
