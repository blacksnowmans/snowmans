AI Cheating Scandal Shakes South Korea’s High-Stakes Exams

November 22, 2026

South Korea’s top financial regulator has filed a police complaint against the highest-scoring candidate on the national tax accountant exam over alleged AI-assisted cheating, exposing how easily wearable tech can bypass traditional test security.

  • Watchdog crackdown: The Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) reported the top scorer on the Certified Tax Accountant exam to police, while similar cheating suspicions have surfaced around the Certified Public Accountant exam, creating an early test for newly appointed National Police Agency Commissioner Kim Jong-cheol.
  • Extreme screening measures: Proctors at national licensing tests—including the security instructor exam—made candidates wear standardized aprons, while major universities deployed both aprons and metal detectors for essay admissions tests, yet authorities still caught and disqualified five to six additional cheaters.
  • College entrance turmoil: On the Suneung, South Korea’s national college entrance exam, a World History question with a 92.6% error rate triggered mass appeals after test-takers argued that even ChatGPT—which achieved only 34% accuracy on the item—selected the disputed answer.
  • Broader ethics scramble: Outside education, Korean church groups issued AI ethics guidelines after a survey showed 88.7% of pastors use AI for sermons or teaching materials, while UN talks to establish binding rules on autonomous "killer robots" by 2028 face resistance from the United States and Russia.

Why Aprons and Metal Detectors Won’t Hold

In South Korea, national licensing exams and university entrance tests are treated as vital guarantors of fairness and social mobility, making the sight of candidates lining up in aprons and passing through metal detectors especially jarring. Yet physical screening at the door is already a losing battle. Protests calling for tighter rules on AI smart glasses broke out near exam venues on the day of Anthropic’s IPO, and with AI contact lenses nearing commercialization, proctors cannot reliably spot hidden devices by sight alone.

What is breaking is not just exam security, but the underlying model of rote-memory testing. Experts cited in Korean media warn that memorization-based assessments cannot survive tools capable of feeding answers invisibly in real time. Even the Suneung dispute shows how deeply generative AI is reshaping public expectations of "correct" knowledge, turning classroom assignments into AI showcases and straining trust in grading systems.

A Governance Gap From Classrooms to Warfare

The same enforcement vacuum extends far beyond test halls. Whether it is AI-assisted "centaur hackers" exploiting software flaws in under three hours or major military powers dragging their feet on binding UN treaties for autonomous weapons, institutions are consistently lagging behind deployment. South Korean officials are now weighing technical coordination with AI developers alongside exam reform, because checking pockets at the door cannot fix an assessment system built for an offline world.
