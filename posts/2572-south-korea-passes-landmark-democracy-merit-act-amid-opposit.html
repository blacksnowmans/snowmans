South Korea Passes Landmark Democracy Merit Act Amid Opposition Boycott

November 21, 2026

On November 20, South Korea’s National Assembly passed the Democratic Merit Recognition Act, a long-contested bill aimed at honoring activists who fought against the country’s past authoritarian regimes, effectively reviving legislation that was blocked by a veto during the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

  • Legislative Victory: The bill passed with 178 votes in favor—all from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and its allies—following a 24-hour filibuster by the opposition.
  • Historical Recognition: It grants official "merit" status and support to 635 individuals, including iconic martyrs Bak Jong-cheol and Lee Han-yeol, whose deaths were catalysts for the 1987 June Democratic Struggle.
  • Support Framework: The law provides medical and elderly care support to activists and their bereaved families, ending nearly three decades of political deadlock.
  • Opposition Boycott: The conservative People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main opposition party, boycotted the final vote, labeling the act a "special privilege law" for the pro-democracy activist generation.

A Victory Decades in the Making

The passage of this bill marks the culmination of a struggle for legal recognition that has spanned 26 to 28 years, depending on the starting point of the various legislative iterations. For the ruling DPK, the move is a definitive step toward "restoring the honor" of those who paved the way for modern Korean democracy. The emotional weight of the day was evident in the National Assembly gallery, where family members of the activists were seen in tears as the final tally was announced.

By securing this law, the current administration and the DPK have successfully bypassed the roadblock set during the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who exercised his presidential veto against a similar version of the bill two years ago. The new law specifically targets those involved in major historical turning points, including the anti-Yushin protests in the Daegu-Gyeongbuk region and the 1987 June Struggle. While the DPK emphasizes that the law was refined through three separate revisions to address concerns about "self-awarded privileges," the political divide remains as sharp as ever.

The Debate Over "Special Treatment"

The opposition People Power Party remains steadfast in its criticism, framing the act as "welfare for the protest generation" and a "handover of privileges" to the descendants of activists. Their primary concern lies in the perceived ambiguity of the selection criteria. Conservative lawmakers have questioned whether the law could inadvertently honor individuals with records of violating the National Security Act, a Cold War-era law that remains a point of intense friction in Korean politics.

To mitigate public concerns, Kwon Oh-eul, the Minister of Patriots and Veterans Affairs, explicitly stated that the law does not include controversial benefits such as preferential college admissions for the children of merit recipients—a common point of contention in South Korea’s highly competitive education system. Instead, the focus is restricted to medical and nursing care for an aging cohort of activists. Minister Kwon has pledged to further supplement the law’s implementation guidelines to address the opposition's concerns regarding eligibility, though the PPP's total boycott suggests that reaching a cross-party consensus on the legacy of the protest era remains a distant goal.

Key Provisions of the Act

  • Scope: 635 individuals recognized for their contribution to democratization movements.
  • Benefits: State-funded medical services and access to government-run elderly care facilities.
  • Exclusions: Clarification that no special university admission quotas or employment "points" are granted to descendants under this specific act.
"This is not about special treatment; it is about finally fulfilling a state responsibility that has been delayed for nearly thirty years," a DPK spokesperson noted during the session.
