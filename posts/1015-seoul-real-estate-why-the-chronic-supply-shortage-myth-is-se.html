Seoul Real Estate: Why the "Chronic Supply Shortage" Myth Is Setting a Buyer Trap

October 12, 2026

Recent price upticks in select Seoul apartment complexes are re-igniting fears of missing out, but banking on a structural housing shortage to guarantee endless price appreciation ignores how quickly buyer demand can evaporate.

  • Localized rebounds are being mistaken for a market-wide rally: Price gains remain confined to specific pockets of Seoul, while major benchmark areas remain subdued and many who bought at the 2021 peak still sit on steep unrealized losses.
  • Supply does not dictate the floor: Physical housing supply is notoriously slow to build, but effective demand can collapse almost overnight under strict mortgage regulations, elevated interest rates, and exhausted household borrowing capacity.
  • The jeonse fallacy: The popular assumption that surging jeonse (South Korea's unique lump-sum lease deposit system) inevitably lifts sales prices contradicts market history, including the 2010–2014 downturn where lease deposits spiked while purchase prices tumbled.
  • The "end-user" blind spot: Retail buyers stretching credit to the limit—popularly dubbed yeong-kkeul, or "scraping together one's soul"—frequently rationalize speculative gambles as practical housing needs, leaving them without an exit strategy if values fall.

The Flawed Equation of Supply Shortages and Lease Spikes

Real estate bulls in South Korea often argue that because new housing delivery in Seoul cannot quickly meet demand, prices have nowhere to go but up. Yet supply-constrained markets like Sejong City, South Korea's administrative hub, have previously experienced severe downturns despite a lack of new construction. When prices outpace local purchasing power, transactions dry up, rendering physical scarcity irrelevant. Real demand is volatile and driven by sentiment and liquidity, both of which are currently constrained by tightening credit and persistent high borrowing costs.

Similarly misleading is the claim that higher jeonse deposits will mechanically pull property values upward. In a true bull run, home prices push lease deposits higher in a subordinate relationship. In a sluggish or declining market, however, purchase and rental markets act as substitutes: terrified buyers retreat into leasing, driving up jeonse deposits while property valuations continue to slide. Confusing this substitution effect for an impending purchase rally misreads historical cycles.

Herd Mentality and the Illusion of Safe Havens

The re-emergence of "buy now or never" headlines signals late-cycle herd psychology rather than healthy market fundamentals. When public discourse narrows entirely to property speculation, incoming liquidity is usually approaching exhaustion. The danger is amplified by buyers convincing themselves that purchasing for "actual residence" exempts them from market risk. In reality, taking on excessive leverage without a clear downside plan turns supposed family security into an unhedged speculative bet.
