South Korea's Political Turmoil: Insurrection Probe, Supreme Court Scandals, and Public Backlash

October 05, 2026

  • Overwhelming support for an open-ended insurrection probe: Recent polls show strong public backing—ranging from 56.1% to nearly 70%—for an unlimited special investigation unit to thoroughly uncover the truth behind recent constitutional crises.
  • Public rejects Supreme Court autonomy: 55% of respondents believe the president should negotiate and rename Supreme Court justice nominees rather than accepting the chief justice's unilateral picks.
  • Scandal hits the judiciary's top tier: Chief Justice Cho Hee-de faces intense public backlash over allegations of falsifying meal expense reports—allegedly splitting expensive 160,000-won (about $115) dining bills across multiple fake entries over 25 times.
  • Political deflection tactics: Conservative politician Han Dong-hoon has drawn sharp criticism for invoking former President Roh Moo-hyun to attack the legacy of prosecution reform and distract from mounting legal liabilities.

South Korea’s political and judicial systems are currently locked in a cycle of deep institutional rot, where the public's demand for basic accountability clashes head-on with entrenched political self-preservation and judicial arrogance. Recent polling makes one thing crystal clear: ordinary citizens are thoroughly exhausted by systemic cover-ups. Whether it is the demand for an indefinite special investigation into insurrection-related offenses or the outright rejection of how the judiciary handles its own affairs, public sentiment has moved past partisan divides to focus purely on the survival of rule of law.

At the center of this storm is a judiciary that has lost its moral compass. When the head of the Supreme Court is credibly accused of using state budgets to quietly expense luxury meals through doctored paperwork—behavior that would instantly ruin a lower-tier civil servant—it signals a rot at the very top. Paired with conservative politicians attempting to rewrite history and manufacture political distractions using the memory of former presidents, these events expose a ruling class desperately trying to evade legal reckoning. Unless these institutions face structural overhauls, such as dismantling the opaque National Court Administration, public trust in South Korea’s democracy will remain on shaky ground.
