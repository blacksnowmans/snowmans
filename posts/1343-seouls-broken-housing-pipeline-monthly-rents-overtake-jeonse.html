Seoul’s Broken Housing Pipeline: Monthly Rents Overtake Jeonse for First Time

October 20, 2026

A catastrophic 50% plunge in new apartment completions has pushed Seoul's rental market past a historic milestone, with monthly rents outnumbering traditional lump-sum jeonse leases for the first time in South Korean history.

  • Supply halved: Seoul saw only 17,000 new apartments delivered between January and July 2026, down from 34,000 in the same period in 2025—the delayed result of financing freezes and stalled construction starts three years ago during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure.
  • Historic lease reversal: Monthly rental contracts (wolse) hit 8,700 in July 2026, surpassing jeonse (8,600) for the first time on record as soaring deposit requirements forced cash-strapped tenants into costly monthly payments.
  • Tenants frozen in place: Contract renewals accounted for 51.3% of all lease transactions in August, with roughly 60% of renewing tenants accepting steep, uncapped market hikes simply to avoid being displaced into an empty market.
  • Suburban ripple effect: Severe rental shortages and rigid purchasing rules in the capital have pushed priced-out renters into surrounding Gyeonggi Province, driving sharp price jumps in commuter hubs such as Bundang and Suwon.

The Three-Year Echo of Inaction

South Korea’s residential development cycle creates a predictable three-year lag between groundbreaking and move-in day. Today’s supply drought is the inevitable fallout from 2022 and 2023, when soaring construction costs and a credit crunch paralyzed housing starts under Mr. Yoon's administration. While Seoul groundbreakings rebounded 36% year-on-year to 15,000 units in the first seven months of 2026, that volume barely matches today’s depressed completion figures, signaling that the supply famine will persist through at least 2029.

Regulatory distortions have exacerbated the shortage. Frameworks like Land Transaction Permission Zones—which mandate immediate owner-occupancy on purchases—were designed to curb speculation, but instead choked off private rental supply. By preventing buyers from leasing out newly acquired properties and locking existing tenants in place, the policy simultaneously closed all three avenues of rental liquidity: newly built units, existing turnover, and private investor supply.

Market Shifts and the Window for Buyers

This supply squeeze is altering buying behavior across the metropolitan area. Prospective buyers are flocking to properties priced under 1.5 billion won (about $1.1 million), where state mortgage limits allow up to 600 million won in borrowing. Faced with landlords demanding tens of thousands of dollars in additional deposits or doubling monthly fees, many renters calculate that paying monthly mortgage interest on an entry-level home is now cheaper than remaining in the rental market.

In response to the gridlock, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport has relaxed owner-occupancy rules, allowing qualified first-time buyers until December 31, 2027, to purchase tenant-occupied properties in restricted zones and defer moving in until lease terms expire—potentially as late as 2029. While this opens a window to acquire tenanted units at modest discounts, it offers cold comfort to the millions of tenants absorbing punishing rent spikes in a market stripped of new homes.
