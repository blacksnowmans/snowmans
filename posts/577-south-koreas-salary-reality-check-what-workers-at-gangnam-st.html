South Korea’s Salary Reality Check: What Workers at Gangnam Station Really Earn

October 02, 2026

  • A popular YouTube street-interview video in Seoul's bustling Gangnam district reveals the actual take-home pay, working conditions, and financial anxieties across various South Korean jobs.
  • While corporate bankers and lawyers pull in high salaries near or above $75,000 to $110,000 (100 million won), they face punishing workloads, strict licensing exams, and grueling 70-hour workweeks.
  • Meanwhile, younger generations are reshaping career strategies—balancing grueling civil service exams, trading IT hubs for traditional heavy industry giants, and even saving $75,000 by working arcade part-time jobs at age 23.

A recent street-interview video filmed in the heart of Gangnam Station—one of Seoul’s busiest commercial hubs—has struck a chord by stripping away the secrecy surrounding South Korean paychecks. Featuring everyone from mid-level bank tellers and overworked attorneys to daycare teachers and industrious college students, the footage offers a raw look at what people actually earn and the heavy mental toll exacted in return.

The numbers behind elite Korean professions often mask harsh realities. A 31-year-old mid-level employee at Kookmin Bank—South Korea's largest commercial bank—earns roughly $65,000 to $70,000 (about 90 million won) after five years on the job, falling just short of the coveted six-figure mark. The compensation comes with endless internal testing, eight mandatory professional licenses, and the quiet irony that working in finance doesn't automatically translate to successful personal wealth building. Similarly, a third-year associate lawyer at a smaller South Korean law firm clears around $75,000, but pays for it with 70-hour workweeks handling emotionally draining domestic and criminal cases.

The Shift in Youth Ambition: From IT Monopolies to Traditional Industry

Beyond the high-earning professions, the street interviews capture a broader generational pivot in how young South Koreans view labor and financial survival. While tech giants like Naver and Kakao once dominated college graduates' wish lists, top students are increasingly pivoting toward traditional manufacturing stalwarts like Hyundai Motor, drawn by stability and structured career tracks. At the same time, others are rejecting corporate ladders entirely: one 23-year-old university student has quietly amassed $75,000 (100 million won) through disciplined savings while working night shifts at an arcade, fueled by the work ethic he observed in his parents.

Ultimately, the snapshot of Gangnam Station cuts through the social media veneer of instant wealth and crypto millionaires. Whether it is an experienced advertising designer jumping ships to double their salary or a tax-accountant hopeful enduring isolation for a shot at a stable career, the underlying message is clear: in South Korea's high-pressure economy, every won earned is tied directly to the heavy price of time, stress, and relentless adaptation.
