Why South Korea's Semiconductor Rout is Mostly Market Noise

September 07, 2026

Recent panic over US tariffs and export controls has dragged down South Korean tech heavyweights Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, but the underlying fundamentals of the memory chip market remain intensely bullish.

  • What happened: US regulatory warnings, tariff rhetoric, and the removal of South Korean factories in China from "Validated End-User" (VEU) lists have triggered widespread sell-offs among retail investors.
  • What it changes: Operating Chinese-based production lines will involve more bureaucratic friction and individual permit applications, adding short-term complexity for Samsung and SK Hynix.
  • Who is affected: Global AI infrastructure developers relying on South Korean high-bandwidth memory (HBM), and retail investors caught in the current market downdraft.

Separating Washington's Rhetoric from Chip Realities

US trade pressure—including threats of tariffs and equipment restrictions—is best understood as a political tool designed to encourage manufacturing reshoring rather than an actual attempt to cripple South Korea's semiconductor industry. Just as previous disputes in the power grid sector ultimately resulted in US importers absorbing costs rather than foreign suppliers, Washington cannot realistically bypass South Korean high-performance memory without grinding its own AI ambitions to a halt. Even though export controls and VEU exclusions complicate fab operations in China, global hyperscalers simply lack viable alternatives for cutting-edge AI memory.

Nomura and other global investment banks currently point to severe undervaluation, with major memory makers trading at price-to-earnings ratios near 3. As long-term supply contracts now make up over 40% of sales, structural stability has replaced the volatile consumer-cycle swings of the past. Major tech capital expenditure remains locked in, and upcoming technological catalysts like Nvidia's Blackwell chips and HBM4 rollout mean the current discount window is likely temporary.

Managing Exposure and Looking Toward December

While corporate share buybacks have temporarily caused supply indigestion as foreign funds offload old positions, the broader structural trend is upward. Investors should adjust semiconductor portfolio weightings from extreme highs down to around 40% to manage volatility, while keeping an eye on software and AI-inference beneficiaries. With export numbers hitting record highs and AI hardware transitioning from training to heavy inference phases, the fundamentals strongly favor a rebound heading into late autumn and December.
