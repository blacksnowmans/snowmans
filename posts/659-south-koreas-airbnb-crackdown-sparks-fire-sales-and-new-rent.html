South Korea's Airbnb Crackdown Sparks Fire-Sales and New Rental Hacks

September 11, 2026

  • South Korea's temporary exemption allowing Airbnbs in buildings over 30 years old is set to expire on December 31, 2027, triggering a wave of panicked property dumping by owners.
  • Surging foreign tourist numbers—projected to pass 18.5 million—have crashed against strict zoning laws, exposing major data flaws in government supply forecasts.
  • Savvy investors are pivoting from traditional Airbnb hosting to platforms like Enkostay, a short-term rental service catering to South Korea's growing population of foreign exchange and university students.

The 2027 Airbnb Cliff and the Government Data Mess

For years, South Korea maintained a strict ban on operating short-term rentals in buildings older than 30 years or in semi-basement units, citing liability concerns over safety accidents involving foreign tourists. But as the K-pop boom drove international arrivals to a record-breaking 17.4 million between January and November alone, traditional hotels could no longer handle the wave. In response, the government introduced a provisional waiver in October 2025 to permit short-term rentals in older buildings—with one major catch: the exemption expires entirely on December 31, 2027.

This sunset clause has turned older rental units into ticking time bombs, freezing the market and causing nervous owners to dump properties at steep discounts. Meanwhile, government incompetence has compounded the chaos. A recent audit by the Board of Audit and Inspection revealed that the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism relied on deeply flawed statistics from the Seoul Metropolitan Government, which mistakenly calculated business establishment growth rates instead of actual room capacity. While bureaucrats drafted reports predicting a market oversupply, auditors warn that Seoul faces a severe hotel room shortage as incoming tourist numbers surge toward 19.1 million.

Enter Enkostay: Why Smart Landlords Are Ditching Airbnb

With traditional short-term rentals suffocating under red tape and impending deadlines, operators are finding loopholes through alternative platforms. Enkostay, a short-term rental platform designed specifically for foreign exchange and international students—a demographic the government aims to grow from 160,000 to 300,000 by 2027—has emerged as a primary escape hatch.

Unlike Airbnb, which requires cumbersome resident registration filings to secure business licenses, alternative short-term rentals can be listed immediately upon signing a lease. The operational differences are stark. Airbnb demands relentless, exhausting daily turnovers of linens and deep cleaning after every one- or two-night stay, alongside expensive aesthetic upgrades to satisfy vacationers. In contrast, targeting students for two- to six-month blocks allows landlords to manage properties remotely—even from another country—using smart doorlocks and automated cleaning services. While per-night revenues on Airbnb may look higher on paper, the scalable, systemic nature of medium-term rentals offers a far more sustainable business model amid tightening regulations.

The Bottom Line on South Korea's Evolving Rental Market

South Korean real estate has always been a game of rapid regulatory shifts, where panic and policy collide to create sudden wealth transfers. While retail investors dump properties at a loss ahead of the 2027 rule changes, entrepreneurial operators are negotiating bargain leases and leveraging operator networks to keep occupancy high. The takeaway is clear: as long as government bodies bungle housing statistics and clamp down on traditional short-term rentals, rigid compliance is a dead end. Success in South Korea's rental market belongs to those who look past regulatory fear and build automated, data-driven systems around real demographic demand.
