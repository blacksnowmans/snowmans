Physical AI: South Korea’s New Industrial Frontier

November 03, 2026

South Korea is pivoting toward "Physical AI"—the next generation of artificial intelligence that moves beyond screens to physically operate robots, vehicles, and factory machinery—viewing it as the essential solution to the nation’s shrinking workforce.

  • What happened: Industrial giants, led by Samsung, are committing massive capital to build "Physical AI clusters" that integrate software intelligence with robotic hardware.
  • What it changes: AI is evolving from a "digital brain" that generates text or images into a physical labor force capable of navigation, manufacturing precision, and hazardous task management.
  • Who is affected: South Korean manufacturers, regional industrial hubs, and global tech players are racing to secure leadership in hardware-integrated AI and "spatial intelligence."

The Shift from Virtual Intelligence to Physical Labor

For years, AI has been confined to the screen, excelling at writing, coding, and generating imagery. However, these models lacked a physical body to interact with the real world. Physical AI bridges this gap by marrying AI software with sensors, motors, and articulated joints. This is not merely a technological upgrade but a response to South Korea’s demographic crisis. With a rapidly aging population and a diminishing labor pool, robots equipped with spatial intelligence—the ability to understand, measure, and navigate three-dimensional environments—are now seen as the only sustainable path forward for the nation's manufacturing backbone.

Samsung is at the forefront of this movement, spearheading a massive 60 trillion won (about $43 billion) investment into a Physical AI cluster. This ambitious project aims to unify the entire development cycle, from training ultra-large AI models to mass-producing the hardware robots will eventually inhabit. To support this, Samsung SDS has begun construction on an AI data center in Gumi, which will serve as the primary hub for processing the vast amounts of spatial data generated by factory floors and robotic simulations.

Global Competition and Regional Ambitions

While South Korea builds its infrastructure, it faces stiff competition, particularly from China, which has increased its investment in humanoid robotics fivefold. Companies like Unitree are rapidly commercializing robots that can perform human tasks, threatening the market share of major global players like Tesla. In response, domestic firms like Polaris AI are forming strategic partnerships with global entities such as China’s UBTECH to accelerate commercialization.

Regional governments are also competing to become the domestic testing ground for this technology. From North Jeolla Province to Ulsan, local authorities are lobbying for state funding to establish "extreme environment" testing sites, where robots can be stress-tested for dangerous jobs like firefighting or heavy industrial work.

The transition is clear: the race for Physical AI is no longer about who can write the best code, but about who can best bridge the gap between intelligent software and physical execution. Under the current administration, the focus is on creating a national synergy where massive corporate investments, local infrastructure, and a robust regulatory framework for AI ethics align to ensure South Korea remains a global leader in the robotics era.
