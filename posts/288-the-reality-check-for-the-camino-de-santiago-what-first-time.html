The Reality Check for the Camino de Santiago: What First-Timers Get Wrong

September 07, 2026

  • Record crowds: More than 530,000 pilgrims tackled the Camino de Santiago, the historic network of walking trails across Europe leading to the shrine of apostle Saint James in Spain, setting an all-time record.
  • Route shift: While the traditional French Way remains the most popular route, the Portuguese Way now captures over a third of all trekkers.
  • Budget reality: A 23-day trek on a budget costs about $860 (1.15 million won) excluding flights, defying myths that the journey requires thousands of dollars.
  • The core rule: Pack light, ditch rigid travel companions, and expect cramped, snorer-filled hostels.

Social media feeds are flooded with romanticized snapshots of the Camino de Santiago—golden sunsets, profound personal epiphanies, and endless joy. The reality on the dusty gravel paths, however, is a relentless 30-kilometer daily grind, wet shoes, and the symphony of strangers snoring in crowded dormitories. As record numbers crowd the trails, surviving the trek requires less poetic daydreaming and a lot more logistical discipline.

Veterans of the trail emphasize that choosing the right path is the first critical test. While starting the Portuguese Way from Lisbon sounds ambitious, it subjects beginners to endless highway walking and poor infrastructure; most experienced walkers start further north in Porto. Similarly, packing heavy is the fastest way to end up in a medical clinic. Ideal pack weight should hover around 4 to 5 kilograms, a stark contrast to the grueling 15.6-kilogram loads many novices mistakenly haul across the Iberian Peninsula.

Hostels, Apps, and Ditching the Itinerary

Forget the luxury resort mindset. Accommodation along the route relies primarily on albergues—pilgrim-specific dormitories operating on a first-come, first-served basis without reservation systems. Sanitation is a gamble, and bedbugs are an occupational hazard. Pilgrims navigate these hurdles using real-time navigation tools like the Buen Camino app and the Gronze website, skipping rigid bookings to maintain the flexibility to stop when injuries flare up or weather turns sour.

Ultimately, the appeal of the Camino isn't about ticking off a bucket-list achievement or finding a magic cure for life's problems. In an hyper-connected world obsessed with efficiency, the primitive act of putting one foot in front of the other offers a rare, radical simplicity. The trail won't rewrite your life overnight, but stripping your routine down to a heavy pack and an empty road is a powerful antidote to modern exhaustion.
