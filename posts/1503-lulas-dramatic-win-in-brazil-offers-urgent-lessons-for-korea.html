Lula’s Dramatic Win in Brazil Offers Urgent Lessons for Korea’s Ruling Progressives

October 26, 2026

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s narrow victory over far-right incumbent Jair Bolsonaro has become a crucial case study for South Korea's progressive camp on how to defeat reactionary populism and sustain power through tangible economic relief.

  • The turnaround: Lula overcame a razor-thin 3.5 percentage point margin from the first round to clinch the presidency amid Brazil's 81% mandatory-voting turnout, surmounting last-minute voter suppression efforts targeting transit in low-income districts.
  • A tale of two diasporas: Brazilian expatriates living in South Korea voted decisively for Lula at 64.35% (148 out of 230 ballots), while those in Japan backed Bolsonaro by an overwhelming 80.08% (28,538 out of 35,636 ballots).
  • The policy engine: Tangible bread-and-butter commitments—namely a 15% expansion of the Bolsa Família welfare subsidy and Senate passage of a five-day workweek—proved decisive in galvanizing the working-class base.
  • The Seoul takeaway: Progressive commentators argue that Lula’s post-victory warning—asking why progressive administrations so frequently crumble after taking power—serves as an urgent blueprint for South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

Diaspora Divides and Democratic Culture

The astonishing divergence between Brazilian voters in Seoul and Tokyo highlights how host countries' political climates permeate expatriate communities. Korean observers view the pro-Lula landslide among local Brazilians as a reflection of South Korea's civic atmosphere, where the legacy of mass public protests and active defense of democratic institutions shaped expatriate sensibilities. In contrast, the vote in Japan—where voters backed the far-right by a wide margin—is seen by analysts as an outcome of living within a conservative-dominated media landscape under decades of rule by Japan’s Liberal Democratic Party.

Sustaining Progressive Power in Seoul

For South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea, Brazil's election carries immediate domestic significance. Having reclaimed power following the acute democratic strains and governance crises of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s term, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces the reality that public antipathy toward the conservative opposition will not guarantee long-term stability.

Lula’s return proves that progressive coalitions falter when they compromise their core identity for vague moderation or fail to improve everyday lives. The lesson being drawn in Seoul is uncompromising: to resist a resurgent right, the government must avoid internal factionalism and aggressively pass transformative legislation—delivering the same direct, pocketbook relief that carried Brazil’s working class across the finish line.
