South Korea's Conservative Fracture and the Global Rise of the Far-Right

October 27, 2026

South Korea's conservative opposition is descending into infighting over election strategy while far-right groups face mounting legal pressure at home and surging populist movements threaten democratic norms worldwide.

    Internal feud in the Reform Party: Lawmaker Chun Ha-ram has openly rejected leadership demands to relocate his constituency to southern Gyeonggi Province, insisting he will honor his pledge to run in Suncheon instead. Conservative leadership under fire: People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative opposition) leader Chang Dong-hyuk is leaning hard into traditionalist messaging, but his survival strategy faces deep skepticism over potential alliances with other conservative factions. Far-right legal scrutiny: Figures associated with historical revisionism and anti-comfort-woman rhetoric, notably Kim Byeong-heon, are facing police investigations over alleged illegal political donations. Global populist parallels: From Marine Le Pen's anti-immigrant platform in France to election denialism in Brazil, far-right movements are increasingly testing democratic systems across the globe.

Cracks in the Opposition and the Limits of Conservative Unity

The public clash within the Reform Party—a smaller conservative splinter group—highlights a wider identity crisis plaguing South Korea's right wing. As the party struggles with stagnant approval ratings, leadership attempts to parachute high-profile figures into competitive districts are colliding with individual lawmakers' political survival instincts. Rather than projecting a unified alternative to President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, the party's squabbling reveals a lack of strategic cohesion.

Meanwhile, People Power Party leader Chang Dong-hyuk is attempting to consolidate power by signaling party orthodoxy, recently rallying his leadership team at a memorial for former authoritarian leader Park Chung-hee. Yet talk of a grand conservative coalition involving figures like former party leader Han Dong-hoon and Reform Party chief Lee Jun-seok remains fraught with cynicism. Critics argue these discussions are driven by bare-knuckle electoral math rather than shared principles, leaving voters unconvinced that the opposition offers a genuine alternative.

Extremism at Home and a Warning Light Abroad

Beyond institutional politics, the boundaries of acceptable public discourse are being tested by radical groups. Police are currently investigating far-right activists accused of gathering illegal donations while pushing inflammatory historical narratives. Coupled with persistent, baseless conspiracy theories regarding election integrity pushed by fringe YouTubers and sympathizers, these movements pose a steady threat to public trust in democratic institutions.

This domestic radicalization mirrors a troubling international trend. In Europe, figures like France's Marine Le Pen are leveraging economic anxiety to normalize exclusionary nationalist agendas, while post-election denialism continues to destabilize democracies from Brazil to Israel. Across these disparate cases, far-right factions share a common playbook: weaponizing societal grievances while treating democratic rules as negotiable conveniences. For South Korea's mainstream conservatives, drawing a hard line against extremism is no longer just a tactical choice—it is a baseline requirement for the survival of constitutional democracy.
