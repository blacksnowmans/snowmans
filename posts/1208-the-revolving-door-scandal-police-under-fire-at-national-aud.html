The "Revolving Door" Scandal: Police Under Fire at National Audit

October 16, 2026

South Korean police are facing intense scrutiny following revelations at the National Assembly’s audit, where lawmakers accused the force of bowing to "revolving door" corruption—a practice where former high-ranking officials use their status and connections to benefit clients in cases they once oversaw.

  • The Core Issue: Lawmakers criticized the National Police Agency for failing to investigate Lee Jong-geun, a former senior prosecutor (Director of the Criminal Department at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office), who allegedly represented clients involved in the "V Global" cryptocurrency fraud case—a massive scheme he previously supervised.
  • The Police Response: The Seoul Suseo Police Station closed the case in February without filing charges, labeling it an "investigation prior to indictment" (an internal review that stops short of a formal probe).
  • Political Pushback: Representative Chae Hyun-il of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the party of President Lee Jae-myung) slammed the police for their "lenient" handling of the case, demanding a full, transparent reinvestigation.
  • Broader Impact: The scandal has fueled public distrust, as many victims of the V Global scheme—which defrauded tens of thousands of people of billions of won (potentially hundreds of millions of dollars)—feel the justice system is shielding the very "legal experts" who helped exploit them.

A Test of Institutional Integrity

The controversy centers on whether the South Korean justice system remains captive to its old guard. During the audit, Woo Jong-soo, head of the National Office of Investigation (the agency overseeing police investigations nationwide), struggled to justify why the police cleared Lee of violating the Attorney-at-Law Act. His vague explanations—citing "technical" interpretations of when the lawyer was hired—did little to satisfy critics who see this as a classic case of former high-ranking officials using their influence to evade accountability.

For many, this is a painful reminder of the era of the impeached former leader, Yoon Seok-youl, whose administration was often criticized for deep-seated ties between law enforcement and the legal elite. While President Lee Jae-myung has campaigned on cleaning up these "cartels," this case suggests that remnants of the old "prosecutor-first" culture remain embedded within the police’s investigative processes.

What Comes Next

The case has now been referred to the Major Crimes Investigation Agency (a specialized body tasked with probing corruption in high-level offices). This shift represents a significant embarrassment for the police, who effectively abdicated their duty to investigate their own potential systemic bias. The outcome of this probe will serve as a bellwether for whether the current administration can truly dismantle the influence of former power brokers.

Ultimately, the public is watching to see if "justice" is a reality or just a talking point. If the police continue to hide behind bureaucratic technicalities, they risk losing the trust of a generation that is already deeply cynical about the fairness of a system that appears to favor the elite while leaving victims to bear the brunt of financial ruin.
