South Korea’s First-Generation New Towns Face Deepening Real Estate Polarization

November 10, 2026

Real estate activity across South Korea’s first-generation satellite cities has split sharply, with affordable outer districts seeing transaction surges under strict mortgage rules while premium hubs maintain record prices despite falling sales volumes.

  • Sales volume doubles in affordable outer towns: Home purchases in Sanbon jumped 108% as end-users, squeezed by tighter loan restrictions, shifted away from high-priced districts toward lower-cost satellite areas like Sanbon and Jungdong.
  • Prices stay high in prime satellite hubs: Apartment prices in Bundang surged 35% even as transaction volume fell 41%, supported by strong local infrastructure and proximity to major job centers like Pangyo.
  • Seoul shifts approval powers to accelerate supply: Licensing authority for 213 urban redevelopment projects across Seoul is being delegated to local municipal district heads, paired with streamlined one-stop approval efforts in districts like Dongjak.
  • Mega-contracts move ahead amid tax hurdles: Construction giant Samsung C&T secured a ₩2.3 trillion (about $1.7 billion) reconstruction project in Mokdong, even as heavy charges under the excess profit recapture system—such as a ₩220 million (about $165,000) per-owner assessment in Banpo—threaten project momentum.

Regulation and Job Proximity Drive Market Division

South Korea’s housing market is reacting unevenly to regulatory pressure and credit constraints under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. Rather than moving as a single block, the country's first-generation satellite cities—built in the early 1990s to ease Seoul's housing shortage—are splitting along structural lines. Sanbon and Jungdong have turned into practical targets for young first-time buyers seeking primary residences without taking on unmanageable debt. In contrast, districts like Ilsan continue to lag due to a lack of local corporate headquarters, demonstrating how vital self-sustaining employment hubs are to long-term property values.

Meanwhile, high-demand areas like Bundang and Pyeongchon show persistent price resilience despite dropping sales volume. Buyers remain willing to pay high prices for top-tier infrastructure and short commutes, though price gains without trading volume raise concerns over market sustainability. Outside the Seoul metropolitan area, regional cities like Daejeon are also pushing for urban renewal, actively demanding tax relief from central regulators to keep their local projects viable.

Administrative Fast-Tracking Meets Rising Cost Bottlenecks

To tackle ongoing urban housing shortages, local authorities are trying to dismantle bureaucratic delays. Dongjak District in southern Seoul recently consolidated administrative consultations from 21 separate departments into a single reconstruction department. Expanding on this approach, Seoul plans to transfer approval powers for 213 redevelopment sites directly to local district chiefs, aiming to shorten timeline delays and reduce holding costs for property owners.

However, financial obstacles continue to slow down the pipeline. While major builders are returning to secure landmark contracts in prime neighborhoods like Mokdong, heavy regulatory charges remain a major flashpoint. The reinstatement of significant excess profit recapture levies—demonstrated by the ₩17.9 billion ($13.4 million) total tax bill handed to homeowners in Banpo—threatens to stall redevelopment economics. Combined with issues like Daegu's 77 stalled, unstarted construction sites, policymakers face a difficult task balancing anti-speculation measures with the urgent need for new housing supply.
