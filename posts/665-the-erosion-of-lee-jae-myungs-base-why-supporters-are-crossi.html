The Erosion of Lee Jae-myung’s Base: Why Supporters are Crossing Their Arms

October 04, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is facing a deepening rift with its core support base, as declining approval ratings spark intense debate over the government's direction—particularly regarding judicial reform.

  • The Friction: Tensions have peaked between the administration and prominent progressive voices, including the influential media outlet News Factory (hosted by Kim Ou-joon).
  • Media Access: Journalists, such as Park Hyun-kwang, have publicly complained of being marginalized and ostracized by the presidential office, labeling the atmosphere as "school-like bullying."
  • Disciplinary Action: The presidential office has suspended several journalists from press access, citing breaches of "embargoes" and the disclosure of private briefings, fueling accusations of silencing dissent.
  • Core Disillusionment: While the administration blames "disruptive" media influencers for declining polls, critics argue the base is alienated by the government’s retreat from promised structural reforms to the prosecution service.

A Crisis of Communication

The current turbulence is not merely about media personalities—it is a sign that the administration’s "pipeline" of communication with its own supporters has ruptured. Historically, the relationship between the South Korean government and the press has been fraught, but the current administration appears to be choosing isolation over engagement. By framing legitimate criticism as "fake news" or "uncomfortable noise," the presidential office risks alienating the very voters who brought them to power.

The conflict over judicial reform is central to this divide. Supporters who expected sweeping changes to the prosecution's structure view the current government's "pragmatic" compromises as a betrayal of their core mandate. When the administration dismisses these concerns as the result of "media incitement" rather than genuine policy disappointment, it commits a strategic error. It ignores the fact that the progressive base is not blindly obedient; they are observant, and they are now increasingly keeping their arms crossed in silent skepticism.

The "Stone in the Shoe"

Writer Rhyu Si-min, a prominent intellectual often associated with the progressive camp, has described the current predicament using the metaphor of a "stone in the shoe." The administration is focused on external culprits—blaming influencers and pundits for their political woes—while failing to acknowledge the sharp, structural pain being felt by their own constituents.

Whether this is a deliberate strategy by the so-called "New Lee Jae-myung" faction—the inner circle surrounding the President—or simply a reaction to the pressures of governance remains a subject of intense speculation. However, one thing is clear: treating loyalists as adversaries and critics as enemies is a path to political isolation. If the administration continues to prioritize the suppression of dissent over the fulfillment of its reformist promises, the "disappointment" currently cooling their approval ratings may soon harden into permanent opposition.
