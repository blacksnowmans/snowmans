Japanese Prime Minister Takaichi Faces Diplomatic Cold Shoulder in China

November 19, 2026

Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s first visit to China since taking office has been marred by a series of diplomatic slights, highlighting Japan’s diminishing influence in Northeast Asia amid shifting regional alliances.

  • Diplomatic Snub: Upon arriving in Shenzhen for the APEC summit, Prime Minister Takaichi was met with minimal protocol, starkly contrasting the elaborate welcomes afforded to other world leaders.
  • Stalled Relations: Takaichi failed to secure a formal bilateral meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping, with their interaction limited to a brief, cold handshake and a nod.
  • Regional Isolation: Japanese media have sharply criticized the administration, drawing unfavorable comparisons between Takaichi’s treatment and the warm receptions given to U.S. President Donald Trump and South Korean President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Domestic Fallout: Takaichi’s approval rating for her China policy has dropped to 44%, as internal friction over defense spending and skepticism regarding her diplomatic strategy grow.

A Test of Diplomacy or a Sign of Decline?

The "empty airport" reception in Shenzhen is widely viewed as a calculated signal from Beijing. In diplomatic circles, arrival protocol serves as a barometer for bilateral relations; by stripping away the usual pageantry, China has publicly underscored its dissatisfaction with the current Japanese administration. For Takaichi, whose political brand is built on a "strong Japan" and a hawkish stance toward China, the optics of being treated like a peripheral player are devastating.

The situation is made worse by the glaring contrast with South Korean President Lee Jae-myung. While Tokyo struggles to gain traction with Beijing, the Lee administration has successfully positioned itself as a pivotal broker in Northeast Asian security. The sentiment in Tokyo is one of growing anxiety, as Japan fears being sidelined in the "big deals" negotiated between the U.S., China, and the Korean Peninsula.

Internal Friction and Strategic Uncertainty

Beyond the humiliation on the world stage, Takaichi is fighting a war on two fronts. Her push to increase Japan’s defense budget—a move intended to project strength—is facing stiff resistance at home, both from fiscal hawks within the ruling Liberal Democratic Party and from industrial stakeholders concerned about the feasibility of such spending.

By attempting "gap diplomacy"—such as her brief, largely unproductive encounter with Russian President Vladimir Putin—Takaichi is clearly searching for any leverage to break her isolation. However, these sporadic meetings do not substitute for a coherent foreign policy. As it stands, Japan remains trapped in a cycle of domestic political conflict and international ambiguity, failing to reconcile its pacifist constitutional identity with its aspirations for military expansion. The APEC summit has served as a brutal reality check: without genuine dialogue and regional trust, Japan’s attempts to assert itself are only leading to further marginalization.
