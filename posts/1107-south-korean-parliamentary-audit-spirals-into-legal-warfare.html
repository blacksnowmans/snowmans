South Korean Parliamentary Audit Spirals into Legal Warfare and Golf Scandals

October 14, 2026

The second week of South Korea’s annual parliamentary audit has shifted from policy oversight to an all-out political war involving the judiciary, the presidency, and local government leadership.

  • The Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP), a minor liberal party, filed a criminal complaint against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae with the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO), alleging embezzlement and misuse of business expenses.
  • The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition party, proposed a "Special Counsel" bill to investigate President Lee Jae-myung over alleged luxury golf outings, dubbed "Emperor Golf."
  • Choo Mi-ae, the Governor of Gyeonggi Province (the country's most populous province) and a heavyweight in the ruling Democratic Party, faced a heated first audit where she was accused of prioritizing national political ambitions over provincial management.
  • A sobering report revealed that 180 out of 847 state-supported businesses in the "Gwangwang Dure" (regional tourism cooperatives) project have closed, highlighting a failure in government oversight of the local economy.

Judicial Independence Under Fire

The RKP’s decision to target Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae marks a significant escalation in the liberal bloc's friction with the judiciary. By alleging that the Chief Justice misappropriated "encouragement funds" for fellow justices, the party is effectively challenging the moral authority of the Supreme Court. While the ruling Democratic Party is weighing whether to join the call for his resignation, the PPP has labeled the move a "parliamentary tyranny" designed to intimidate the courts. This maneuver risks turning judicial oversight into a tool for political retribution, further eroding public trust in the country's legal institutions.

Personal Scandals Overpower Policy

The "Emperor Golf" controversy involving President Lee Jae-myung reflects the extreme polarization currently paralyzing the National Assembly. Lawmakers from both sides have traded lawsuits and vitriolic insults, with some telling colleagues that their "political lives will be short." This focus on personal conduct has come at a steep cost to the public interest. In the Gyeonggi provincial audit and the Ministry of Labor discussions, vital issues such as the "Basic Act on Workers' Rights" and the crisis of local business closures were buried under a deluge of partisan theater.

A Crisis of Local Governance

The most telling moment of the audit was the collapse of regional tourism projects. When government funding stopped, nearly 20% of these businesses folded immediately. This illustrates a chronic problem in Korean governance: a "funding-first" approach that lacks sustainable follow-up. While President Lee asserts a "sovereign" foreign policy to distance himself from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the domestic political landscape remains fragile. If the audit continues to prioritize viral political clips over the "bread and butter" issues of local shop owners and platform workers, the gap between the political class and the public will only continue to widen.
