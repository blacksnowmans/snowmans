South Korea's Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice

November 21, 2026

On November 20, South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party adopted the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae as its official party platform and pledged to pass the motion before the end of the year, according to broadcasters MBC News and Yonhap News TV.

  • The grounds: Kyunghyang Shinmun, Newsis, and Chosun Ilbo report that the Democratic Party included a Supreme Court decision sending President Lee Jae-myung’s legal case back to a lower court in its impeachment grounds, while also reviewing an overturned ruling involving former prime minister Han Duck-soo that Democratic Party Rep. Choo Mi-ae and civic group National Emergency Action claim minimized insurrection responsibility over the December 3 martial law crisis.
  • The opposition backlash: The People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition, condemned the push as "madness," a "national tragedy," and "retaliatory impeachment" that threatens judicial independence, launching evening street marches alongside conservative figure Han Dong-hoon, who called the move a "self-coup," according to Channel A, Yonhap News, and KBC.
  • Ruling-party dissent: The decision exposed divisions inside the Democratic Party; Kyunghyang Shinmun reported that Rep. Kwak Sang-eon publicly opposed the platform over how voters would view it, while JoongAng Ilbo noted internal fears of factional conflict and political backlash.
  • What changes: Moving forward in the National Assembly—where JTBC noted the ruling party holds strong legislative power—would put South Korea’s top judge on an unprecedented path toward a Constitutional Court trial, though Gyeongbuk Domin Ilbo warned that proving specific legal violations within a tight year-end deadline remains a major hurdle.

What this clash actually signals

Pursuing the impeachment of a sitting Supreme Court chief justice is uncharted territory in South Korean politics. While the Democratic Party frames the drive as holding the judiciary accountable for eroding public trust, broadcasters such as JTBC and SBS have raised a pointed question: why is a ruling party with firm control of parliament accelerating such a high-stakes confrontation right now?

The legal bar ahead is steep. In my view, turning controversial court decisions—including the rulings involving Lee and Han—into a viable case before the Constitutional Court requires proving concrete constitutional or statutory violations, not just political disagreement with a verdict. If that legal case looks thin, the push risks reinforcing the People Power Party's charge that the motion is partisan retaliation dressed up as accountability.

Politically, both camps are taking a calculated gamble. Kwak's public dissent shows that even within the ruling bloc, some lawmakers worry an aggressive timetable could alienate moderate voters. By taking its protest to the streets over "retaliatory impeachment," the People Power Party is betting it can rally conservatives around judicial independence—a standoff that looks set to freeze legislative politics through December.
