Internal Friction Grows in South Korea’s Ruling Party Over Loyalty and Dissent

November 27, 2026

Mounting internal conflicts inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) are exposing deep fractures over how much criticism of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration should be tolerated within party ranks.

  • What happened: Over the past five months, the DPK has been shaken by the forced resignation of party spokesperson Lee Ji-eun, unsubstantiated conspiracy claims aired during the August national convention, and controversial cabinet nominations on November 23.
  • What it changes: Party discipline is increasingly enforced through closed-door maneuverings and loyalty tests, marginalizing factional allies of past figures like former President Moon Jae-in and former Justice Minister Cho Kuk.
  • Who is affected: DPK lawmakers, party members, and moderate voters who worry that stifling internal debate will erode the ruling coalition's broad base of support.

Punishing Criticism and Shielding Insider Missteps

The rift became glaringly visible in June 2026, when DPK spokesperson Lee Ji-eun resigned following remarks made on a political broadcast. Warning against presidential intervention in selecting the next party leader, she cautioned against replicating habits seen during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former leader who was impeached and imprisoned. In response, pro-Lee supreme council members immediately demanded disciplinary action during a closed-door meeting, pushing Lee Ji-eun to step down despite efforts by party leadership to de-escalate the issue.

Conversely, high-profile party figures faced little public accountability for baseless attacks. During the August DPK national convention, Rep. Kim Min-seok alleged that anti-Lee forces had formed a secret alliance with the Shincheonji religious group. By November 3, party officials confirmed that an internal probe found zero evidence supporting the claim. However, rather than addressing party members publicly, Kim offered his apology in a private meeting of lawmakers, sparking anger among grassroots supporters who felt misled during the leadership vote.

Loyalty Tests and the Risk of Alienating Voters

Presidential appointments have further reinforced concerns over internal power dynamics. On November 23, President Lee Jae-myung nominated two-term lawmaker Han Jun-ho as Minister of Interior and Safety and presidential spokesperson Kang Yu-jung as Minister of Culture, Sports and Tourism. While supporters view these appointments as a routine effort to strengthen executive drive with trusted aides, critics argue it sends a clear signal: proximity to the president guarantees political advancement, while candid feedback carries heavy professional costs.

This dynamic poses a subtle but significant risk for the ruling party. Critics like Cho Kuk argue that the DPK is replacing constructive internal debate with unconditional loyalty. While suppressing dissent may preserve short-term political control for President Lee's government, it risks alienating core voter groups who feel increasingly excluded from the party's decision-making process.
