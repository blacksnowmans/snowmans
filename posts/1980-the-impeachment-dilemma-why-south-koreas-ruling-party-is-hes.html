The Impeachment Dilemma: Why South Korea's Ruling Party is Hesitating on the Chief Justice

November 05, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party is locked in a fierce internal debate over whether to launch a parliamentary investigation or move for the immediate impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

  • The Democratic Party (DPK), the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, is considering a formal probe into Chief Justice Cho over allegations of judicial bias and dereliction of duty.
  • Internal critics argue a parliamentary investigation is "useless" because the Chief Justice is likely to refuse to testify, citing the separation of powers and judicial independence.
  • Hardliners within the DPK are pushing for simultaneous impeachment, warning that appearing weak will alienate the party's base and repeat historical failures that led to political disaster for past liberal administrations.
  • The party leadership faces a self-imposed deadline of December 12 to finalize its strategy before the political momentum of the current session dissipates.

The Trap of "Practicality" vs. Political Will

Within the DPK, a divide has emerged between lawmakers with legal backgrounds and party activists. The legalists argue for "practicality," fearing that if an impeachment motion is eventually rejected by the Constitutional Court, the party will face a massive public backlash. They prefer a parliamentary probe as a more cautious first step. However, this cautious approach is being criticized as a sign of complacency. Since the removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment—the DPK has held a dominant position. Critics argue that using "legal practicality" as an excuse to avoid a fight makes the party look arrogant and out of touch with voters who demand systemic reform of the judiciary.

The current situation mirrors a painful chapter in Korean political history from 22 years ago. In 2004, the liberal predecessor of the DPK held a majority of 152 seats but hesitated on key reforms, such as the repeal of the National Security Act. That indecision led to a total collapse in support, with the party shrinking to just 81 seats by 2008. Hardliners argue that if the DPK fails to act decisively against the judicial leadership now, they risk the same fate in the 2028 general elections. They contend that a parliamentary probe should be used as a public stage to expose judicial misconduct, creating the necessary momentum for a full impeachment.

Deadlines and the Opposition's Distractions

The DPK's window for action is closing. If the issue is not settled by mid-December, it will likely be buried by the year-end news cycle, allowing the judiciary to escape scrutiny. Meanwhile, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition party, is dealing with its own scandals. Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent PPP figure, is currently facing allegations regarding improper funds and favoritism in city projects like the "Han River Bus." The DPK sees this as a moment to seize the initiative; by pushing a high-profile investigation into the Chief Justice, they can maintain pressure on the conservative establishment across both the judicial and local government branches.

The Road Ahead

Ultimately, the DPK must decide if it is a party of "accountants or leaders." If they spend too much time calculating approval ratings and legal risks, they may lose the "golden time" for judicial reform. To succeed, the party needs to present a united front. Some members have even suggested a "marathon debate" where lawmakers surrender their phones and stay in a room until a single, aggressive strategy—likely combining both the probe and impeachment—is finalized. In the volatile landscape of post-Yoon politics, the DPK cannot afford to be seen as the party that blinked first.
