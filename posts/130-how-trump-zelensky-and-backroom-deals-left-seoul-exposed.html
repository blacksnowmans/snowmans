How Trump, Zelensky, and Backroom Deals Left Seoul Exposed

September 29, 2026

  • US President Donald Trump is signaling unexpected tactical alignment with Beijing to stabilize soaring global oil prices near $100 a barrel ahead of his economic priorities.
  • Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy blindsided Seoul at the UN General Assembly by publicly revealing the transfer of captured North Korean soldiers to South Korea, violating a secret bilateral agreement.
  • South Korea faces mounting pressure to deliver massive U.S. investments without securing reciprocal concessions on nuclear cooperation or military autonomy.
  • Seoul must abandon naive value-based diplomacy and leverage its critical shipbuilding and capital power to extract hard concessions from Washington.

The Brutal Realities of Transactional Diplomacy

Recent diplomatic maneuvers expose a global stage driven entirely by leverage and self-interest rather than lofty values or ironclad alliances. Donald Trump’s pragmatic pivot toward Beijing is less about friendship and more about oil. With international crude hovering around $100 a barrel, Washington needs China's cooperation on energy demand to avoid a runaway inflation crisis that could threaten the U.S. economy and Trump's political standing.

Meanwhile, Ukraine's decision to air its secret repatriation deal with Seoul on the global UN stage underscores the desperation of wartime governance. Kyiv traded South Korea's trust for a theatrical performance aimed at securing Western sympathy and supplies, leaving Seoul's diplomatic planners empty-handed and furious. For South Korea, these betrayals serve as a harsh wake-up call: in a world of backroom deals, unrequited loyalty makes a nation a pushover.

Leveraging Seoul's Indispensable Industrial Edge

South Korea cannot afford to keep writing blank checks in the name of the U.S. alliance. As Washington pushes for trillions in foreign investments, Seoul's negotiators must tie these funds directly to vital strategic gains—such as revising the bilateral nuclear agreement to lift restrictions on spent fuel and naval propulsion technology. With the U.S. Navy struggling to maintain and build advanced fleets, South Korea's world-class shipbuilding capacity is an absolute necessity for Washington.

Seoul has every right to play hardball. Just as past administrations forced Washington's hand through sheer stubbornness and credible alternatives, today's leaders must be willing to push back against unfavorable terms. Blind compliance is not diplomacy; it is an invitation to be exploited.
