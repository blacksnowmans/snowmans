South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Chief Justice, Sparking Constitutional Crisis

November 21, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DP), the nation’s ruling party, has officially adopted the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae as a formal party platform, signaling an all-out confrontation between the legislature and the judiciary.

  • Official Party Line: On November 20, the DP formalized its intent to impeach the Chief Justice, aiming to pass the motion through the National Assembly before the end of the year.
  • Opposition Backlash: The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, has labeled the move an "institutional coup" and an act of "madness" aimed at subordinating the courts.
  • Immediate Suspension: If the motion passes the DP-controlled legislature, Chief Justice Cho’s powers will be suspended immediately, leaving the Supreme Court of Korea without a leader for months.
  • Constitutional Stakes: The move has triggered a fierce debate over the separation of powers, with critics warning that judicial independence is being sacrificed for political leverage.

The Drive for a "Year-End Finish"

The Democratic Party of Korea (DP), which supports the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, is moving at a breakneck pace to finalize the impeachment. By making the removal of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae an official party directive, the DP has ensured that its legislative majority will be mobilized for a floor vote. The party justifies the move by claiming Cho has violated his duty to uphold constitutional values and has failed to maintain the impartiality of the judicial branch. This is an extraordinary escalation; while the South Korean legislature has the power to impeach judges, targeting the head of the Supreme Court is a rare "nuclear option" that threatens to paralyze the country’s highest legal body.

The DP’s leadership has emphasized a "speed war," stating they want the motion reported and voted on as quickly as possible. This urgency suggests the ruling party wants to resolve the judicial friction before the new year, even at the risk of bypassing deep procedural vetting. For the DP, this is framed as a necessary "reform" to hold the judiciary accountable for perceived biases in recent high-profile rulings and administrative decisions.

"Palace Coup" and Judicial Independence

The opposition response has been scorched-earth. Han Dong-hoon, the former leader of the People Power Party (PPP), did not mince words, calling the impeachment drive a "palace coup" and an "insurrection against the republic." The PPP views this not as a legitimate check on power, but as a "revenge impeachment" intended to intimidate judges who might rule against President Lee Jae-myung or his allies. Senior PPP members have argued that the ruling party is using its legislative weight to take the judiciary "hostage," essentially telling the courts that any ruling displeasing to the DP will result in the removal of their leadership.

The implications for South Korean democracy are severe. The independence of the judiciary is designed to be a buffer against political swings, yet this move suggests that even the Chief Justice is no longer immune to the polarized "tit-for-tat" politics that have come to define the current era. If the impeachment proceeds and is eventually rejected by the Constitutional Court, the DP will likely face a massive political backlash for creating a leadership vacuum in the courts. However, in the short term, the ruling party appears willing to accept that risk to assert dominance over a judicial branch they view as an obstacle to their agenda.

"If the independence of the judiciary collapses, the damage will fall entirely on the 100% of citizens who rely on the protection of the law." — Statement from the People Power Party leadership.
