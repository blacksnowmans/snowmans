Inside South Korea's Progressive Civil War: Can the Democratic Party Be Saved?

October 13, 2026

South Korea’s ruling liberal bloc is facing a bitter internal fracture as plunging poll numbers and economic pain spark an open revolt against President Lee Jae-myung among his own party's traditional base.

  • What happened: Factions within the Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal party—are openly rebelling against President Lee Jae-myung and party leadership, accusing them of abandoning the progressive principles of former liberal leaders.
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party faces collapsing public approval ahead of upcoming general elections, threatening the broader progressive movement's grip on power.
  • What it changes: Critics within the party are arguing for a total break from the current administration, even suggesting loyalists form a splinter faction rather than compromise foundational values.

A Party Hijacked by "Cuckoos"

For veteran Korean progressives, the current state of the Democratic Party bears little resemblance to the legacy left by former icons like Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in. Critics argue that the modern inner circle surrounding President Lee Jae-myung acts like cuckoos laying eggs in another bird's nest—invading the party apparatus while discarding its foundational identity. Rather than engaging in constructive policy debate, the administration's core is bogged down in internal factionalism, social media spats, and public disputes over appointments.

This ideological disconnect has also surfaced in foreign policy, notably surrounding quiet troop deployments that critics claim mirror past conservative maneuvers without public accountability. Meanwhile, fears persist that the judiciary—led by figures like Chief Justice Cho Hee-de—could weaponize legal risks against progressive leaders to permanently lock the left out of power. For party purists, remaining tied to the current leadership risks dragging the entire democratic movement down with them.

The Road Ahead: Splintering to Survive

The core dilemma facing South Korea's liberal base is whether to reform the party from within or abandon it entirely. Some commentators argue that even if it means shrinking down to a minor "rump party" of thirty or forty committed lawmakers, true believers must reject the current establishment to preserve their integrity. In their view, holding onto a majority is meaningless if the party loses its moral compass.

As the ruling party struggles with economic discontent and infighting, the pressure on lawmakers to distance themselves from President Lee will only intensify. Whether the traditional progressive movement can purge its newly arrived leadership and rebuild under its original banner will define the trajectory of South Korean liberal politics for years to come.
