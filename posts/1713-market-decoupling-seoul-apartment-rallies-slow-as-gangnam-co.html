Market Decoupling: Seoul Apartment Rallies Slow as Gangnam Cools and Outskirts Surge

October 30, 2026

While Seoul apartment prices have marked 90 consecutive weeks of growth, the rally is losing steam as cooling sentiment in the wealthy Gangnam districts clashes with a desperate "gap-filling" surge in the suburban outskirts.

  • Momentum is fading: The weekly growth rate for Seoul apartments slowed to 0.09%, a second consecutive week of deceleration as buyers grow weary of high valuations.
  • The "Gangnam Invincibility" myth is cracking: The prestigious Gangnam 3 districts—Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa—saw prices drop for 12 straight weeks as expensive reconstruction projects struggle under high interest rates and tighter lending rules.
  • The "Balloon Effect" in Gyeonggi: Southern Gyeonggi Province hubs like Gunpo and Suwon saw spikes of 0.58% in a single week, six times the Seoul average, as frustrated buyers flee the capital’s high entry costs.
  • A widening rental-sale gap: Sale prices for standard 84㎡ units (the "national size" of approx. 900 sq ft) fell 2.5% in the third quarter, while Jeonse (South Korea’s unique lump-sum rental deposit) prices jumped 3.8%.

A Market Divided by Regulation and Geography

The current market is defined by a stark "decoupling" between luxury districts and affordable outskirts. Under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, the Democratic Party of Korea has maintained a firm grip on financial liquidity, strictly enforcing Debt Service Ratio (DSR) limits. This has effectively choked off the "panic buying" (locally known as Yeong-kkeul, or "pulling every last soul") that characterized the era of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi. During the previous administration, reckless financial deregulation fueled a speculative bubble in Gangnam’s high-end reconstruction market. Today, those same properties are the first to hit the market as "fire sales" (geup-mae) because owners can no longer afford the carrying costs.

However, this cooling at the top is creating a dangerous "balloon effect" elsewhere. As the average price of an apartment in northern Seoul (Gangbuk) surpassed 1.2 billion KRW (about $860,000) for the first time, middle-class families are being pushed further out. In cities like Gunpo and the Dongtan district of Hwa-seong, prices are jumping by as much as 120 million KRW (about $86,000) in a matter of days. These buyers are not speculators but "survival buyers" terrified that they will be permanently priced out of the Greater Seoul area.

The Growing Risk of the Rental Crisis

Perhaps the most alarming trend for the Lee Jae-myung government is the divergence between sale prices and rental deposits. While the Korea Real Estate Board (the state-run housing agency) reports a nominal 90-week rally, actual transaction prices for standard family units are dipping. In contrast, Jeonse deposits are skyrocketing. As potential buyers retreat to the rental market to wait out the volatility, they are driving up deposits to nearly 90% of the property's value in some areas.

This creates a precarious situation for the "housing ladder." When Jeonse prices rise while sale prices stagnate, it often invites a return of speculative "gap investment," or worse, leaves tenants vulnerable to losing their deposits if a property’s value falls below the rental debt. While the current administration’s focus on crushing speculative demand is necessary to correct the distortions left behind by Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi, it must now pivot toward stabilizing the rental market. Without urgent measures to increase the supply of affordable public housing, the very people the government seeks to protect may find themselves as "housing refugees" in their own city.
