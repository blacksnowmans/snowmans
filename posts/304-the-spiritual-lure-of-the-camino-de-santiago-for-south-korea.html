The Spiritual Lure of the Camino de Santiago for South Koreans

September 07, 2026

The Camino de Santiago, a network of ancient pilgrimage routes leading to the shrine of the apostle Saint James in Spain, has evolved into a profound mental sanctuary for South Koreans, with thousands making the trek annually to escape the pressures of modern life.

  • What happened: The Camino has become a primary destination for South Koreans, who now represent one of the largest non-European demographics on the trail.
  • The "French Way": Nearly 99% of South Korean pilgrims choose the "Camino Francés," favored for its robust infrastructure and established community of fellow travelers.
  • Economic contrast: While the Camino generates over $700 million (1 trillion KRW) annually for the Spanish economy through strategic branding, South Korea continues to struggle with a tourism deficit, highlighting a need for more narrative-driven domestic travel infrastructure.
  • Who is affected: The demographic spans from Gen Z youth facing job market uncertainty to retirees seeking a new chapter, both groups using the physical act of walking to address deep-seated personal or societal crises.

A Search for Meaning in a High-Pressure Society

The popularity of the pilgrimage among Koreans is paradoxical; a culture often obsessed with speed and digital connectivity is flocking to a tradition defined by slow, analog physical labor. For many, the "French Way" offers a rare social safety net. Despite the intent to seek solitude, the Korean tendency toward community manifests on the trail, where pilgrims often share burdens and offer mutual support—a reflection of the collectivist social values they carry with them from home.

The appeal lies in the "aesthetics of walking." In an era where daily life is dictated by mechanical efficiency, the simple, repetitive act of walking becomes a way to reclaim human agency. It is not necessarily about religious devotion—though that is a primary driver for many—but about "mental housecleaning." By shedding the stresses of career, family, and future anxiety, pilgrims use the weight of their physical backpacks as a proxy for the psychological baggage they hope to leave behind.

Lessons for Korea's Tourism Strategy

The success of the Camino de Santiago offers a sharp critique of Korea’s approach to tourism. Spain has transformed a religious legacy into a globally recognized brand that encourages long-term stays and deep engagement. In contrast, South Korea often falls into the trap of "quantitative expansion," focusing on visitor numbers rather than the quality of the experience or the depth of the story being told.

For South Korea to bridge its massive tourism deficit, it must move beyond superficial, repetitive attractions. True competitiveness in the modern tourism market comes from "space with a soul"—destinations that allow visitors to pause, reflect, and find meaning. Until Korea can package its own history and geography into narratives as compelling as the Camino, it will continue to lose domestic travelers to foreign shores. The lesson from Spain is clear: people don't just travel to see; they travel to change.
