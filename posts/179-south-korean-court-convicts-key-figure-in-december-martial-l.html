South Korean Court Convicts Key Figure in December Martial Law Attempt

September 05, 2026

  • A South Korean court has officially convicted a key figure involved in the December 2024 emergency martial law declaration, rejecting claims that the defendant was merely an "accomplice" or "assistant."
  • The ruling confirms that the defendant carried out "major duties" of insurrection, citing the mobilization of over 5,300 armed personnel and police to block democratic institutions.
  • The decision establishes a critical legal precedent that the declaration of martial law without proper democratic process—such as cabinet approval—constitutes clear evidence of subverting the constitutional order.

The End of a Dangerous Gamble

The court’s verdict effectively dismantles the defense’s argument that the defendant was merely an aide to the insurrection rather than a primary actor. By classifying the martial law attempt as a "collective crime" where internal helpers are legally indistinguishable from the instigators, the judge stripped away the defendant’s claims of diminished responsibility. The ruling emphasized that the defendant was fully aware of the criminal nature of the acts, dismissing the narrative of a subordinate following orders.

The night of December 3, 2024, stands as a dark chapter in modern Korean history. The presidential office bypassed all democratic oversight, including the mandatory State Council, to declare martial law. By deploying 1,605 soldiers and 3,790 police officers to seal off the National Assembly—South Korea’s unicameral parliament—the administration attempted to paralyze the only body capable of checking presidential power. The court’s rejection of this "security measure" sends a sharp signal: the state cannot treat its own democratic foundations as obstacles to be swept away by force.

Legal Precedent vs. Political Reality

While the court has fulfilled its duty by upholding the law, the broader impact of this event continues to reverberate through South Korean politics. Following the crisis, the approval rating for the president plummeted to a record low of 13%, yet some factions have attempted to pivot the discourse toward petty partisan squabbling. This refusal to confront the gravity of the insurrection is a dangerous trend; when the survival of democracy is reduced to a game of approval ratings, the risk of repeating history increases.

The legislative focus has now shifted toward reforming the Martial Law Act to make it impossible for a president to bypass parliamentary approval. However, laws alone are not enough. The tragedy of that night was enabled by thousands of civil servants and military personnel who chose to obey unconstitutional orders without questioning their legitimacy. As we look at this verdict, it serves not as a moment for relief, but as a sobering reminder: institutions are only as strong as the people who operate them. Democracy in Korea survived because of the citizens and legislators who resisted that night, but the duty to guard that flame remains a daily responsibility.
