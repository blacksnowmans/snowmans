South Korea’s New Anti-Corruption Agency Risks Becoming ‘Prosecutors in Disguise’

September 29, 2026

  • What happened: Leaked candidate shortlists for the inaugural head of South Korea's newly established Serious Crime Investigation Agency (SCIA) are heavily stacked with former high-ranking prosecutors.
  • Who is affected: Reform-minded voters, civil society groups, and the architects of South Korea’s long-running efforts to strip prosecutors of their sweeping, unchecked investigative powers.
  • What it changes: If appointed, veterans of the traditional prosecution service risk turning the new anti-corruption body into little more than a rebranded extension of the old guard, threatening to stall years of institutional reform.

The Reform Dilemma: Clean Breaks vs. Practical Experience

South Korea’s push to reform its powerful investigative agencies has hit a familiar wall. The SCIA was designed to break the monopoly that prosecutors held over investigations and indictments. Yet, as potential chiefs like former Senior Deputy Prosecutor Kim Ji-yong are floated for the top job, critics see a familiar elite recycling old habits. Appointing alumni of the very institution meant to be checked makes a mockery of institutional separation, trading systemic overhaul for business as usual.

Defenders of these appointments argue a pragmatic point: running a massive investigative apparatus requires deep, practical expertise that outsiders simply do not possess. They warn that installing an inexperienced outsider could paralyze the agency and trigger political backlash if investigations falter. But hiding behind an alleged "talent drought" risks cementing the exact cozy cartels reformers vowed to dismantle. If the movement cannot cultivate or find capable leaders outside the traditional legal establishment, every hard-won structural victory risks collapsing under the weight of its own compromises.

Echoes of the Past: The Choo-Yoon Clashes

For many observers, the current personnel debate feels like déjà vu. It mirrors the bitter 2020 turf war between then-Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae and then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol, a protracted conflict that laid bare just how entrenched and autonomous the prosecutor’s office had become. When ministers attempt to assert democratic control through personnel shifts, the institutional pushback from career prosecutors has historically been fierce and coordinated.

The lesson from past battles is clear: personnel choices are policy. Handing the keys of a reform-minded agency back to figures steeped in the traditional prosecutorial mindset threatens to defang the institution before it even opens its doors. True reform requires tolerating short-term growing pains and actively grooming leaders outside the old boys' club. If decision-makers bow to expediency this time, the SCIA may end up with a fresh coat of paint over the exact same old machinery.
