South Korea Faces a "Judicial Coup" as Public Demands Probe into Chief Justice

November 03, 2026

  • Overwhelming public backing: Around 60% of South Koreans support a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-de, according to recent polling.
  • Controversial acquittals: A lower court recently acquitted military generals who deployed troops during the December 3 martial law attempt, accepting arguments that they were "too busy to watch TV" and thus unaware of the illegality.
  • Allegations of a slow-motion coup: Critics, including dissenting judges, accuse the judiciary under Chief Justice Cho of slow-walking insurrection trials and protecting loyalists linked to imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol.

South Korea’s political crisis has shifted from the streets and the legislature to the courtroom. Weeks after the dramatic collapse of Yoon Suk Yeol's short-lived martial law attempt, public anger is now directed at the judiciary. With imprisoned former leader Yoon sitting behind bars, critics and a large majority of the public argue that South Korea's courts are issuing soft punishments and laying the groundwork for a rehabilitation of the insurrectionists.

The controversy intensified after a lower court acquitted former military generals who led troops into the National Assembly during the December 3 crisis. The court ruled that the commanders lacked the time to watch TV and understand the illegality of the decree—a rationale widely ridiculed by the public as an absurd loophole. Meanwhile, internal whistleblowing within the court network has revealed intense power struggles over Supreme Court appointments, fueling fears that conservative judges appointed during Yoon’s tenure are orchestrating a coordinated defense to protect allies before key trial timelines peak.

The Battle to Purge the Remnants of Insurrection

The standoff highlights deep institutional fractures reminiscent of South Korea's historical struggles with military rule, notably the incomplete purge of Chun Doo-hwan’s regime after 1980. Observers argue that relying solely on regular legal proceedings is failing because the judiciary itself is seen as compromised. With 25% of conservative opposition voters also voicing support for an investigation into Chief Justice Cho, the demand for parliamentary oversight has transcended traditional partisan divides.

As the Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party face mounting pressure, the upcoming months will be crucial. Reform-minded judges and labor groups within the courts are calling for increased scrutiny of judicial appointments and secret meetings among legal elites. Without aggressive parliamentary inquiries to break through the judiciary's institutional shield, South Korea risks seeing the legal system become a safe haven for the architects of its worst constitutional crisis in decades.
