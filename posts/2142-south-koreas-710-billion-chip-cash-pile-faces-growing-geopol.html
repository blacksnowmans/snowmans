South Korea’s $710 Billion Chip Cash Pile Faces Growing Geopolitical Headwinds

November 09, 2026

South Korea’s semiconductor boom has driven corporate cash reserves to historic highs, but mounting US regulatory risks and geopolitical espionage scandals threaten to disrupt the windfall.

  • Record corporate liquidity: Corporate bank deposits exceeding 1 billion won ($740,000) reached a record 960 trillion won (about $710 billion), fueled by a 369% year-over-year surge in third-quarter global DRAM revenues and a 44% jump in corporate dollar deposits.
  • Washington security fallout: Allegations stemming from a US intelligence memo involving Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang have heightened scrutiny over AI technology leaks to China, threatening tighter trade controls on high-bandwidth memory (HBM) supplied by South Korea's Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.
  • AI data center pushback: Growing US political scrutiny over power grid strains in states like Virginia and Texas threatens to curb AI data center expansion, potentially cooling demand for server memory chips.
  • Domestic policy response: Amid uneven domestic recovery, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is moving to restore R&D budgets slashed during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s term and build an 800 trillion won (about $590 billion) semiconductor hub in the southwestern Honam region.

A Paradox of Record Cash and Idle Capital

South Korea’s macroeconomic indicators look exceptionally strong on paper, with total annual exports closing in on $1 trillion and the trade surplus with the US topping $100 billion for the first time. Yet, the vast majority of this capital is not flowing into broad domestic employment or local supply chains. Fearing global volatility, major tech firms are parking their earnings in liquid bank accounts rather than committing to long-term capital investments, creating a stark contrast between top-line export metrics and frozen household sentiment.

Navigating Washington’s Tech War and Infrastructure Brakes

Extraterritorial risks pose an even greater challenge to South Korea's single-engine growth model. Because South Korean memory producers are tightly integrated into Nvidia’s supply chain, any US congressional action restricting Nvidia's export routes directly threatens domestic memory shipments. Concurrently, US political moves to cap data center power consumption could slow capital expenditure by global cloud giants. To counter these vulnerabilities, the Lee Jae-myung administration is restoring state R&D funding and attempting to balance regional development via the Honam semiconductor hub, but navigating the tightrope between US security demands and market execution will require sharp policy precision.
