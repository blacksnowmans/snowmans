South Korea’s ‘Megaregion’ Bill Sparks Outrage Over 52-Hour Workweek Carve-Outs and Corporate Perks

October 26, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung is pushing forward a controversial industrial bill that exempts companies in designated regional hubs from South Korea's statutory 52-hour workweek, drawing fierce accusations from labor unions that the progressive administration is trading core worker protections for corporate favors.

  • The Legislation: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea is advancing the Special Megaregion Act, ostensibly designed to foster balanced regional growth by deregulating semiconductor manufacturing clusters outside the Seoul capital area.
  • The Labor Rollback: The bill suspends the mandatory 52-hour weekly labor cap, extends fixed-term contract maximums from two to four years, and permits foreign-invested firms to withhold pay for statutory weekly holidays.
  • The Corporate Carve-Out: Buried in the text is a targeted antitrust waiver that relaxes holding company ownership thresholds for "great-grandchild" subsidiaries, an exclusive concession tailored specifically for memory-chip giant SK Hynix.

A Decoy for Corporate Restructuring

While public fury has focused on the return of grueling factory and research hours, critics argue the aggressive labor rollbacks function partly as a political smoke screen. By throwing radical labor deregulations onto the bargaining table, the administration has forced labor federations like the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU) into an all-out defensive fight over workplace survival. In doing so, the government distracts from the legislation’s primary corporate prize: a major exemption from South Korea's Fair Trade Act.

Under existing competition rules, a holding company's second-tier subsidiary must hold a 100% equity stake in any third-tier ("great-grandchild") subsidiary to prevent chaebol conglomerates from reckless octopus-style expansion. The pending legislation removes this 100% capital requirement exclusively for the semiconductor industry, bypassing battery and display makers facing identical pressures. The custom design of the clause has led industry analysts and opposition critics to label the measure a bespoke relief package for SK Group chairman Chey Tae-won, enabling SK Hynix to expand its corporate web with far less upfront capital.

Progressive Backsliding and Governance by Decree

The push has triggered sharp disillusionment among President Lee’s traditional working-class base. During an October 23 meeting of the Regulatory Reform Committee, Lee stunned union leaders by brushing off months of protests, remarking that the divide between labor and industry over the 52-hour cap was not large enough to justify "extreme conflict" and suggesting that extra compensation makes excessive overtime acceptable. The rhetoric treats statutory labor limits—established as non-negotiable minimum human safeguards under the Labor Standards Act—as mere transactional costs.

Equally contentious is the administration's willingness to circumvent parliament. Lee openly suggested that if legislative revisions prove too friction-heavy, the Ministry of Employment and Labor should preemptively implement the exemptions through presidential enforcement decrees. This willingness to sideline legislative scrutiny, paired with simultaneous disputes over regional finances drained during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, has stoked internal friction between the presidential office and local party leaders such as Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae. For a president who once campaigned on establishing a 4.5-day workweek, dismantling basic labor baselines in the name of corporate competitiveness marks a profound ideological retreat.
