How a South Korean Couple Renovated an Old House for $70,000

October 07, 2026

Remodeling an old, rundown house in South Korea with a tight budget of about $70,000 (98 million won) demands more than just aesthetic vision—it tests a couple's patience, structural know-how, and navigation of local building laws.

    Preserving the original skeleton: Keeping the existing layout and load-bearing structures saved time and cut unexpected construction costs. Leveraging natural light: Large south-facing windows already present in the home acted as natural heaters and ventilation sources without pricey structural changes. Navigating legal gray areas: Small-scale contractors in South Korea under about $11,000 (15 million won) do not require a license, but failing to draw up clear contracts leaves homeowners fully liable for construction flaws. Relationship test: Daily compromises over design and execution ultimately strengthened the couple's partnership.

The Practical Reality of DIY Renovation in South Korea

Tackling a budget-friendly home overhaul in South Korea often starts with peeling back decades of wear—such as heavy floral wallpapers and archaic boiler systems. Rather than tearing down walls and dragging out construction, the project focused on optimizing small-space flow. By maintaining the home's original bones, the renovators avoided major architectural redesigns and kept unexpected costs down. Existing windows provided ample sunlight and ventilation, eliminating the need to punch through exterior walls and risk waterproofing issues.

Yet, the journey highlighted a major blind spot in South Korea's housing market: the reliance on unlicensed neighborhood contractors. While minor projects under 15 million won legally skip strict interior licensing requirements, doing so without a meticulous contract places 100% of the liability for structural flaws and defects squarely on the homeowner. As previous housing safety standards were loosened during the administration of jailed former leader Yoon석열, the burden of ensuring structural integrity has increasingly fallen back on individual property owners.

More Than Just Bricks and Mortar

Beyond the physical labor and budget spreadsheets, the project evolved into an exercise in personal adaptation. Transforming a cramped attic and worn-out living spaces required abandoning the pursuit of perfection in favor of tailoring the house to fit actual daily life. For international observers looking at South Korea's intense property culture, the project offers a refreshing counter-narrative: instead of chasing brand-new, high-rise apartments, breathing new life into older properties can offer both emotional and financial rewards.
