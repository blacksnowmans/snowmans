President Lee’s Social Media Offensive Exposes Deep Rifts Inside Korea's Ruling Party

October 19, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s controversial online conduct—most recently resharing vitriolic attacks against business leaders and internal party critics from an anonymous supporter account—has laid bare an aggressive campaign to purge rivals and consolidate absolute control over the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

  • The trigger: President Lee retweeted an anonymous partisan account targeting SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won with crude insults, signaling an open invitation for online supporters to attack the tycoon and dissenting voices.
  • The internal fight: Hardline loyalists to Lee, known as the "pro-Myung" faction, have systematically sidelined traditional party mainstays loyal to former presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in to remodel the Democratic Party into a personal political vehicle.
  • The blocked purge: After Lee's ally Kim Min-seok secured party leadership under contested rules, senior liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min and lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae halted an expected exodus by pleading with grassroots members to stay and fight from within.
  • The fallout: With national approval ratings dipping into the 30% range, critics charge the administration with abandoning the party's legacy of coalition-building in favor of toxic factional warfare.

Weaponizing Anonymous Fandoms from Cheong Wa Dae

The presidential office has increasingly bypassed standard communication channels in favor of raw digital mobilization. Lee’s decision to share posts from an anonymous attack account—specifically one taking aim at corporate figures like SK Group’s Chey—is seen by party insiders not as an accidental slip, but as a deliberate "target-marking" exercise. By validating fringe online rhetoric, the administration gives marching orders to militant online fandoms, trading traditional political dialogue for digital pressure tactics.

This tactic reflects a long-running identity conflict within the ruling camp. Unlike predecessors Roh Moo-hyun or Moon Jae-in, who governed through procedural consensus and broad liberal coalitions, Lee’s rise relied heavily on assertive, populist mobilization. Sidelining party moderates and vocal critics has replaced standard party management, raising sharp concerns that presidential power is being funneled into private political vendettas rather than stabilizing state affairs.

A Failed Attempt to Remodel the Party Base

The underlying objective appears to be a forced demographic and factional reset. Allied commentators and loyalist organizers have openly floated the radical idea of "replacing" the party’s voter base—pressuring pro-Moon loyalists out so the ruling bloc can absorb remnants of the conservative opposition following the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The implementation of preferential voting rules to cement Kim Min-seok’s grip on the party leadership was designed to trigger mass defections from the traditional wing.

That plan has stalled. Instead of quitting, mainstream rank-and-file members rallied around calls from Rhyu Si-min and Jung Cheong-rae to hold their ground and expand membership. The resulting friction was palpable during a recent, strained presidential lunch with former President Moon, where former First Lady Kim Jung-sook notably did not appear. Alienating seasoned supporters while trying to engineer a synthetic political base leaves the administration in a precarious bind: isolated within its own ideological home and distracted from pressing economic priorities.
