Why Your Adult Friendships Are Fading—And Why That’s Totally Normal

October 10, 2026

As we get older, friendships don't just fade—maintaining them starts to feel like navigating a minefield, a phenomenon that psychiatrist Dr. Choi Myung-ki argues stems from our own unrealistic expectations and a lack of healthy boundaries.

    What happened: Dr. Choi Myung-ki breaks down the psychology of adult friendship fatigue, explaining that what we often call "closeness" is actually an unhealthy demand for constant validation and boundary-crossing.
  • What it changes: It challenges the cultural guilt around losing friends as we age, reframing social shrinkage as a natural limit of human brain capacity and diverging life paths rather than personal failure.
  • Who is affected: Adults struggling with burnout, people-pleasers caught in draining relationships, and anyone suffering from unexplained physical pain tied to psychological depression.

The core of adult relationship burnout comes down to an obsession with forced intimacy. According to Dr. Choi, people who constantly try to speed-run relationships—turning acquaintances into "brothers" within five meetings—are often just trying to fill an internal void, using others to soothe their own anxieties. On the flip side, keeping a respectful distance isn't coldness; it's an acknowledgment of how easily relationships "suffocate" when people try to pry into every corner of another person’s life.

Moreover, adult friendships naturally decay when life trajectories diverge. If one person in a group of struggling friends achieves massive success, the economic and lifestyle gap makes casual interactions awkward and unsustainable. Rather than agonizing over these shifts as moral failures, we should accept them as the brain's processing limits at work. Trying to maintain a sprawling network of close ties is cognitively impossible past a certain threshold.

Ultimately, much of our relational misery is fueled by what Dr. Choi calls "infinite expectations"—the lingering, childish belief that a partner, friend, or even a casual acquaintance should completely understand and rescue us. When we stop weaponizing our past trauma as an excuse to demand absolute emotional fulfillment from others, and instead focus on protecting our mental energy through deliberate distance, life gets a lot quieter—and a lot healthier.
