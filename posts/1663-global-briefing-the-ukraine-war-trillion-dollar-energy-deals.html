Global Briefing: The Ukraine War, Trillion-Dollar Energy Deals, and Europe's Fractured Front

October 29, 2026

As the U.S. and Russia float a massive energy and economic cooperation deal worth about $13 trillion, Ukraine faces mounting isolation while European leaders scramble to cut independent deals with Moscow.

  • What happened: U.S. emissaries offered to lift economic sanctions and restart the Sakhalin oil and gas projects if Russia ends the war, sparking outrage from Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy.
  • European panic: Fearing a backroom U.S.-Russia deal will leave them vulnerable, the leaders of Finland and Norway broke ranks to hold direct 90-minute phone calls with Vladimir Putin.
  • The ground reality: While a localized truce in Oleshky saw zero violations on its second day, heavy aerial bombardment—including supersonic drones and stealth missiles—continues to batter Ukraine's air defenses.
  • Who is affected: Ukraine is increasingly sidelined in negotiations concerning its own sovereignty, while Western security alliances face a severe test of unity.

The Trump-Putin Energy Pivot and Ukraine's Marginalization

The contours of a potential end to the war are taking shape through the lens of transactional diplomacy, where multi-trillion-dollar business opportunities eclipse national sovereignty and democratic principles. By dangle the carrot of revived energy projects and sanctions relief, Washington aims to break the deadlock on its own terms. Yet this high-stakes dealmaking completely bypasses Kyiv, reducing a nation fighting for its survival to a powerless spectator.

For President Zelenskyy, the prospect of a U.S.-brokered peace that ignores Ukrainian input is a non-starter. While ground skirmishes may briefly pause in select areas, the skies above Ukraine remain a relentless war zone of suicide drones and advanced missile strikes. This glaring disconnect between diplomatic wheeling and dealing on the world stage and the brutal reality on the ground underscores just how expendable weaker nations can become when great powers decide it is time to cut a deal.

European Fractures and the Cost of U.S. Dependency

The tremors of Washington's shifting stance are sending shockwaves across Europe, shattering the continent's united front against Moscow. The recent direct outreach by Nordic leaders to the Kremlin highlights a desperate scramble for self-preservation. European capitals are waking up to the bitter reality that relying entirely on American security and satellite infrastructure—such as the recent Starlink crises—leaves them dangerously exposed to the political whims of a changing White House.

As international pressure mounts and global capitals weigh the precedent this sets for regional security, the Lee Jae-myung administration and South Korean foreign policymakers are watching closely. The erosion of multilateral norms in favor of raw power politics and bilateral backroom deals offers a sobering lesson on the fragility of alliances—a dynamic that carries profound implications for security paradigms closer to home.
