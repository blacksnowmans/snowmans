South Korea's Endless Prosecutor Battle Reignites Over Investigative Powers

October 06, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's long-running debate over prosecutor reform has flared up again, centered on whether prosecutors should retain any direct investigative powers or be strictly limited to indicting suspects.
  • Who is affected: Opposition politicians, law enforcement agencies, and the country's entire criminal justice system are locked in a fierce dispute over the division of powers.
  • Key friction point: While reform advocates demand a total split between investigation and prosecution, opposition leader Lee Jae-myung posted social media remarks hinting at compromise on supplementary investigative rights, sparking internal confusion.
  • What it changes: The debate exposes deep divisions within the progressive camp over how far to strip prosecutors of their traditional grip on criminal probes, casting doubt on whether true reform is within reach.

South Korea's decades-long struggle to rein in its powerful prosecution service is back at the forefront of national politics. At the heart of the latest uproar is the so-called supplementary investigative power—the ability of prosecutors to conduct their own fact-finding after police hand over cases. Reformers argue that letting prosecutors keep any investigative role defeats the entire purpose of breaking up their historically unchecked authority.

The core argument from reform-minded politicians, such as Park Eun-jung of the Rebuilding Korea Party, is simple: prosecutors should exclusively handle legal reviews and indictments, leaving all investigative work to police and dedicated agencies. Critics point out that out of roughly 1.41 million criminal cases handled annually, prosecutors' direct supplementary investigations account for a murky fraction, often functioning as little more than bureaucratic overreach used to preserve institutional turf.

Yet the political opposition itself is stumbling over the finish line. When main opposition leader Lee Jae-myung recently signaled an openness to institutional tweaks that could preserve some form of investigative role for a newly conceived prosecution office, it immediately contradicted hardline allies who demand an absolute ban. For casual observers outside Korea, it looks like tactical political hedging; for reformers, it feels like an avoidable betrayal of a foundational democratic principle.

Ultimately, this isn't just about legal technicalities or bureaucratic workflows. It is a fundamental test of whether South Korea can permanently shed its legacy as a "prosecution republic" where an elite legal corps wields arbitrary political and judicial muscle. Until investigation and prosecution are cleanly separated, every compromise risks leaving the old, bloated power structures intact under a new name.
