Korean Markets: A Showy Facade Over Structural Rot

September 30, 2026

  • The "Korea Premium Week" initiative, aimed at boosting the local stock market through massive investor relations (IR) events, has failed to deliver the structural reforms investors actually demanded.
  • Global instability is rising as U.S. 30-year Treasury yields hit a 21-year high of 5.6%, driven by massive corporate debt issuance to fund artificial intelligence infrastructure.
  • Systemic corruption, including the misuse of non-public information, continues to plague the Korean financial sector, undermining trust and creating an uneven playing field for retail investors.

The Flaws Behind the "Korea Premium"

The "Korea Premium Week 2026," a three-week government-led effort to showcase South Korean capital markets, has left investors underwhelmed. While large conglomerates like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix participated to add prestige, the event lacked the substance to address the "Korea Discount"—the tendency for Korean stocks to trade at lower valuations than global peers. Investors were hoping for concrete structural shifts, such as commercial law reform or clearer delisting criteria for the KOSDAQ (the tech-heavy junior stock market), but instead received little more than a polished PR showcase.

Furthermore, government plans to move toward a 24-hour trading system are being met with skepticism. Critics argue that extending hours benefits brokerage commissions rather than retail investors. True market advancement requires a foundation of trust—such as fixing the broken system for detecting illegal short-selling—rather than simply expanding trading times.

The AI "Black Hole" and the Semiconductor Trap

The global financial environment is currently witnessing a strange anomaly: U.S. 30-year Treasury yields are skyrocketing despite sluggish economic indicators. This shift is largely driven by "AI infrastructure." Tech giants are projected to pour about $500 billion (650 trillion won) into AI infrastructure over the coming years. Because these firms offer higher corporate bond yields than the government, they are effectively starving the Treasury market of capital.

This creates a dangerous cycle where high-interest rates, intended to curb inflation, are instead fueling corporate investment in AI, which in turn drives up prices. Meanwhile, the semiconductor sector—the backbone of the Korean economy—remains in a delicate state. While some fear an HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) supply glut, the reality is that HBM production consumes so much DRAM capacity that it keeps general semiconductor prices artificially high. The true test for this "supercycle" arrives next year: if tech companies cannot prove that their massive AI spending is generating real cash flow, the current market optimism may quickly evaporate.

Ultimately, the Korean market remains hampered by a "closed-circle" culture where privileged information is shared among elites. Recent scandals involving illicit trading rings at top universities are likely just the tip of the iceberg. Until the financial industry undergoes a deep, systemic cleansing of these corrupt practices, no amount of marketing or infrastructure upgrades will successfully restore confidence to the average investor.
