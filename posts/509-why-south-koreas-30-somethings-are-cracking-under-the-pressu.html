Why South Korea's 30-Somethings Are Cracking Under the Pressure to Succeed

September 09, 2026

  • What happened: Popular South Korean communication lecturer Kim Chang-ok opened up about the profound isolation and anxiety he experienced during his 30s, a theme he explores in a new art exhibition called Line, Ruin, What Is Your Consolation?
  • The core pressure: The modern 30-something is trapped in a system that demands constant self-proof through career titles, housing, and social status, turning internal doubt into harsh self-criticism.
  • The shift: Kim and neuroscientist Chang Dong-sun argue that overcoming burnout requires moving away from passive healing and toward active self-expression—like physical art creation—and redefining failure not as a dead-end, but as a new door.

The Architecture of 30-Something Isolation

South Korea's thirties are often framed as the decade to prepare for your prime. In reality, for many living through it, the decade is a grueling exercise in self-censorship. When Kim Chang-ok looked back at his 30s—marked by financial struggles, a lack of elite credentials, and a deep-seated fear of an unforgiving world—he realized he had built an emotional fortress just to survive. But the higher that fortress of titles and achievement gets, the more isolated the person inside becomes.

This isn't just about one man's past. Rising depression rates among South Korea's 30-somethings point to a systemic crisis: the gap between social responsibility and self-actualization has hit a breaking point. When a person's worth is tied strictly to external performance, the brain's fear centers take over, inducing paralysis and burnout. The constant internal question—"Am I qualified enough?"—turns everyday life into an endless trial where no verdict ever feels good enough.

Redefining Healing and Failure

Kim’s recent art exhibition offers a sharp counter-narrative to modern wellness culture. By engaging in exhausting, repetitive physical labor like lacquering and drawing massive lines with heavy rollers, he experienced what neuroscientists call a meditative state. Physical action quieted the overactive, anxious mind. More striking is his "Ruin" series, which features rejected artwork he initially thought was ruined. To a gallery director, however, those "failed" pieces possessed genuine artistic value.

It is a fitting metaphor for a generation terrified of messing up. In a high-pressure society, failure feels like a fatal mark. But as Kim suggests, when life refuses to go according to plan, it often opens up a world you never knew existed. Treating failure as a natural cost of living—rather than a personal defect—is the only way out of the trap. Stop trying to be a finished product, and start treating your 30s as a workshop where the rough edges of who you are actually get shaped.
