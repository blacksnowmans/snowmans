High Court Upholds Guilty Verdict for Yoon Suk-yeol, Threatening Opposition Party with $29 Million Penalty

November 05, 2026

An appellate court has upheld a suspended prison sentence against Yoon Suk-yeol for violating election laws, bringing the main opposition People Power Party face to face with a disastrous 39.7 billion won (about $29 million) financial penalty.

  • What happened: A South Korean high court affirmed that false statements made by Yoon Suk-yeol during his presidential campaign violated the Public Official Election Act and distorted voter choices in a closely contested race.
  • What it changes: The suspended prison sentence legally constitutes an election-invalidating verdict, meaning a final ruling by the Supreme Court will force his party to return all state-reimbursed campaign expenses.
  • Who is affected: The main conservative opposition People Power Party faces potential financial insolvency, while the ruling progressive Democratic Party of Korea is escalating pressure for immediate repayment.

The Financial Threat to the Opposition

The judicial bill left behind by Yoon Suk-yeol—who remains imprisoned following his impeachment—has pushed the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition, into a severe crisis. Under South Korean election law, if a candidate receives an election-invalidating sentence for election law violations, the party that nominated them must return all campaign funds reimbursed by the government. For the PPP, that penalty totals 39.7 billion won (about $29 million). Draining such a massive sum from a party budget reliant on state subsidies and membership dues would effectively paralyze day-to-day operations, staff payroll, and future election organizing.

Political Deflection Meets Legal Reality

Rather than issuing an official apology for defending false campaign statements, the PPP has retreated behind procedural delays, emphasizing that the case still faces a final Supreme Court appeal. Party leaders quickly denied rumors regarding the potential sale of their headquarters building in Seoul's Yeouido political district, insisting that party business will continue uninterrupted. In an attempt to shift public focus, opposition figures have attempted to counter by raising questions about the ongoing legal trials of President Lee Jae-myung, relying on familiar political tactics to reframe a court conviction into a partisan dispute.

However, this stall tactic offers little protection against legal precedent. South Korean courts prioritize swift resolutions in election law cases, and with both the first-instance court and the appellate panel issuing identical guilty verdicts, legal experts consider a Supreme Court reversal improbable. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea continues to press its advantage, arguing that returning public funds gained through false campaign claims is a fundamental legal obligation. By refusing to take responsibility, the opposition risks transforming a massive financial clawback into total political bankruptcy.
