The "Prosecution Service" Reform: A Masterclass in Bureaucratic Survival

September 07, 2026

South Korea is on the verge of a historic legal shift: the abolition of the Prosecutor’s Office and the creation of a new "Public Prosecution Office" (a body intended to focus solely on indictments). However, critics argue that the government’s proposed organizational structure is less about reform and more about preserving the prosecution's traditional grip on power through rebranding and tactical restructuring.

  • Personnel Retention: Despite the goal of separating investigation from prosecution, the new office plans to retain almost all existing prosecutors and investigators.
  • Cosmetic Rebranding: Departments like the "Crime Intelligence Bureau" are being dissolved only to re-emerge under new names with identical functions.
  • The "Judicial Control" Gambit: A newly proposed "Judicial Control Department" would allow prosecutors to monitor police investigations, effectively maintaining their oversight power.
  • Executive Overreach: The government is attempting to implement these structural changes via presidential decree, bypassing parliamentary debate and oversight.

Old Power, New Labels

The core of the prosecution's power has always been its ability to control the flow of information and money. Under the guise of reform, the new Public Prosecution Office is simply shifting its internal focus. By consolidating anti-corruption and drug units into a "Major Crimes Department," the prosecution is ensuring it maintains the same investigative reach it enjoyed under the former "Special Investigation" units. The creation of the "Judicial Control Department" is particularly controversial; it grants prosecutors the right to review police decisions on non-indictment cases, effectively keeping the police under their thumb while pretending to relinquish investigative command.

Furthermore, the prosecution is maneuvering to keep a firm hand on financial investigations. By moving the "Crime Proceeds Recovery" unit to a more influential position within the main office, they are securing control over the "money trail"—a classic leverage point in any major criminal probe. By controlling the flow of money, the prosecution can influence the direction of investigations conducted by other agencies, such as the proposed Major Crimes Investigation Agency.

Democratic Backsliding or Necessary Transition?

The current administration’s reliance on executive orders to push these changes through has sparked significant backlash. Critics, including members of the opposition, note that this "rule by decree" mirrors the undemocratic practices of previous conservative governments. For a public that demanded radical reform to curb prosecutorial overreach, seeing these same tactics used by a new administration feels like a betrayal of the democratic process.

True reform requires dismantling the "imperial prosecutor" culture, where these officials treat themselves as quasi-judicial figures rather than civil servants. If the new Public Prosecution Office is simply a vehicle for the same old power dynamics, the "reform" will be a failure. Without real external oversight and a genuine ceding of power, the transition to the new office risks becoming a shell game—where the name changes, but the unchecked authority remains firmly entrenched.
