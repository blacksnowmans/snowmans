South Korea’s Ruling Party and President Clash Over Property Tax Relief

November 10, 2026

A growing rift between the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the ruling party—and the administration of President Lee Jae-myung over proposed property tax reforms is threatening to paralyze policy and rattle voters ahead of upcoming legislative elections.

  • The Proposal: The DPK is pushing to raise the tax-exempt threshold for the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (the "comprehensive property tax") to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million) for all single-home owners, regardless of whether they occupy the property.
  • The Standoff: President Lee’s administration is firmly resisting, citing concerns that such a broad tax break violates the principle of "tax justice" and undermines the government’s commitment to curbing speculative real estate gains.
  • The Stakes: With elections approaching, DPK lawmakers representing Seoul and the surrounding capital region fear that failure to ease the tax burden will cost them their seats.
  • The Deadline: The party has set the fourth week of November as a "red line" to pass the legislation before annual tax notices are mailed out.

A Tug-of-War Over "Tax Justice" vs. Political Reality

The DPK’s demand for a flat 1.4 billion won exemption represents a significant retreat from its long-standing focus on taxing speculative wealth. By prioritizing a uniform threshold, the party hopes to provide immediate relief to middle-class homeowners in high-cost areas like Seoul, where dissatisfaction with the current tax burden has become a major electoral liability. Supporters of the move argue that the tax has become a "punishment" for those who simply happen to own a home in a market where prices were driven up during the previous administration of Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently incarcerated following his impeachment.

However, the presidential office remains cold to this pivot. President Lee’s administration views the proposal as an inconsistent policy that risks alienating the party’s core base of low-to-middle-income voters. For the administration, the memories of the tax shortfalls and economic imbalances caused by the loose fiscal policies of the Yoon era are still fresh. There is deep-seated concern that if the ruling party abandons its principles for short-term electoral gains, it will lose the moral authority and policy consistency required to govern effectively.

Market Uncertainty and the Search for a "Third Way"

The public airing of these internal disagreements is creating confusion in the real estate market. Potential buyers and sellers are adopting a wait-and-see approach, paralyzed by the conflicting signals coming from the party and the Blue House. Policy flip-flopping—tinkering with exemption thresholds and eligibility criteria—is being criticized as a failure of leadership, as it leaves market participants guessing about the government’s long-term housing strategy.

To break the deadlock, some are suggesting a "third way" that could allow for the 1.4 billion won threshold while introducing tiered deductions based on property holding periods or primary residence status. While this could satisfy both sides, it risks making an already complex tax system even more cumbersome. Ultimately, the DPK and the administration must reconcile their differences quickly; if they cannot present a unified front, they risk failing not just their policy goals, but the voters they are trying to protect.
