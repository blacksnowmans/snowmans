How to Survive the 800km Camino de Santiago: A Guide to Foot Care

September 07, 2026

Completing the Camino de Santiago, a network of pilgrimage routes leading to the shrine of the apostle Saint James in Spain, is less about physical endurance and more about the brutal management of your own feet.

  • Footwear selection: Never buy shoes online; try them on in the afternoon when feet are naturally swollen, and always go one size up.
  • The "Double Sock" strategy: Wear thin toe socks (like those by Injinji) underneath thick wool socks to minimize friction and prevent blisters.
  • Routine maintenance: Treat foot care as a survival ritual—apply cream daily, keep nails short, and address "hot spots" (painful areas) before they turn into blisters.
  • Pacing: Avoid "over-pacing" to reach popular albergues (pilgrim hostels); maintain your own rhythm to avoid injury and burnout.

The Non-Negotiable Rules of Gear

On the Camino, your shoes are more important than your home. While popular brands like Salomon trekking shoes or Hoka cushioned runners are common choices, the brand is secondary to the fit. A common rookie mistake is prioritizing comfort out of the box without considering how feet expand over 800 kilometers (approx. 500 miles). If you ignore your specific foot anatomy in favor of generic recommendations, failure becomes nearly inevitable.

Equally critical is the gear inside the shoe. Experienced pilgrims swear by a two-layer sock system. While wool might sound counterintuitive for summer trekking, it is the gold standard for moisture management and blister prevention. Treat these items as high-performance equipment rather than disposable clothing—carry sewing kits to repair wear and tear, and never assume you can simply "buy your way out" of a bad fit once you are already on the trail.

Beyond Gear: The Mental Game

The difference between those who finish and those who quit often comes down to 10 minutes of disciplined care every evening. This is not just a hike; it is a meditation. You must observe your skin, recognize the subtle warning signs of a blister before it forms, and cultivate the courage to stop for the day if your body demands it. Trying to force a pace set by younger or more experienced hikers is a recipe for medical evacuation.

Ultimately, the Camino is a mirror. It reveals your ability to manage your own physical limits and your willingness to prioritize self-care over rigid itineraries. The goal is to finish the 800km not as a feat of endurance, but as a deliberate, thoughtful experience. If you fail to respect the tiny details of your daily routine, the grand goal will remain out of reach.
