South Korea Freezes Property Valuation Targets for 2025, but Prime Seoul Tax Bills Are Still Set to Soar

November 23, 2026

South Korea’s Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport has frozen the official property tax valuation ratio at 69% for 2025, but rising market prices in Seoul’s prime neighborhoods mean holding taxes for high-end homeowners will still jump by as much as 46%.

  • What happened: The government extended its freeze on official property tax valuation ratios at 69% for the fifth consecutive year, keeping the cap locked through 2027.
  • What it changes: Homeowners in sought-after areas face steep holding tax hikes despite the policy freeze, while a landmark court ruling allows tax authorities to levy gift taxes based on neighboring unit sales prices rather than lower official valuations.
  • Who is affected: High-end apartment owners in Seoul, reconstruction project members hit by average levies of 110 million KRW (about $79,000), and families transferring property to younger generations.

Soaring Taxes in Seoul and Reconstruction Recapture Fears

While freezing the valuation ratio—which caps the portion of market value used for property tax calculations—is intended to cushion tax increases, it fails to shield owners in hot markets. Because holding taxes are calculated by multiplying actual market value by the 69% ratio, surging real estate prices in prime Seoul districts mean assessed values are climbing anyway. For instance, major complexes like Banpo Xi in Seoul’s affluent Seocho District are projected to see holding taxes jump up to 46% next year, widening the tax disparity between core Seoul and stagnant regional markets.

Compounding market anxiety is the enforcement of the Reconstruction Excess Profit Recapture System, a regulation designed to claw back high gains from property redevelopment. Members across 52 reconstruction projects in Seoul face an estimated average windfall levy of 110 million KRW (about $79,000) per owner. These heavy levies have expanded beyond Gangnam to satellite cities in Gyeonggi Province like Gwangmyeong and Suwon, leading property owners to warn that taxing unrealized gains could stall urban redevelopment and exacerbate housing supply shortages.

Stricter Gift Tax Rules and Political Backlash

Property owners attempting to bypass holding taxes by gifting real estate to their children are also hitting legal hurdles. A court recently ruled in favor of tax authorities who calculated gift taxes based on the actual sale price of a neighboring unit sold seven months prior, rather than relying on the lower official tax valuation. This decision directly targets tax-minimization strategies, particularly as the number of thirtysomethings holding properties valued above 1.7 billion KRW (about $1.22 million) has topped 18,000 due to intergenerational wealth transfers.

The issue has quickly escalated into a political debate. The main opposition People Power Party criticized the administration, arguing that surging property taxes and tight mortgage restrictions leave ordinary citizens bearing the brunt of housing market volatility. Conversely, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and government officials maintain that fixing the valuation ratio at 69% through 2027 delivers much-needed tax predictability during uncertain economic conditions.
