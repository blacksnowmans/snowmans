Inside the Democratic Party's Fracturing Coalition: Why Lee Jae-myung's Numbers Are Slipping

October 30, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party and the Lee Jae-myung administration are hemorrhaging support not from conservative opposition pressure, but from self-inflicted sectarian infighting and a rigid intolerance of internal dissent.

  • A cratering progressive base: Automated telephone polling (ARS) shows that Democratic Party support among self-identified progressives plunged 13.8 percentage points between late August and late October, dropping from 74.4% to 60.6%, pulling overall party backing down from 47.0% to 39.7%.
  • The "stepping-stone" migration: Rather than crossing the aisle to the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, frustrated liberal voters are decamping to the smaller, progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, whose support among progressives more than doubled from 10.5% to 22.4%.
  • Leadership fallout: Democratic Party Chairman Kim Min-seok is mired in a 63% negative job rating—including a 52.5% disapproval rate among progressives—weighed down by convention-era factional smears and hardline legal attacks against critical media figures.

The Self-Isolation of Fandom Politics

As prominent progressive commentator Rhee Si-min recently noted, the ruling camp is engaging in collective political self-harm. Partisan media channels and party surrogates have made a habit of attacking internal moderates and dissenting voices, branding them as "fractionalists." This exclusionary behavior, designed by hardline factions to enforce absolute loyalty around President Lee Jae-myung, has paradoxically isolated the presidency by dismantling the broad center-left coalition that brought it to power.

Because crossing from the liberal camp to the conservative People Power Party is a massive psychological leap for left-leaning Koreans, disaffected voters are instead utilizing the Rebuilding Korea Party as a temporary staging ground. Polling reveals that between 80% and 90% of those defecting hold negative views of the current ruling leadership. Their exit is a calculated warning shot rather than permanent ideological realignment.

The Danger of the 40% Fortress

Korean political history demonstrates that administrations fail when they retreat into their core 40% base and dismiss the remaining 60% of the electorate. A similar culture of non-communication and factional purging doomed governance during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, culminating in his impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment. The current leadership risks repeating those same structural errors by mistaking a strong parliamentary majority for an excuse to run a unilateral, one-man show.

Recovering lost ground requires the Democratic Party to abandon its bunker mentality. If Chairman Kim and the presidential office continue to pursue legal intimidation against critical journalists and treat internal allies as adversaries, the governing coalition will permanently dissolve. Retaining the power to govern effectively hinges on restoring tolerance and embracing a broader reformist coalition, rather than catering exclusively to an aggressive partisan fringe.
