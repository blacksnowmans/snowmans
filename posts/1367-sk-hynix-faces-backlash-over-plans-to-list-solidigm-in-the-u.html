SK Hynix Faces Backlash Over Plans to List Solidigm in the US

October 21, 2026

SK Hynix is triggering fierce market and political outrage over plans to publicly list its US enterprise SSD subsidiary, Solidigm, in a move critics brand as the latest predatory "split-off listing" to gut minority shareholder value.

  • The move: Memory chip giant SK Hynix is pursuing a Wall Street initial public offering (IPO) for Solidigm, the US-based NAND flash and solid-state drive business it acquired from Intel.
  • The consequence: The listing would establish an unwieldy five-tier corporate pyramid (from holding firm SK Inc. down to Solidigm), siphoning high-margin enterprise SSD growth away from parent shareholders and threatening to worsen the "Korea discount."
  • The market reaction: Foreign investors are dumping SK Hynix shares amid dilution fears and growing suspicion that management is rushing the IPO before the NAND memory cycle peaks.
  • The regulatory threat: President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are preparing aggressive legal countermeasures, including a cap on controlling shareholders' voting rights for subsidiary spin-offs.

The Curse of the Double Listing

In South Korea's capital markets, subsidiary carve-out listings—known locally as jjogaegi sangjang—have long infuriated investors. Conglomerates fund high-risk expansion using parent-company balance sheets, only to spin off the profitable crown jewels into separate IPOs, leaving original shareholders holding an empty shell. The playbook evokes the painful precedent of battery maker LG Energy Solution's 2022 spin-off from LG Chem, which permanently cratered the parent company's market valuation. Solidigm accounts for roughly 9 to 10 percent of SK Hynix's consolidated revenue; carving it out threatens to push SK Hynix's already depressed price-to-book ratio (around 0.92) even lower.

SK Hynix does not lack cash, having posted record earnings on the back of the artificial intelligence boom in high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Rushing Solidigm—estimated to be worth between $7.5 billion and $15 billion (10 trillion to 20 trillion won)—onto an American exchange looks less like a capital necessity and more like an opportunistic cash grab. Once listed separately, it will also pit the interests of parent and subsidiary shareholders against each other over internal wafer pricing.

Capital Flight and a Political Reckoning

Markets are already reacting aggressively. SK Hynix's US-traded American Depositary Receipts (ADRs) are trading at an astonishing 40 percent premium over the underlying shares listed in Seoul. Foreign institutional investors see little incentive to hold the discounted Korean parent when they can buy the US-listed ADR or wait for Solidigm's direct debut on Wall Street. Worse, management's rush to market sends a troubling signal: investors suspect SK Group believes the memory cycle is nearing its apex and is racing to cash out before prices soften around 2026.

Unlike during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term, when such corporate carve-outs were largely tolerated under the banner of market deregulation, the policy landscape has shifted decisively under President Lee Jae-myung. The Lee administration has compared split-off listings to "raising a cow only to have someone steal the newborn calf." Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are now moving to amend the Capital Markets Act to mandate parent-shareholder approval for carve-out IPOs while capping majority shareholder voting rights at 3 percent. With foreign capital fleeing and Seoul regulators circling, SK Group's Wall Street gambit risks becoming a costly governance blunder.
