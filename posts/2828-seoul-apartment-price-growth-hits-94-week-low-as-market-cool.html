Seoul Apartment Price Growth Hits 94-Week Low as Market Cools

November 28, 2026

Seoul’s residential property boom has virtually ground to a halt, with weekly apartment price growth dropping to a 94-week low of 0.01% amid tightening credit controls and surging seller inventory.

  • Price freeze across districts: Nine of Seoul's 25 municipal districts have flipped into negative price growth, while active unsold listings in the capital have topped 80,000 units.
  • Shift to compact housing: Soaring unit costs have forced buyers away from standard 84 m² (approx. 900 sq ft) homes toward smaller 59 m² (635 sq ft) layouts, where presale competition is 3.7 times higher.
  • Gyeonggi-heavy supply plan: The Lee Jae-myung administration has slated 103,000 new suburban homes for the capital region, but 99% of them are located in neighboring Gyeonggi Province.
  • Outward demand spillover: About 43,000 residents leave Seoul annually due to high housing costs, fueling price surges in southern Gyeonggi industrial hubs like Dongtan and Pyeongtaek.

Mortgages and Presale Prices Outpace Middle-Class Buyers

The sharp cooling in Seoul marks a psychological turning point after nearly two years of frantic panic-buying. Stricter mortgage restrictions enforced by financial regulators have severely curbed purchasing power, causing market inventory to swell by 20,000 units over just three months. Even when presale opportunities arise—such as the recent Gyeonghuigung Epit development priced at 2.57 billion won (about $1.9 million) for an 84 m² layout—the vast upfront cash requirement renders central Seoul real estate inaccessible to ordinary workers. Consequently, younger buyers are crowding into compact 59 m² apartments as their last entry point into the capital, squeezing residential quality even further.

Policy Blind Spots and Capital Spillover

The government's strategy relies heavily on expanding land supply outside the capital, but dumping 99% of new housing stock into Gyeonggi Province risks creating distant bedroom communities without adequate transit infrastructure to Seoul. Rather than calming the market uniformly, these credit restrictions and supply mismatches have triggered a classic regional "balloon effect." While Seoul transactions stall, liquidity is flowing into southern Gyeonggi manufacturing hubs backed by major corporate employers like Samsung Electronics. Apartment prices in Dongtan have surged 23% this year, while key projects near Pyeongtaek's Jije Station have crossed the 1 billion won ($740,000) threshold—demonstrating that suppressing Seoul lending simply pushes housing cost pressures into the broader metropolitan fringe.
