South Korea’s Ruling Coalition Moves to Curb Chief Justice’s Authority with New Reform Bill

October 30, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea and its allies have introduced a bill requiring the Supreme Court Chief Justice to nominate two candidates for each high court vacancy, seeking to break the judicial head's exclusive control over bench appointments.

  • What happened: Liberal lawmakers proposed mandatory dual nominations for Supreme Court vacancies and initiated a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over trial delays and court administration.
  • What it changes: The bill diffuses the Chief Justice's sole appointment authority, giving President Lee Jae-myung and parliament options between multiple pre-vetted candidates.
  • Who is affected: The judiciary faces unprecedented legislative scrutiny, while the conservative main opposition People Power Party (PPP) has launched a counteroffensive claiming the bill violates the constitution.
  • Internal debates: The ruling camp remains divided over tactics, with some lawmakers warning that a aggressive probe into the Chief Justice could trigger a severe political backlash.

Diluting Judicial Power or Restoring Democratic Oversight?

Under current practice, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court holds exclusive power to select a single nominee for each bench vacancy. Reform advocates argue this monopoly created an insular judicial hierarchy that eroded public trust, particularly during the period of governance under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. By mandating two candidates per open seat, the bill aims to introduce competitive vetting and curb court conservatism, allowing elected leaders to select from a broader pool.

The PPP and Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon have fiercely condemned the initiative as an unconstitutional assault on the separation of powers. Opposition figures argue that forcing the judiciary to submit multiple candidates turns judicial selection into a political contest, effectively allowing the ruling party to dictate court appointments under the guise of reform.

High Stakes and Strategic Friction in Yeouido

The simultaneous push for a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has exposed tactical rifts within the ruling camp. While vocal progressive commentators demand outright impeachment, moderate Democratic Party lawmakers, including Representative Kim Young-jin, have publicly questioned the effectiveness of an investigation. They worry that a protracted clash with the court system could alienate moderate voters without producing concrete legislative gains.

Structural relief is overdue for South Korea’s highest court, where individual justices are bogged down handling an overwhelming 3,000 to 4,000 cases annually. However, using legislative dominance to forcefully restructure court procedures risks miring crucial judicial reform in an endless constitutional standoff before the Constitutional Court.
