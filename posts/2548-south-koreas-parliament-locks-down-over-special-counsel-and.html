South Korea’s Parliament Locks Down Over Special Counsel and Judicial Clashes

November 20, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung triggered a sharp legislative and constitutional confrontation on November 19 by approving a special prosecutor bill targeting alleged prosecutorial misconduct during Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, according to a political briefing from Korean commentary site Blacksnowman.

  • Special counsel and street protests: President Lee, of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party, signed the Manipulated Indictment Special Prosecutor Act while on an overseas trip. The main conservative opposition People Power Party (107 seats) and the minor Reform Party (3 seats) denounced the decision, with People Power Party floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik announcing plans to challenge the bill at the Constitutional Court and stage street marches near universities on November 20.
  • Filibuster over democracy honors: In the National Assembly, the Democratic Party introduced a bill granting official recognition and benefits to victims of past democratization movements. The People Power Party immediately launched a filibuster—an unlimited floor debate—arguing the bill offers unfair favors to former activist groups, while Democratic lawmakers accused the opposition of blocking livelihood and historical legislation, including revisions to the Jeju April 3 Special Act supporting bereaved families on Jeju Island.
  • Supreme Court ruling sparks impeachment talk: The Supreme Court overturned and remanded an insurrection-related case involving former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo with a finding of partial guilt. While the ruling party welcomed the decision, Democratic Party leaders including lawmaker Kim Min-seok argued there are sufficient grounds to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over alleged failures to uphold judicial neutrality—a claim People Power Party leaders condemned as a "legislative coup" and retaliation against the courts.
  • Stalled budgets and local autonomy: The floor standoff sidelined committee budget reviews—including in the Foreign Affairs and Unification Committee—as well as demands from regional council leaders for a Local Council Act that would grant local assemblies independent budget and organizational powers.

What the Standoff Signals

The November 19 developments point to an escalating institutional clash between South Korea’s executive and legislative majority on one side, and the conservative opposition and judiciary leadership on the other. According to the Blacksnowman briefing, which strongly backs the administration's stance, the Democratic Party views the special prosecutor law, the democracy merit bill, and scrutiny of Chief Justice Cho as overdue steps to correct past abuses of state power and restore trust in the legal system.

The opposition parties, by contrast, frame the same sequence of events as political retaliation and an assault on judicial independence. By combining a Constitutional Court petition with a parliamentary filibuster and street rallies, the People Power Party is leaning on institutional delays and public mobilization to counter a majority it cannot outvote on the chamber floor.

With both camps treating judicial rulings and prosecutorial oversight as existential battles, routine governance is bearing the cost. Until the two main parties find a procedural off-ramp, key fiscal and administrative items—from foreign policy budgets to local government reforms—remain trapped in parliamentary gridlock.
