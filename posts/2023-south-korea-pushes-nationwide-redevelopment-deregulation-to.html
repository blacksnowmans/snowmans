South Korea Pushes Nationwide Redevelopment Deregulation to Boost Housing Supply

November 06, 2026

South Korea has launched a sweeping push across Seoul, its surrounding metropolitan region, and provincial areas to ease redevelopment and reconstruction rules in an effort to unblock stagnant housing supply.

  • What happened: Land Minister Hong Ji-sun called for a major speed-up in Seoul's urban renewal projects, while South Gyeongsang Province enacted an ordinance lowering the required proportion of aged structures for redevelopment eligibility from 60% to 50%.
  • Local policy moves: Municipalities like Seongnam City are securing public housing near Sanseong Station to prepare for displaced residents, while Gwacheon is adopting resident-led planning models to streamline approvals.
  • Market impact: Major builder Daewoo E&C surpassed ₩5 trillion (about $3.6 billion) in urban renewal orders this year, though industry watchers highlight growing polarization between top-tier and mid-sized contractors.
  • Who is affected: Property owners face surging construction costs and unexpectedly high extra contribution fees, while local renters face heightened housing instability as displacement demands peak.

Speed vs. Red Tape: The Reality on the Ground

Following the rigid real estate controls during Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency, the current Lee Jae-myung administration is pushing a supply-focused urban renewal agenda to stabilize the housing market. However, central government signals continue to run into severe administrative delays at the municipal level. In Seoul's Changsin-dong neighborhood, for example, redevelopment remains paralyzed in the permitting stage despite securing a 75% resident consent rate. Real estate experts and legal analysts point out that inconsistent legal interpretations by local municipal officials regularly delay projects for years, worsening Seoul's lease inventory shortages.

The Surging Cost Trap for Homeowners

Deregulatory measures like South Gyeongsang’s lowered structural age threshold are intended to revitalize shrinking provincial city centers, but easing entry requirements does not automatically make projects financially viable. As inflation drives up building material and labor expenses, many redevelopment union members are hit with severe late-stage "contribution fee" surcharges just as construction nears completion. Without transparent appraisal standards and government mediation mechanisms for rising construction costs, rapid regulatory relief risks pushing long-term residents out of their neighborhoods under the weight of unexpected debt.
