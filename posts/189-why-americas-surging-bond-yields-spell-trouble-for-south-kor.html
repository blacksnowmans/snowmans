Why America’s Surging Bond Yields Spell Trouble for South Korea

September 05, 2026

  • What happened: Surging U.S. long-term bond yields and a $40 trillion national debt have pushed the U.S. federal government's annual interest payments past $1 trillion.
  • Why it's happening: Massive government borrowing collides with Big Tech firms—such as Microsoft and Google—scooping up over $200 billion in corporate bonds this year to fund AI infrastructure, triggering a tug-of-war for capital.
  • Who is affected: Everyday retail investors and export-driven economies like South Korea, where capital outflows can quickly strain foreign exchange reserves.

The Debt Trap and Policy Mismatch

The U.S. Treasury faces a structural crisis: it must keep borrowing while debt exceeds 123% of GDP, forcing it to spend over $1 trillion a year just on interest. At the same time, policy is fracturing. While the Federal Reserve tries to keep interest rates elevated to tame inflation, the Treasury attempts to lower long-term yields through buyback strategies that inadvertently drive up short-term debt issuance, further distorting the yield curve.

For South Korea, this macroeconomic friction is an imported danger. When global investors dump emerging market and local assets to chase safe-haven dollars, South Korea's $420 billion foreign exchange reserves offer limited defense against a massive capital flight. Heavy leverage and overreliance on local currency assets have become high-stakes gambles under these conditions.

My Take: The Illusion of Cheap Money is Dead

The era of cheap money born after the 2008 financial crisis and extended through the pandemic is definitively over. While past deficit spending was often justified as necessary stimulus or social support, today's ballooning deficits reflect an economic system where unmoored capital distorts asset markets and eats away at sovereign credit. Politics has been rendered largely powerless by a swamp of unpayable debt.

Investors cannot afford to ignore these warning signs. Surviving the volatility ahead requires cutting down on debt and hardening portfolios by diversifying into dollar-denominated holdings, gold, or alternative assets. The high interest rates we see today are simply the overdue bill for a decade of living on easy credit.
