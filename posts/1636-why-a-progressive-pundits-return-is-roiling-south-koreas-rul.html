Why a Progressive Pundit’s Return Is Roiling South Korea’s Ruling Camp

October 28, 2026

Prominent progressive intellectual Rhyu Si-min’s return to political commentary has triggered sharp infighting within South Korea’s ruling camp, as partisan loyalists accuse his candid critiques of dragging down President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings.

  • What happened: Rhyu returned to live political broadcasts after a five-week hiatus, drawing over 87,000 concurrent viewers on YouTube, but drew fire from pro-government channels claiming his commentary deliberately sabotages rising presidential polls.
  • The pivot: In response to factional backlash, Rhyu announced a tactical shift toward detached observation—likening his new approach to 19th-century entomologist Jean-Henri Fabre studying insects—rather than offering direct prescriptive advice to the administration.
  • The core warning: Rhyu cautioned that if the ruling Democratic Party compromises on fundamental structural mandates like prosecutorial reform, its value-driven core supporters will not defect, but will simply "turn cold" and stop defending the government.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung's administration and the broader liberal media landscape, which is wrestling with rising factional intolerance toward internal dissent.

The Trap of Uncritical Loyalty

The accusation that a single commentator can single-handedly tank presidential approval ratings underestimates the public. South Korean voters do not adjust their support on the say-so of public intellectuals; they respond when a critic articulates frustrations they already harbor. Treating legitimate policy critique as factional sabotage insults voter agency and cuts the ruling party off from ground-level reality.

Unconditional cheerleading is dangerous for any administration. South Korea witnessed this dynamic during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose insular circle branded internal critics as hostile forces, ultimately paving the path to his impeachment and imprisonment. For President Lee's government, silencing constructive criticism in the name of factional unity risks repeating the exact insularity that brought down its predecessor.

Core Values Versus Mercenary Punditry

Rhyu’s underlying critique targets a real structural fault line within the ruling camp: the tension between opportunists chasing political spoils and ideological loyalists who demand deep-seated reforms, particularly reining in the state prosecution service. When an administration soft-pedals its core reform promises to preserve short-term polling, the opportunists flee at the first sign of trouble, while the reform base disengages emotionally. That leaves the government vulnerable to counterattacks from the conservative opposition, the People Power Party.

Compounding this vulnerability is the rise of partisan online influencers—dubbed "mercenary commentators"—who manufacture outrage against seasoned progressive figures such as Rhyu, former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, and podcaster Kim Ou-joon. Purging internal debate to enforce ideological conformity destroys the ruling bloc's intellectual diversity. If the Lee administration hopes to sustain durable public trust, it must treat candid criticism as an early warning system rather than a hostile act.
