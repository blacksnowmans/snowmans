South Korean President Lee Jae-myung Faces Fire Over Disaster-Day Golf Outing and Unfiltered Social Media

October 12, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing sharp political blowback after the presidential office admitted he played a round of golf on the same day a building collapse injured 38 people—just hours after he used social media to publicly condemn elite privilege.

  • The incident: On October 10, while rescue operations were underway following a commercial ceiling collapse that injured 38 in the southeastern city of Daegu, Lee spent the afternoon golfing with family at the military-run Taereung Golf Course in Seoul.
  • The exposure: The presidential office acknowledged the outing only after Representative Joo Jin-woo of the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition party, announced plans to release photo and video evidence.
  • The double standard: Hours before hitting the links, Lee had posted on X (formerly Twitter) excoriating officials who enjoy luxury on taxpayers' money while cutting welfare budgets—a remark widely seen as a swipe at fellow party member and Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae, who is under fire over her 1.22 billion won (about $900,000) official residence.
  • The official defense: Aides insisted Lee managed the crisis by phone from the course and argued the outing served as practice for potential golf diplomacy with U.S. President Donald Trump at the G20 summit in Miami this December.

Direct Communication as a Double-Edged Sword

Lee has long credited direct, unmediated communication on social media with sustaining his political rise. During a five-night trip to the United States and Mexico, he published roughly 30 posts, part of a surge that saw his monthly output on X exceed 100 messages. Yet bypassing normal vetting and institutional channels has generated repeated liabilities. Past missteps include a post referencing the Holocaust that drew an official protest from Israel's foreign ministry, an abruptly deleted warning in Khmer directed at Cambodian crime rings, and an accidental leak of classified markings in a photo touting voice-phishing enforcement.

Internal Feuds and Institutional Damage

The latest controversy highlights a deeper structural problem: the blurring of official state apparatus with the president's personal social media feed. By using his account to air grievances and indirectly target intra-party rivals like Governor Choo—who was already clashing with the presidential office over the nomination for the Serious Crimes Investigation Office—Lee is encouraging his online base to wage factional warfare against members of his own ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

Relying on social media to project moral authority while skirting formal vetting mechanisms creates an acute vulnerability. When aggressive populist rhetoric on digital platforms clashes directly with private conduct during a domestic emergency, defending a leisure outing as diplomatic prep work does little to counter opposition charges of hypocrisy.
