Why South Korea’s Culture of Endless Workplace Toil Is Crumbling Under AI

October 25, 2026

South Korea’s traditional veneration of blind corporate diligence and top-down deference is collapsing into obsolescence as artificial intelligence and lean production render repetitive, rule-bound white-collar labor worthless.

  • The cultural reckoning: Futurist and big data analyst Song Gil-young argues in his latest work that sugo—a ubiquitous Korean term for workplace toil that literally means "accepting suffering"—has transformed from a collective badge of honor into an engine of pointless busywork.
  • The structural shift: Decades of accumulated red tape and defensive bureaucracy, designed largely to help managers dodge personal accountability, are failing against agile tools and automated workflows.
  • Who is affected: Middle managers who built careers on administrative surveillance and following orders ("copycat humans") face rapid displacement, while younger professionals are abandoning the illusion of lifetime corporate security.
  • The survival rule: Economic value is migrating away from standardized functional labor toward personal taste and public accountability—requiring individuals to execute work directly, publish it openly, and stake their own names on the result.

The Death of the Corporate Shield

For decades, South Korean corporate culture treated endurance as competence. Leaving the office before one's supervisor was considered a breach of etiquette, and arriving an hour early to wipe senior colleagues' desks was praised as loyalty. Offices sustained an expanding labyrinth of procedural checklists not to create value, but to answer the perennial bureaucratic threat: "Will you take responsibility if something goes wrong?" The result was a culture of institutional paralysis, where avoiding blame took precedence over innovation.

That bargain has broken down. While human lifespans push toward 100, average corporate lifespans have shrunk to roughly 15 years. The apprentice model—where junior recruits endured harsh feedback in exchange for institutional know-how—has disintegrated because junior staff can acquire cleaner, faster instruction from AI platforms than from disengaged managers. Mid-level employees who faithfully executed orders now find that software handles data aggregation, reporting, and scheduling far more transparently than human supervisors ever did.

From Functional Labor to Distinctive Taste

Song frames the coming economic divide through a stark retail contrast: a 50-cent mass-produced supermarket ice pop versus a $6 scoop of artisanal gelato. Both solve the functional problem of delivering cold sugar, but the market happily pays a tenfold premium for raw ingredients, human vision, and refined taste. When generative models reduce the marginal cost of producing baseline code, text, and analysis to near zero, standardized functional labor inevitably faces brutal downward price pressure.

South Korea’s emerging workforce is adapting by operating as "nuclear individuals." Rather than relying on corporate titles that vanish upon retirement, professionals must build independent credibility through three practices: doing the hands-on technical work themselves rather than delegating it away, publishing their outputs to build an audience, and attaching their own names to their work instead of hiding behind a team banner. In an economy where corporate ladders are disappearing, an individual portfolio of taste and execution is becoming the only viable safety net.
