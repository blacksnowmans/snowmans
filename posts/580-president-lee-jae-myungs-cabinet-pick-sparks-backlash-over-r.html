President Lee Jae-myung’s Cabinet Pick Sparks Backlash Over Reform Promises

October 02, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s appointment of Kim Ji-yong as the head of the new Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA)—an organization designed to reduce the historical power of the prosecution—has triggered a fierce outcry from his own supporters and media figures, highlighting a deep rift in his administration's reform agenda.

  • The Appointment: Kim Ji-yong, a former prosecutor, was tapped to lead the MCIA, an agency explicitly created to break the legal establishment’s monopoly on investigative power.
  • The Reaction: The decision stunned political commentators, including popular host Choi Wook of the political talk show Mae-bul Show, who argued the move contradicts the administration’s core promise of "prosecutorial reform."
  • The Defense: President Lee has taken to social media to defend the pick, labeling criticism of the appointment as "obstruction of state affairs" and dismissing claims that Kim is a pro-Yoon Suk-yeol (former president) figure.
  • The Conflict: Supporters who helped elect Lee are increasingly frustrated, feeling that the government is trading its reformist identity for institutional comfort.

A Clash of Visions for Reform

The controversy centers on the fundamental purpose of the MCIA. Originally envisioned as a neutral body that would strip prosecutors of their exclusive control over investigations, the appointment of a career prosecutor to lead it has been interpreted by many as a betrayal of these goals. While President Lee argues that Kim is qualified and was unfairly sidelined under previous administrations, critics point out that the issue isn't Kim’s personal history—it is the structural reliance on the same legal "caste" the MCIA was meant to dismantle.

By framing the criticism as "obstruction," President Lee is signaling a shift toward a more aggressive, direct communication style via social media. However, this approach risks alienating his base. Observers note that by bypassing official briefings and engaging in public spats, the President is creating unnecessary friction. This style—often viewed as "tinkering" with personnel rather than pursuing deep structural change—has left the new agency struggling to launch with a clear identity, just as the nation’s criminal justice system is undergoing a massive, uncoordinated transition.

The "Anxious Support" Dilemma

The backlash from figures like Choi Wook represents what some call "anxious support"—a base that is not inherently anti-government but is terrified that the administration is losing its way. There is a palpable fear among supporters that if this government fails to deliver genuine reform, the resulting political vacuum will be filled by the far-right.

This incident reflects a broader pathology in Korean politics: the tendency to silence dissent by labeling it as betrayal. If the administration continues to view concerned feedback as an attack, it risks isolating itself in an echo chamber of blind loyalty. For President Lee, the challenge is not just winning a debate over whether a specific appointee is "pro-Yoon" or not; it is proving that he still represents the structural change his voters were promised.
