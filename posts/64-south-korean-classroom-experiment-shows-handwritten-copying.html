South Korean Classroom Experiment Shows Handwritten Copying Revives Kids' Focus

September 27, 2026

  • An EBS documentary project had South Korean sixth-graders spend an hour a day copying out the children's book Long Long Night by hand for four weeks.
  • Brain scans and clinical evaluations during the project showed significant gains in attention span, self-efficacy, and activation of the prefrontal cortex compared to digital typing.
  • The findings challenge South Korea's heavy push into digital-first education, highlighting the cognitive necessity of analog learning tools.

South Korean classrooms have spent years racing toward digital innovation, handing out tablets and replacing textbooks with screens. But a recent experiment broadcast by the educational network EBS suggests this rush may be costing students their ability to concentrate. When teachers at Songre Elementary School tasked sixth-graders with copying a book out by hand for one hour every day, the struggle was immediate. Students accustomed to the instant dopamine hits of short-form videos could barely sit still for ten minutes, let alone fill a page with handwriting.

Yet, as the four-week project progressed, the physical act of shaping letters sparked a remarkable neurological shift. Clinical evaluations using the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure showed sharp jumps in students' self-reported focus and satisfaction. Brain imaging using functional near-infrared spectroscopy revealed that handwriting intensely stimulated the prefrontal cortex—the region responsible for high-level cognition, working memory, and planning—far more than typing on a keyboard ever did. In an education system obsessed with speed and efficiency, this low-tech intervention proved that the slow, deliberate pain of holding a pen is precisely what builds a child's mental resilience.
