South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Chief Justice Over Insurrection Ruling

November 23, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party has launched a task force to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, accusing him of procedural violations and judicial bias following a rapid decision to overturn a lower-court insurrection ruling against former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo.

  • The disputed ruling: In just nine days, the Supreme Court overturned and remanded key portions of Han’s insurrection conviction, raising the legal threshold required to prove criminal intent for disrupting the constitutional order under Article 87 of the Criminal Act.
  • Grounds for impeachment: Democratic Party lawmaker Noh Jong-myeon said the impeachment push targets alleged procedural breaches—including bypassing statutory timelines under the special prosecutor act and the "6-3-3" trial deadline rule—rather than the verdict’s substance, while accusing the conservative opposition People Power Party of trying to stoke political backlash.
  • Martial law and ethics claims: Critics and a cited Yonhap News report allege Cho instructed during martial law that judicial authority would shift to the martial law commander, and question Justice Kwon Young-joon’s refusal to recuse himself despite previously receiving roughly 1 billion won (about $700,000) in advisory fees from the law firm Kim & Chang, where Han once served as an adviser.
  • What comes next: The Democratic Party aims to pass an impeachment motion in a December parliamentary plenary session to immediately suspend Cho from duty, while attention turns to an upcoming meeting of the National Conference of Judges.

Legal and Ethical Flashpoints

At the center of the dispute is what critics—including Justice Oh Kyung-mi in a rare dissenting opinion—describe as an abrupt shift in evidentiary standards. According to legal analysts cited by the ruling party, the court departed from earlier military mutiny and insurrection precedents by requiring proof of active intent rather than willful awareness, while treating Yoon Suk-yeol’s insurrection actions separately from Han’s alleged role. Critics also note that just 30 minutes earlier, the same court applied a comprehensive standard to assess overall conduct in a separate case involving Kim Keon-hee.

Conflict-of-interest allegations have deepened the political fallout. Records cited in the impeachment push show Justice Kwon earned more than 1.8 billion won (about $1.3 million) writing 63 advisory opinions for seven major law firms between 2018 and 2022 while serving as a professor, with over half of that sum coming from Kim & Chang alone.

Constitutional Stakes Ahead of December

Whether Cho remains in office now hinges on a high-stakes clash between legislative oversight and judicial independence. For the Democratic Party, passing an impeachment motion in December would represent an extraordinary effort to hold the judiciary accountable for alleged procedural shortcuts and martial law-era directives. Yet attempting to remove a sitting chief justice also carries political risk, as opposition lawmakers argue the campaign threatens the independence of the courts.

Before parliament votes, the upcoming National Conference of Judges will serve as an important barometer of internal sentiment. Although the autonomous body of roughly 120 elected judge representatives holds no legal power to impeach a chief justice, rank-and-file judicial dissent has historically catalyzed major institutional reckoning in South Korea—and its stance could shape public legitimacy on both sides of the standoff.
