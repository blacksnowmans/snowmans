The "Great Betrayal": South Korea's Stalled Prosecution Reform

September 23, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has sparked a political firestorm by admitting that his vision for "prosecution reform"—a cornerstone promise of his administration and the ruling Democratic Party—amounts to little more than splitting existing agencies rather than the total separation of investigation and indictment that voters were promised.

  • What happened: In a recent press conference, President Lee suggested that the maximum extent of reform would be dividing the Ministry of Justice into separate investigative and prosecutorial units, a stark retreat from the campaign pledge of complete functional separation.
  • The backlash: Prominent writer and liberal intellectual Rhyu Si-min publicly criticized the President, arguing that neither the President nor any serious proponent of reform ever intended to merely duplicate the existing bureaucratic structure.
  • The tactics: Critics allege the government has used "delay tactics," such as creating task forces under the Prime Minister's office, to bypass genuine legislative debate and maintain administrative control over state investigations.
  • The impact: The revelation has shattered the expectations of the liberal base, fueling accusations that the administration is prioritizing the preservation of political power over systemic institutional reform.

A Blueprint for Power, Not Justice

For years, "prosecution reform" has been the rallying cry of South Korea's liberal bloc. The goal was simple: strip the powerful prosecution service of its dual authority to both investigate crimes and prosecute them, thereby preventing institutional overreach. However, the President’s recent admission has exposed a vast chasm between these campaign slogans and the administration’s actual blueprint.

The pattern has become familiar. When legislative hurdles emerge, the government shifts gears, opting for internal restructuring—such as creating new bureaus or task forces—that effectively recreates the old power structures under new names. This maneuvering, often executed under the guise of "administrative efficiency," effectively shelves the promised separation of powers until after the upcoming local elections, suggesting that political calculation is trumping systemic change.

The Silence of the Intellectuals

Perhaps most alarming is the silence from the political establishment and the media. Intellectuals and critics who once demanded radical transparency have largely avoided dissecting the contradictions in the President’s statements. Rhyu Si-min’s critique serves as a rare, sharp reminder of the observer's duty: to analyze the sequence of events and the discrepancy between a leader's promises and their actions.

When the ruling camp treats reform as a political tool rather than a moral imperative, they risk alienating the very voters who put them in office. If the "reform" process continues to result only in the expansion of state organizations—costing billions of won (often millions of USD)—without addressing the core issue of unchecked power, the public perception of the administration’s credibility may be permanently damaged. The "reform" narrative is now being scrutinized not as a pursuit of justice, but as a mechanism for political self-preservation.
