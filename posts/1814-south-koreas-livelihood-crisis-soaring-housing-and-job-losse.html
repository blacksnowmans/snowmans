South Korea’s Livelihood Crisis: Soaring Housing and Job Losses Undermine Economic Reality

November 01, 2026

South Korea's working-class economy is reaching a breaking point as skyrocketing housing prices and falling real incomes hit small businesses and young workers hard despite record-breaking semiconductor exports.

  • Self-employed hardship: Average monthly real income for self-employed business owners fell to 1.44 million won (about $1,050), well below the national monthly minimum wage of 2.06 million won (about $1,500).
  • Housing cost spike: Seoul apartment price indices jumped to 118.2 within 16 months of President Lee Jae-myung taking office, increasing faster than under the previous administration of Moon Jae-in.
  • Youth job collapse: Youth employment (ages 15–29) plummeted by 177,000 jobs, while overall employment figures were sustained primarily by 300,000 jobs created for seniors aged 65 and older.
  • Deteriorating living standards: Average household real consumption spending dropped back to 2017 levels, while monthly real retail sales for small merchants fell below levels recorded during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.

The Illusion of Export Wealth vs. Local Stagnation

While the government under President Lee Jae-myung—led by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—celebrates record semiconductor exports, the domestic economy is unravelling. Real wage growth for workers has stalled against inflation, and regular job creation at small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) has dropped from over 350,000 per month under Moon Jae-in to just 53,000 under Lee. South Korea's tax redistribution system also remains near the bottom among OECD countries, with tax deductions heavily favoring high-income earners while lower-income citizens face deteriorating purchasing power.

Broken Promises on Housing and Job Quality

Housing instability has emerged as a major crisis for the government. Seoul housing prices and jeonse (lump-sum rental deposits) have surged rapidly, effectively destroying the ladder to homeownership for young adults and newly married couples. Monthly job growth under Lee has averaged 116,000—far below the 275,000 seen under Moon Jae-in or the 241,000 logged during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure—with gains concentrated almost exclusively in elderly welfare roles while productive positions for younger demographics vanish.

For the nation's 8.67 million non-regular workers, monthly wages dropped to an average of 1.76 million won (about $1,280), with temporary workers' real pay falling to levels last seen in 2013. Without structural tax reform, targeted income support, and real-estate stabilization, South Korea risks a severe domestic slump driven by crushed household spending.
