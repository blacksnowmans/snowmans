How to Lend Money to Family Without Triggering a South Korean Tax Audit

September 29, 2026

In South Korea, transferring large sums of money between family members—even as a loan—is automatically flagged by the National Tax Service (NTS), the country’s tax authority, as a taxable gift. To avoid hefty "gift tax bombs" and penalties, you must go beyond merely writing a promissory note; you must treat the transaction like a formal bank loan.

  • The Danger: The NTS views large transfers between family as gifts by default. Without rigorous documentation, you face gift taxes plus additional penalties.
  • The "Safe" Math: Under tax law, a 4.6% annual interest rate is considered "appropriate." However, you can avoid gift tax issues if the annual interest amount is less than 10 million won (about $7,000).
  • The Evidence: A promissory note is useless without a paper trail. You must document the principal, interest payments, and a realistic repayment schedule.
  • The Audit Risk: The NTS tracks how the borrower spends the money (e.g., buying property) and will audit the source of funds if the loan appears to be a disguised gift.

The "Three Pillars" of a Tax-Compliant Family Loan

To prove a loan is legitimate, you must focus on the borrower’s ability to pay, the interest rate, and the movement of money. First, the loan amount must be proportional to the borrower’s income. A common rule of thumb is that the total loan should not exceed 100 times the borrower’s monthly disposable income. If a child earns 2 million won (about $1,400) a month, a loan of 200 million won (about $140,000) is considered the upper limit of reasonable.

Second, interest is not optional just because you are family. While you aren't strictly required to charge the 4.6% rate if the total annual interest remains under 10 million won, the NTS will still look for evidence of repayment. Third, you must leave a clear digital footprint. Every monthly repayment should be made via bank transfer to create a verifiable record of the loan being serviced. Relying on "handshake deals" without bank records is effectively an invitation for a tax audit.

Proving the Loan's Validity

A promissory note acts as your primary shield during a tax audit. It should include basic identification, the principal amount, and a clear repayment schedule. While notarization is not strictly required, obtaining a "fixed date" (an official stamp from a government office or legal authority) helps prove the document wasn't fabricated after the fact to dodge taxes. If you need to extend the loan term later, ensure you update the agreement in writing rather than ignoring the original maturity date.

Furthermore, if you do charge interest, the borrower must fulfill their obligation to report withholding tax. Failing to file the necessary payment records (a "payment statement") can lead to a 1% penalty on the total payment amount. While many families skip these administrative steps to keep things simple, doing so sacrifices your only legal defense. Using the NTS’s online "Hometax" portal to formally report these transactions may feel like overkill for a family favor, but it is the only way to prove you are a compliant taxpayer in the eyes of the government.

Ultimately, treating a family loan with the same gravity as a bank loan is not just about tax avoidance; it is a lesson in financial discipline. If a child can demonstrate the ability to service a debt to their parents, they are building the habits necessary for true economic independence.
