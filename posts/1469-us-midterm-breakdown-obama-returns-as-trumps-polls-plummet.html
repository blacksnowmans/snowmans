U.S. Midterm Breakdown: Obama Returns as Trump’s Polls Plummet

October 26, 2026

With ten days until the U.S. midterm elections, former President Barack Obama’s return to the campaign trail has transformed the race into a high-stakes referendum on democratic values as Donald Trump faces a historic polling slump and mounting legal risks.

  • Polling Shift: Recent data shows the Democratic Party leading by up to 12 percentage points, while Donald Trump’s approval rating has fallen to a precarious 31%.
  • Security Variable: A report from U.S. Central Command regarding a potential strike on Iran has reached the White House, raising the possibility of a military "October Surprise" to rally conservative voters.
  • Legal Maneuvers: Trump allies have already begun filing lawsuits in battleground areas like Alaska to delay election certification, signaling a strategy of election denialism in the event of a loss.
  • Economic Stakes: Trump is touting Audi’s recent investment of about $4 billion (5.6 trillion won) as a win for his protectionist "America First" policies, while Obama warns such tactics will ultimately cripple global supply chains.

The Battle of Presidential Legacies

The re-emergence of Barack Obama has elevated this election beyond a mere legislative shuffle, turning it into a direct clash between two very different visions of the presidency. By framing the vote as a choice to end "corruption and incompetence," Obama is attempting to restore the moral standing of the U.S. presidency. This message resonates strongly with the current administration of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung. Having moved past the era of Yoon Suk-yeol 씨—whose presidency ended in impeachment and who is currently serving a prison sentence—the South Korean public and the ruling Democratic Party are particularly wary of leaders who treat public office as a tool for personal or factional gain.

Trump’s response has been characteristically populist, focusing on crowd sizes and personal insults rather than policy. However, his decision to participate in early voting—a system he previously derided as "stupid" and a "hotbed of fraud"—reveals a deep-seated anxiety within his camp. As his inner circle begins hiring defense attorneys in anticipation of post-election investigations, the Trump campaign’s rhetoric is shifting from winning a mandate to surviving a potential legal onslaught. For observers in Seoul, the parallels to the "dogmatism and lack of communication" seen during the time of Yoon Suk-yeol 씨 are a stark reminder of the fragility of democratic norms.

Geopolitical Risks and the Korean Interest

Beyond the domestic theater, the Lee Jae-myung government is bracing for significant shifts in the geopolitical and trade landscape. The reported Iran strike plan is a major concern for South Korea's Ministry of Foreign Affairs; if the Trump administration chooses military escalation to rally its base, the resulting volatility in energy markets would pose a direct threat to Korea’s economy. President Lee’s "National Interest First" diplomatic strategy is currently focused on maintaining a "Hallyu" of stable trade, but a sudden conflict in the Middle East could derail these efforts and force a difficult recalibration of Seoul’s security posture.

Economically, the gap between the two U.S. factions remains wide. While Trump celebrates high-profile investments as proof of his tariff strategy's success, the Democratic platform warns of long-term damage to the automotive industry—a sector vital to the South Korean economy. As the U.S. electorate remains cold toward Trump’s economic appeals despite slowing inflation, it suggests that voters are prioritizing stability over populist promises. For the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, the U.S. situation serves as a warning: focusing on ideological conflict and partisan warfare, rather than the practical livelihoods of citizens, is a path toward losing the public's trust.
