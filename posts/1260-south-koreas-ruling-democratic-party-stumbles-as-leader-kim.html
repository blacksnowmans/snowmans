South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party Stumbles as Leader Kim Min-seok Faces Early Crisis

October 18, 2026

Less than two months after taking the helm, Kim Min-seok, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), is fighting for his political survival amid plummeting poll numbers, intensifying factional warfare, and an embarrassing public-relations gaffe from President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

  • Sharp polling collapse: In a recent Realmeter survey, the Democratic Party’s approval rating plunged 7.0 percentage points to 35.5%—its lowest point since the launch of the Lee Jae-myung government—falling behind the conservative opposition People Power Party (41.3%), driven by double-digit drops among 40-somethings and progressive voters.
  • Unresolved factional strife: Unhealed scars from the party's leadership race have pitted Kim against allies of former representative Jung Chung-rae, who control three of the five seats on the party's decision-making Supreme Council, leaving the leadership paralyzed and sparring in internal lawsuits.
  • The "Golf TF" blunder: The presidential office poured fuel on the fire by publicly branding a working group preparing for summit diplomacy with U.S. President Donald Trump as a "Golf Task Force," sparking fierce voter anger over tone-deaf priorities during an economic squeeze.
  • Talk of emergency leadership: Frustrated by scattered task forces that deliver few tangible reforms, party elders are openly withholding their support, prompting growing chatter about dissolving Kim's leadership committee in favor of an emergency committee.

Friction at the Top and Disillusioned Supporters

The speed of Kim's descent illustrates the difficulty the Democratic Party has faced in shifting from retaking power—following the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol—to governing effectively. Rather than forging a united front with rivals inside the party, Kim has isolated key colleagues and overwhelmed lawmakers with a sprawling collection of ad-hoc task forces that produce headlines rather than concrete legislative results. Unforced errors, ranging from legal battles between prominent figures like Park Sun-won and Choe Kang-wook to awkward mishaps at party gatherings, have drained public goodwill.

The Cost of Bureaucratic Timidity

The crisis is compounded by a rigid administrative culture inside the presidential office. Setting up diplomatic preparations around Donald Trump’s personal affinity for golf is standard practice, but officially registering that effort as a "Golf TF" for public consumption is a staggering communications failure. It highlights an environment where civil servants and presidential aides are so afraid of offending superiors that no one speaks up to stop an obvious public-relations catastrophe.

Unless Kim pivots from factional maneuvering to internal conciliation, and the presidential office starts listening to blunt counsel from within its ranks, South Korea's ruling coalition risks alienating the very progressive base that put it into office.
