President Lee Jae-myung’s Late-Night Social Media Obsession

October 14, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing intense scrutiny for his round-the-clock activity on X (formerly Twitter), where his habit of posting, blocking, and unfollowing users well into the early hours of the morning has sparked concerns about his ability to focus on governing.

  • Digital fixation: Reports reveal the President maintains a frantic social media rhythm, with activity recorded at 2:00 AM, 4:00 AM, and beyond, suggesting a potential dependency on online engagement.
  • Governance concerns: Critics argue that the President’s focus on monitoring and censoring online criticism during a critical legislative audit period reflects a lack of presidential discipline.
  • Polarization: His usage of partisan slang and the tendency to amplify accounts that attack others has led to accusations that he is fueling internal conflict rather than fostering national unity.
  • Plummeting approval: With support for his administration dropping to 35% and disapproval rising to 61%, the public’s frustration is particularly high among voters in their 20s and 30s.

A Pattern of Late-Night Escalation

The President’s digital behavior has become a subject of public concern, with analysts pointing out that he spends significant energy engaging with—and blocking—anonymous social media users. While his administration markets this as "direct communication" to break down authoritarian barriers, many observers describe it as an "algorithmic prison." Prominent political commentators have warned that the President’s inability to disengage from online feuds is effectively a form of self-isolation that diminishes the dignity of his office.

Furthermore, his digital activity has real-world consequences. By citing or interacting with aggressive accounts, the President often inadvertently directs his base toward critics, leading to "coordinated attacks" on ordinary citizens. This behavior stands in stark contrast to his campaign promise to be a "president of the common people." While he adopts an aggressive, "tank-like" stance toward vulnerable small business owners or online critics, his administration has been criticized for remaining passive or deferential when dealing with powerful conglomerates and institutional interests.

Internal Friction and Political Fragility

The political landscape is also shifting as the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party) struggles with internal cohesion. The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Cho Kuk, is drawing support away from the Democratic Party, signaling a fracture within the progressive bloc. Kim Min-seok, the Democratic Party’s leader, appears to view potential allies—and even figures within his own ideological camp—as obstacles to his own path toward the next presidential election.

The administration’s strategy of using high-handed tactics to force cooperation from smaller parties has backfired, creating a toxic atmosphere of infighting rather than collaborative governance. As support wanes across nearly every demographic—including a significant portion of the youth vote—the government risks repeating the downward spiral seen during the tenure of the previous administration (referring to the era of the now-imprisoned Yun Seok-youl). If the President does not move away from his late-night digital battles to address the actual, mounting grievances of the public, his administration may find itself increasingly isolated from the very citizens it claims to represent.
