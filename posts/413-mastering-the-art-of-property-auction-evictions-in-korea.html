Mastering the Art of Property Auction Evictions in Korea

September 08, 2026

Winning a property auction in South Korea is only the first step—the real challenge is a legal and psychological hurdle known as myeongdo, the process of clearing out the existing occupant.

  • What happened: Real estate expert and accountant Jung Seung-ho successfully navigated a high-tension eviction for an officetel (a studio-apartment style building) without paying a single won in illegal moving fees.
  • How he did it: Instead of resorting to costly lawsuits or threats, he leveraged legal rights like unjust enrichment claims and offered strategic time buffers, compelling the occupant to propose their own departure date.
  • The financial upside: By keeping transition costs at zero and managing the timeline, he actually turned the vacant period into a net profit, collecting more in short-term rent than he paid in bank loan interest.
  • The final result: The property was quickly refurnished and rented out for a robust monthly cash flow of about $1,000 (1.35 million won), proving that meticulous planning beats brute-force eviction every time.

The Psychology of Eviction: Why Calm Strategy Beats Cash Payouts

Novice property investors in South Korea often panic when dealing with uncooperative occupants. Common real estate lore dictates that you must hand over a hefty cash settlement or "moving fee" to get people to leave voluntarily. However, illegal occupants with no legal lease protection have zero right to demand a payout. Handing over cash instantly destroys your return on investment and sets a dangerous precedent.

Instead of relying on threats or immediate forced evictions—which can drag on for months through the courts—savvy investors use timing and legal pressure as psychological leverage. By staggering the final balance payment date, an investor can grant a brief, controlled buffer period. This forces the occupant to realize that dragging their feet will only cost them money, turning a hostile standoff into a self-managed exit.

Turning a Squatter Into a Short-Term Cash Cow

In rare cases, rigid rules can bend if the numbers make sense. While letting an existing occupant stay is usually a red flag in auctions, Jung agreed to a temporary two-month short-term lease because the occupant fronted a solid cash deposit of about $22,000 (30 million won). This transformed a potential liability into a reliable income stream during the transition.

The math speaks for itself. Over the brief vacancy and transition period, the bank loan interest totaled about $480, but the short-term rent collected came out to about $880—leaving a net profit of $400 just from managing the eviction phase. Combined with zero relocation payouts, that saved capital was immediately redirected into about $750 worth of interior touch-ups, turning a worn-out unit into mint condition.

Ultimately, a successful auction buy is an end-to-end business operation, not just a cheap purchase. By keeping overhead low and refusing to waste money on unnecessary fees, investors can lock in strong safety margins and turn tedious legal roadblocks into predictable monthly income.
