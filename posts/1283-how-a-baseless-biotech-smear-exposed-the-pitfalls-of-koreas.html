How a Baseless Biotech Smear Exposed the Pitfalls of Korea's Wedge Politics

October 18, 2026

A resurfaced 2019 political scandal highlights how Korean politicians weaponize baseless corporate conspiracy theories to fracture opponents rather than debate policy.

  • What happened: In October 2019, former lawmaker Lee Un-ju claimed biotech firm Sillajen—which collapsed following failed drug trials—was a corrupt "beneficiary stock" tied to the liberal administration of then-President Moon Jae-in.
  • The fact-check: Financial news outlet Edaily debunked the claim, proving Sillajen's expedited Kosdaq listing occurred in December 2016 under conservative President Park Geun-hye, leaving Lee to quietly delete her social media post without an explanation or apology.
  • The underlying strategy: The incident followed a June 2019 speech where Lee explicitly argued that winning elections relies on "uniting our side while continuously dividing the opponent" through provocative narratives rather than factual debate.
  • Who is affected: Retail investors caught in corporate volatility and an electorate increasingly exhausted by factional wedge politics and unverified online rumors.

Divide-and-Conquer as an Electoral Weapon

In South Korean political engineering, dividing the opposition's base has long been a favored tactic, but Lee Un-ju’s explicit endorsement of it in 2019 laid bare how cynical the approach can be. Rather than competing over economic recovery or structural reform, political actors increasingly craft provocative narratives to force opponents into defensive infighting. Labeling a high-profile biotech failure as a presidential scandal was designed to exploit public distress for partisan gain, treating complex capital-market dynamics merely as ammunition for polarization.

Yet, relying on conspiracy theories is an inherently fragile strategy. When scrutinized against public timelines and regulatory filings, the allegation disintegrated immediately. The hasty, unannounced deletion of the social media post demonstrated the lack of accountability that often accompanies digital disinformation in Korean politics. When politicians spread falsehoods to rally hardcore supporters and quietly retreat once fact-checked, institutional trust and public discourse bear the collateral damage.

From Partisan Gimmicks to Institutional Fatigue

This episode serves as a cautionary tale for modern Korean governance under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. The subsequent era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who leaned heavily on divisive polarization before being impeached, removed from office, and imprisoned—demonstrated that governance built on hostile division rather than consensus ultimately collapses under its own weight.

Today, as the conservative People Power Party attempts to recalibrate in opposition, voters and markets show diminishing tolerance for unverified smears. With an electorate increasingly focused on practical economic metrics, the silent retreat of past conspiracy theories reminds political strategists that manufactured division cannot substitute for verifiable facts and policy vision.
