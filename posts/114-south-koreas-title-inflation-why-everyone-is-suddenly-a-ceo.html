South Korea’s "Title Inflation": Why Everyone is Suddenly a "CEO"

September 03, 2026

In contemporary South Korea, the language of daily interaction has shifted toward a phenomenon of "title inflation," where honorifics are being applied so indiscriminately that their original meaning is effectively being diluted.

  • The "CEO" phenomenon: Waiters and service staff now routinely address customers as sajangnim (CEO/boss), regardless of the customer’s actual profession.
  • Object honorifics: It has become standard to use formal, polite verb endings even when speaking to inanimate objects, such as saying a coffee "has arrived" with extreme reverence.
  • Social defense mechanism: Experts argue this is not just a change in manners, but a defensive strategy to avoid being perceived as rude in an increasingly transactional and anonymous society.
  • The "Nim" culture: While the suffix -nim (a high-level honorific) was once a tool for democratic, horizontal communication online, its migration into offline, face-to-face interactions has created a superficial barrier that keeps relationships at a safe, shallow distance.

The Burden of Social Hierarchy

The Korean language is built on a complex foundation of hierarchy. Every time a Korean speaker forms a sentence, they must perform a split-second calculation of the listener’s social status relative to their own. This linguistic "math" is a major source of fatigue in daily life. By overusing honorifics and elevating even strangers or objects to the status of a "boss," people are attempting to bypass the stress of navigating these rigid social ranks. However, this creates a paradox: when everyone is treated with the highest honorifics, the actual weight of the respect vanishes, leading to a "leveling down" of politeness.

Furthermore, the shift in how we use common terms reflects deeper social anxieties. Words like ajumma (a middle-aged woman) were once neutral descriptors but have evolved into pejoratives associated with aging and social decline. Consequently, people now carefully select "safe" titles like sajangnim or -nim to avoid offending others. In a society where a misplaced title can trigger a social firestorm, honorifics have become a form of linguistic armor.

Is Sincerity Being Lost?

This "title inflation" suggests a society struggling with a lack of genuine interpersonal trust. When we rely on hollow, high-level titles to interact, we aren't necessarily being polite; we are protecting ourselves from the vulnerability of real human connection. By masking our interactions in elaborate formalities, we keep others at arm's length, prioritizing emotional safety over authentic engagement.

Language, however, is a living thing. Just as English once moved away from complex distinctions like "thou" and "you," Korean may eventually evolve past these rigid hierarchies. Perhaps the end goal of this current linguistic inflation is to eventually reach a point where we can treat one another with basic human dignity without needing to attach a "boss" or a "sir" to every sentence. For now, however, South Korea remains a place where titles act as a buffer, helping a busy, fast-paced society navigate the discomfort of modern, anonymous living.
