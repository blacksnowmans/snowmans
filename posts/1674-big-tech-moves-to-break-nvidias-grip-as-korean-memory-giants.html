Big Tech Moves to Break Nvidia's Grip as Korean Memory Giants Ride the AI Wave

October 29, 2026

Big Tech is actively moving to break Nvidia’s monopoly on AI hardware by adopting custom chips like Google TPUs, even as extreme shortages keep memory leaders like South Korea's SK Hynix booked out three years in advance.

  • What happened: OpenAI and Oracle are expanding their hardware supply chains to include Google’s Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) alongside Nvidia GPUs, while Nvidia rushes to expand its packaging capacity by 300% to ease severe bottlenecks.
  • Memory market dominance: SK Hynix, South Korea's leading memory chipmaker, posted blowout earnings that triggered a global rally in chip stocks, backed by reports that three years worth of DRAM and High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) stock are already sold out.
  • China's forced push: State-backed ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) is pushing ahead with mass production of advanced HBM3E chips despite abysmal 20–30% yields and a failed supply deal with Apple, highlighting Beijing's drive for chip self-reliance under US sanctions.
  • Supply chain ripple effects: Taiwan’s TSMC is considering price hikes of up to 20% for advanced packaging, while South Korean equipment vendor Techwing landed a ₩9.5 billion ($7 million) inspection gear contract with Micron India.

Big Tech Fights Bottlenecks as Memory Remains King

The scramble by OpenAI and Oracle to procure Google’s TPUs marks a turning point in the AI infrastructure landscape. Tech giants are no longer willing to absorb exorbitant GPU costs or endure months-long shipment delays from Nvidia. While Nvidia is spending heavily to scale its substrate capacity by 2.5 times and packaging capacity by over 300%, alternative silicon is gaining permanent ground. For Korean chipmakers, this shift requires rapid adaptation: custom big-tech processors demand tailored memory configurations, forcing suppliers to move away from standardized GPU setups.

Meanwhile, the insatiable demand for high-bandwidth memory has handed unprecedented leverage to top-tier producers like SK Hynix. With HBM capacity effectively locked up through the next three years, pricing power has shifted entirely to chipmakers. However, this surge is squeezing downstream hardware manufacturers. Smartphone and PC makers are facing surging component costs, driving up retail prices and threatening broader consumer demand if the pricing disparity persists.

China’s Desperate Push and Global Supply Pressure

China’s determination to bypass Western tech sanctions is forcing irrational market behavior. CXMT’s decision to mass-produce HBM3E at a meager 20% to 30% yield—a fraction of SK Hynix’s efficiency—is economically unviable under standard market conditions. Enabled by government subsidies, CXMT is absorbing massive losses to build domestic capability. While Chinese chips currently lack the quality needed to win over buyers like Apple, this brute-force production strategy could eventually trigger supply glut risks if yield hurdles are resolved.

In the short term, the severe global shortage continues to distort trade flows, highlighted by the recent seizure of ₩16 billion ($12 million) worth of smuggled AI chips at an airport in Malaysia. As foundry leader TSMC leverages packaging bottlenecks to raise fees, South Korea's specialized equipment makers are finding lucrative opportunities abroad. The AI race has evolved beyond raw computing power into a high-stakes war of supply chain execution and structural cost control.
