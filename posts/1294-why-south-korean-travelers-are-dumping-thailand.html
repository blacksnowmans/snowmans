Why South Korean Travelers Are Dumping Thailand

October 19, 2026

South Korean travel to Thailand has plummeted by 25.3% as soaring local prices, aggressive new tourist taxes, and a misguided "luxury-first" policy dismantle the country's long-standing appeal as an affordable getaway.

  • A steep departure: While overall foreign arrivals to Thailand dipped around 3% from January to mid-July 2026, South Korean visitors plunged 25.3%—down roughly 50% compared to pre-pandemic 2020 levels. Nearby Vietnam saw a 5.5% drop in Korean visits over a similar period.
  • Severe price distortions: A tall Starbucks latte now costs more in Bangkok (about $4.10, or 5,500 won) than in Seoul ($3.80, or 5,200 won), despite Thailand's per capita income being less than a third of South Korea's.
  • Impending tax shocks: On top of existing airport service fees, proposed new entry and departure taxes will push total mandatory exit/entry levies to roughly $77 (102,800 won) per person, adding over $300 in fixed overhead for a typical family of four.
  • A fast pivot elsewhere: Korean outbound departures actually grew 2.7% overall in the first half of 2026, redirecting massively toward Japan (up 18.6%) and China (up 16.0%).

The Flawed "Louis Vuitton" Tourism Doctrine

Thailand's tourism slump among Koreans is the direct consequence of a bureaucratic miscalculation. In recent years, Thai authorities explicitly declared an ambition to shed the country's "cheap" image, with top officials comparing destination marketing to luxury brand Louis Vuitton and launching a 2026 slogan dubbed "Value is the New Volume." But branding an economy as premium without fixing basic urban infrastructure, transport scams, or pollution simply turned Bangkok into an overpriced disappointment for middle-class travelers.

The aggressive push to extract cash from visitors looks less like a long-term luxury strategy and more like emergency revenue collection. With Thailand's public debt rising to 67.9% of GDP and household debt exceeding 85%, domestic demand has stalled. Burdened by fiscal constraints, policymakers turned foreign arrivals into convenient cash dispensers via stacked passenger levies, forgetting that budget-conscious travelers can easily look elsewhere.

Korean Consumers Vote with Their Feet

South Korean travelers—particularly salaried workers in their twenties and thirties—are extraordinarily sensitive to the price-to-experience ratio. Southeast Asia's traditional draw was affordable pampering: inexpensive massages, lively night markets, and reasonably priced five-star hotels. When shopping malls, taxi apps, and basic dining in Bangkok began generating receipts comparable to Seoul's Gangnam district, the psychological bargain vanished.

The exodus demonstrates that modern tourists carry zero brand loyalty. With the Japanese yen remaining weak and China easing access, travelers shifted to destinations that offer transparent pricing and reliable service. Meanwhile, South Korea itself logged a record 15.05 million foreign visitors in the first eight months of 2026, driven largely by Chinese and Japanese tourists. For Southeast Asian resort hubs, the lesson is stark: raise fees without delivering equivalent quality, and Asia's most travel-hungry consumers will abandon you overnight.
