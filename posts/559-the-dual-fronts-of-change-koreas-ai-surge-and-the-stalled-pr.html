The Dual Fronts of Change: Korea’s AI Surge and the Stalled Prosecution Reform

September 10, 2026

South Korea is currently grappling with two defining challenges: the rapid encroachment of Artificial Intelligence into the human economy and a fierce political battle over the restructuring of the nation’s powerful prosecutorial system.

  • AI's Mathematical Breakthrough: Recent AI systems have solved long-standing mathematical problems that stumped human experts, signaling that Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is no longer science fiction.
  • Labor Displacement: Experts warn that as AI becomes more cost-effective than human labor, structural shifts in employment and social safety nets are inevitable.
  • Prosecutorial "Resistance": Critics argue the Ministry of Justice’s proposed "Public Prosecution Office" structure—intended to strip the Prosecution Service of its direct investigative powers—is a sham that retains bloated staffing levels and preserves the agency's political influence.
  • The Governance Gap: Both issues share a core conflict: the urgent need for democratic institutions to establish legal and ethical guardrails against powerful, unchecked entities—be they autonomous algorithms or entrenched state institutions.

The AI Frontier: Tools or Replacements?

The recent success of an AI system in solving the "Erdős–Schinzel conjecture"—a decades-old puzzle in graph theory—marks a pivotal moment. Unlike the human brain, which learns through physical synaptic changes, AI utilizes massive computational scale to explore complex possibilities. While AI has yet to master human "wisdom"—the nuances of social interaction and judgment—it has reached a tipping point where, for many specialized professional tasks, AI is already cheaper and more efficient than human labor.

This creates a critical vulnerability. As AI begins to handle sensitive tasks like cybersecurity, the potential for misuse—such as large-scale psychological manipulation or automated hacking—is immense. Experts argue that South Korea must move beyond just investing in hardware, like regional semiconductor labs, and prioritize urgent social and legal frameworks to regulate AI's integration into daily life.

The Prosecution Reform Stalemate

Simultaneously, a political firestorm is brewing over the transformation of the Prosecution Service. Opposition parties, including the Democratic Party of Korea (the liberal main opposition) and the Rebuilding Korea Party (a minor reformist party), are pushing back against the government’s reorganization plan. They argue that if the Prosecution is to be converted into a "Public Prosecution Office"—a body focused solely on indictments rather than investigations—it should logically require significantly fewer personnel.

Currently, the Prosecution maintains a massive staff of over 2,200 prosecutors. Critics point out that even with the proposed changes, the agency seeks to retain its vast headcount, including hundreds of "parliamentary prosecutors" and staff currently dispatched to domestic and international agencies like the World Bank. Skeptics see this as a "Trojan Horse": by keeping the staff in place, the prosecution maintains the capability to revert to its old, highly politicized, and investigative roots the moment the political winds shift. For many, this is a clear signal that the agency is prioritizing the preservation of its institutional power over genuine reform.

Ultimately, whether it is the rapid rise of AI or the resistance of an entrenched bureaucracy, the central question for South Korea remains: who controls the levers of power? Without strict legislative oversight and institutional guardrails, both the digital future and the justice system remain at risk of serving narrow interests rather than the public good.
