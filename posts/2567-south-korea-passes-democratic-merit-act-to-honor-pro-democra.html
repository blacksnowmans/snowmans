South Korea Passes Democratic Merit Act to Honor Pro-Democracy Activists

November 21, 2026

South Korea’s National Assembly has passed the landmark Democratic Merit Act, granting official legal status and state welfare support to 635 pro-democracy activists and their families after a 26-year legislative struggle.

  • What happened: The National Assembly passed the bill on November 20, spearheaded by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, two years after it was vetoed during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure.
  • Who is affected: A total of 635 individuals—including iconic figures of Korea’s pro-democracy movement such as Jeon Tae-il, Lee Han-yeol, and Bak Jong-chul—and their surviving families will receive official recognition alongside medical and welfare benefits.
  • What changes: The law focuses strictly on essential healthcare and elder care, explicitly excluding previously criticized perks like special university admissions or civil service hiring bonuses for activists’ children.
  • Political clash: The main opposition People Power Party boycotted the vote, denouncing the measure as a "privilege bill for political activists" and raising concerns over candidate vetting.

A Scaled-Down Bill Focused on Healthcare and Welfare

The passage of the Democratic Merit Act marks the resolution of a quarter-century legal gap for figures who contributed to South Korea’s modern democratization. While participants in specific historic events like the April 19 Revolution of 1960 or the May 18 Gwangju Uprising of 1980 already receive state honors, many general pro-democracy activists remained outside the formal veterans’ support framework. Under the newly passed bill, 635 verified victims—those killed, injured, or missing during pro-democracy movements—will finally receive state-backed medical discounts and welfare services when the law takes effect next June after a six-month preparation period.

Crucially, the legislation was stripped of its most contentious provisions. Addressing public backlash over potential reverse discrimination, the final draft eliminated extra points on civil service exams and special university entrance quotas for children of beneficiaries. Following the vote, Kwon Oh-eul, head of the Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs, held a press briefing to explicitly reassure the public that controversial educational perks were not included, emphasizing that the law provides only basic social safety net support to aging families and injured victims.

Partisan Friction and the Task of Strict Vetting

Despite the concessions, deep political division remains. Lawmakers from the conservative People Power Party boycotted the floor vote, labeling the measure a "free-ride bill for former activists." The opposition argues that the scope of recognition remains vague and warned that individuals with past violations of the National Security Act or involvement in anti-state group activities could end up receiving state honors. In response, government officials insisted that the Ministry's independent veterans review committee will conduct rigorous background checks on every candidate.

Streamlining the bill to center on basic medical and elder welfare was a pragmatic and necessary step to earn broader public consensus. Honoring those who risked their lives for Korean democracy is a fundamental obligation of the state, not a partisan bargaining chip. However, to permanently clear away accusations of political favoritism, the Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs must maintain complete transparency and strict criteria during the upcoming review process before the law takes full effect next June.
