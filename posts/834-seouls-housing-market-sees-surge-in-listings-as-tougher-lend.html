Seoul's Housing Market Sees Surge in Listings as Tougher Lending Rules Bite

October 08, 2026

  • Listings for apartments across Seoul have surpassed 70,000, driven by a dramatic near-doubling of properties for sale in affluent districts like Gangnam and Seocho.
  • The current administration, led by President Lee Jae-myung, is utilizing aggressive tax policies and lending restrictions to cool speculative buying and shift market sentiment from holding to selling.
  • Experts warn that lending curbs alone are insufficient, arguing that the government must pair them with stricter jeonse (lump-sum rent) loan controls and expanded public housing supply to prevent a market rebound early next year.

Gangnam Listings Surge as Policy Shifts Market Psychology

South Korea's high-stakes real estate market is flashing new warning signs for speculators. In wealthy southern Seoul districts like Gangnam and Seocho, the number of properties listed for sale has skyrocketed—jumping from roughly 6,000 units to 11,000 in Gangnam, and from 4,900 to 9,300 in Seocho. This surge pushes total apartment listings across Seoul past the symbolic 70,000 mark.

Unlike previous market cycles where heavier property and comprehensive real estate taxes only fueled further price hikes, today's buyers and sellers are reacting differently. Faced with tighter lending rules under the Lee Jae-myung administration and diminishing expectations of infinite price growth, owners are increasingly rushing to offload properties. This shift indicates that state pressure is successfully denting speculative confidence, though non-Gangnam areas remain a battleground of nerves between anxious buyers and stubborn sellers.

The Golden Time for Structural Reform

While strict lending limits can temporarily freeze the market, analysts note that the underlying addiction to cheap credit remains a major risk. A brief relaxation of loan caps earlier this year triggered an immediate surge in demand, proving that liquidity could easily rush back in if the government lets its guard down. Observers argue that the current administration faces a crucial "golden time" to enact deeper reforms.

To lock in market stability, loan tightening must be accompanied by structural changes—specifically, curbing excessive jeonse rental loans that inflate purchase prices, and aggressively expanding affordable housing models like land-lease homes. Without a reliable supply of low-cost alternatives for first-time buyers, any temporary lull in prices risks giving way to another speculative frenzy when credit loosens again.
