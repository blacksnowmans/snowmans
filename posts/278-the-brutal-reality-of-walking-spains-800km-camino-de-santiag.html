The Brutal Reality of Walking Spain's 800km Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Completing the legendary 36-day, 800-kilometer pilgrimage across northern Spain is less of a romantic bucket-list adventure and more of a gritty daily survival test against blisters, unpredictable weather, and gear failure.

    What happened: A South Korean traveler documented a 36-day trek from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in France to Santiago de Compostela in Spain, sharing hard-earned practical survival lessons. What it changes: It strips away the romanticized myth of the pilgrimage, forcing prospective travelers to budget carefully (about 2 million won, or roughly $1,400 to $1,500, excluding flights) and pack strictly for survival rather than luxury. Who is affected: Outdoor enthusiasts, solo travelers, and South Koreans—who consistently rank among the top nationalities completing the route—looking to tackle the trail without falling victim to preventable injuries.

Gear Failures and the Myth of Minimalist Packing

Carrying a 30-liter backpack is a double-edged sword. While a smaller bag physically forces travelers to pack light, extreme conditions can push gear past its limits—such as frame supports snapping mid-trek and forcing hikers to rely on duct tape. Essential survival items cannot be compromised: a sturdy poncho with arm holes is mandatory to battle unpredictable weather, while trekking poles with rubber stoppers are critical for protecting knees on steep descents. South Korean travelers also face shifting currency exchange rates that directly impact everyday expenses, making self-catering meals at local grocery stores a vital strategy to keep food costs down to just 3 to 4 euros per meal.

Beyond the Bucket List: Finding Inner Resilience

Completing the pilgrimage does not magically rewrite a person's life overnight, nor does it permanently melt away pounds gained back home. Instead, it builds an internal muscle: the quiet confidence that comes from moving forward slowly and steadily through freezing mornings, scorching afternoons, and sheer exhaustion. In a modern culture obsessed with rapid, instant results and curated social media feeds, the Camino offers a brutal yet grounding counter-narrative. It reminds travelers that even when blisters throb and storms rage, putting one foot in front of the other is all it takes to reach the destination.
