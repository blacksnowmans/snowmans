Why Saudi Arabia Can’t Win Its War—and Why Asia Will Pay the Price

October 17, 2026

Saudi Arabia’s high-tech military has become trapped in an asymmetric quagmire against Yemen’s Houthi rebels, largely because the United States—now energy-independent—has quietly abandoned its decades-old commitment to protect Gulf oil monarchies.

  • Asymmetric disruption: Armed with low-cost drones and ballistic missiles, Houthi rebels have struck Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport and oil facilities, exposing the vulnerability of Saudi Arabia’s expensive Western-supplied defenses.
  • The American pivot: The U.S. share of Middle Eastern oil imports has plunged from 17% in 2001 to just 3% by 2025, effectively rendering the 1980 Carter Doctrine obsolete and leaving Washington with little appetite to deploy ground forces.
  • Economic reckoning: Facing an annual fiscal deficit of roughly $75 billion (100 trillion won), Riyadh has been forced to slash Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman’s flagship Neom project—scaling back "The Line" from 170 km to just 2.5 km—and return hosting rights for major international events.
  • Shifting burden to Asia: While Washington extracts itself, East Asian economies remain critically exposed: China, India, South Korea, and Japan account for over 60% of all oil moving through the Strait of Hormuz, while the U.S. accounts for only 2.5%.

The End of the Carter Doctrine

When Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman launched the Yemen intervention in 2015, Riyadh assumed modern airpower would crush the insurgency within weeks. A decade later, the conflict has proved that air campaigns cannot conquer adversaries who control territory housing over 28 million people and operate with a "nothing to lose" doctrine. Each strike on Saudi airports and petrochemical plants inflicts outsized economic damage on the kingdom, while the rebels face few comparable assets at risk.

More damaging to Riyadh is Washington’s cold calculus. The U.S. shale revolution has transformed global energy flows. When Saudi processing facilities were struck in 2019, Washington demurred; today, the Trump administration has opted for pragmatic deals in Oman to shield U.S.-flagged vessels from Houthi attacks while refusing to mount broader offensive campaigns on Riyadh’s behalf. U.S. strategy has pivoted to insulation rather than intervention, leaving its regional partners to fend for themselves.

Neom’s Retrenchment and the Exposure of Asian Importers

The strategic failure in Yemen is derailing Saudi Arabia’s economic transformation. Riyadh requires crude prices between $90 and $110 per barrel to balance its state budget. Instead, ballooning military outlays and volatile oil markets have hollowed out sovereign reserves, forcing the postponement of premier construction projects and the withdrawal of bids like the 2029 Asian Winter Games.

For South Korea and its neighbors, the implications are severe. South Korean engineering firms once eyed massive windfalls from Neom, but veterans of the region now caution against committing capital to an active conflict zone. More critically, South Korea depends on the Strait of Hormuz for 12% of its energy supply. While Seoul’s current administration under President Lee Jae-myung has shifted toward a pragmatic, national-interest-driven diplomacy to navigate volatile Middle Eastern logistics—stepping away from the rigid ideological diplomacy seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure—the fundamental vulnerability remains. Washington no longer has the incentive to police the Persian Gulf, and the bill for regional instability will ultimately land on Asian consumers.
