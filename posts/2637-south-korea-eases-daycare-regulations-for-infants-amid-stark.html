South Korea Eases Daycare Regulations for Infants Amid Stark Regional Disparities

November 23, 2026

South Korea’s Ministry of Education has revised daycare regulations to allow facilities in high-demand suburban areas to operate exclusively for infants, aiming to relieve severe nursery waiting lists for young families.

  • What happened: The Ministry of Education amended the enforcement rules of the Infant Care Act, allowing daycares in high-demand areas to run infant-only facilities while mandating 40 hours of retraining for disqualified teachers seeking license reinstatement.
  • What it changes: Daycare operators in newly developed residential towns can now dedicate their entire capacity to infants under age two without being forced to maintain under-enrolled toddler classes.
  • Who is affected: Parents in rapidly growing suburbs gain better access to infant care, though severe staffing shortages, safety hazards, and funding gaps persist in other districts.

Regulatory Flexibility vs. Local Realities

Easing rules for infant care is an overdue fix for working parents in South Korea's newly built suburban towns. Under previous rules, daycare operators faced rigid mandates requiring them to maintain facilities for older toddlers alongside infant care, even when local neighborhood demand was concentrated almost entirely on babies under age two. Allowing infant-only daycares gives operators the flexibility to match real demographics. Simultaneously, requiring 40 hours of mandatory retraining for daycare teachers returning from license revocations provides a necessary safety check as facility access expands.

Persistent Gaps in Local Childcare Safety and Services

However, nationwide deregulation cannot single-handedly solve South Korea's fractured childcare network. While fast-growing suburbs face nursery shortages, rural and aging districts present stark operational imbalances—such as one facility highlighted in Iksan with just two enrolled children managed by five staff members. Meanwhile, home-visit childcare services remain overwhelmed; in Incheon’s Bupyeong District, 89 families remain on waitlists averaging 94 days due to an acute shortage of home caregivers.

Physical safety and funding equity also remain unaddressed at the municipal level. Local council members recently criticized Cheonan city for allocating zero budget to remove hazardous asbestos from local daycares and community child centers. Furthermore, Gyeonggi Province continues to face criticism over funding disparities that provide higher meal subsidies to public kindergartens than to neighborhood daycares. As South Korea pushes to fully integrate early childhood education and care systems by 2026, administrative flexibilities will yield limited results unless local governments resolve basic safety hazards and regional service bottlenecks.
