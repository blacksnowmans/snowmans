The New Tri-Polar Order: Trump, Xi, and Putin Sign "Peace Declaration" in Shenzhen

November 20, 2026

US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin have signed a historic trilateral "Peace Declaration" in Shenzhen, signaling a radical shift toward a "Great Power" consensus that could reshape global security and the United Nations.

  • A Trilateral Breakthrough: The 90-minute meeting—the first of its kind—produced a four-point agreement focused on nuclear war prevention, energy security, and a total overhaul of the United Nations system.
  • Nuclear De-escalation: The three leaders reaffirmed that "a nuclear war cannot be won," pledging to create a direct trilateral channel to manage nuclear risks and curb proliferation in states like Iran.
  • Transactional Diplomacy: President Trump leveraged the summit to pressure China into massive agricultural purchases while finding common ground with President Xi on stabilizing the Strait of Hormuz to secure oil supplies.
  • The Return of Realpolitik: The leaders agreed to reform the UN to end the current "paralysis," a move critics fear will allow the three powers to bypass international law and dictate terms to smaller nations.

The Return of Great Power Politics

The Shenzhen summit marks the end of post-Cold War multilateralism and the birth of a new "Tri-Polar" order. By meeting in a trilateral format, Trump, Xi, and Putin have effectively signaled that the world’s most pressing issues—from the war in Ukraine to global inflation—will now be settled via "direct deals" between the three giants. This transactional approach was most evident in Trump’s 40-minute side meeting with Putin, where the two discussed ending the Ukraine conflict, though they stopped short of a definitive territorial agreement.

For China, the summit was a major diplomatic victory. President Xi Jinping, acting as host in the symbol of Chinese reform, Shenzhen, positioned himself as the "mediator-in-chief." By proposing a four-point principle for global harmony, Xi successfully balanced Trump’s "America First" protectionism with a facade of global stability. However, the underlying tension remains: while they agree on preventing a nuclear catastrophe, the competition for technological and military dominance between Washington and Beijing continues unabated.

Risks for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

The "Shenzhen Declaration" places the government of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea’s current leader from the liberal Democratic Party, in a precarious position. As the three powers move toward a "New Yalta" style of diplomacy—where they decide the fate of regional spheres of influence—Seoul faces the risk of being marginalized. If Trump, Xi, and Putin reach a grand bargain on nuclear non-proliferation, the future of the Korean Peninsula could be decided without South Korea’s direct input.

Furthermore, the push for UN reform and the agreement to limit the "abuse" of the International Criminal Court (ICC) suggests a world where "might makes right." This is a stark departure from the rules-based order that middle powers like South Korea rely on. While the "Peace Declaration" may lower the immediate temperature of global conflict, it leaves the Lee Jae-myung administration with a difficult task: ensuring that South Korea’s security and economic interests aren't traded away in a boardroom in Shenzhen.

"When the great powers agree, the world finds peace; when they clash, the world finds chaos." — President Xi Jinping during the Shenzhen Summit.
