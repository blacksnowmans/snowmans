President Lee Jae-myung Omits Moon Jae-in in Late-Night Call to End “Leader Worship”

November 08, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung has signaled a deepening rift within South Korea’s liberal establishment by pointedly excluding his predecessor, Moon Jae-in, from a late-night social media message urging supporters to avoid "worshiping" political leaders.

  • The Omission: In a 1:00 AM post, President Lee quoted former liberal presidents Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun on the importance of citizen participation but completely ignored Moon Jae-in, the most recent liberal leader.
  • Approval Crisis: The move comes as Lee’s approval rating hit a record low of 38%, with disapproval climbing to 51% according to recent Gallup Korea polling.
  • Demographic Shift: For the first time, Lee’s support among the "40-50 bracket"—the traditional backbone of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—has seen a "dead cross," where negative sentiment now outweighs the positive.
  • Strategic Pivot: Analysts view the message as an attempt to consolidate Lee’s personal power and distance his administration from the lingering popularity of Moon Jae-in.

Cracks in the Liberal Foundation

The "Moon Jae-in passing" phenomenon reflects President Lee’s growing psychological insecurity as his administration struggles with falling public trust. While Lee framed his message as a high-minded defense of democratic principles—warning that "praise should not become adoration"—the timing suggests a more defensive motive. Just a day prior, a massive crowd gathered to cheer for former President Moon at a public event, highlighting that Moon’s "fandom" remains a potent force that Lee has yet to fully capture or inherit. By invoking the legacies of Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun while erasing Moon, Lee is attempting to claim the mantle of liberal legitimacy for himself alone.

The polling data from Gallup Korea underscores why the President is anxious. Beyond the 38% headline figure, the loss of the 40s and 50s age groups is a flashing red light for the ruling DPK. Disapproval in Seoul has reached 60%, largely driven by frustration over cabinet appointments and economic stagnation. For example, Lee’s nominee for Gender Equality Minister, Yong Hye-in, was forced to withdraw after a disastrous 13% favorability rating. When a president's core base begins to fracture, the typical response is to tighten control over the internal narrative, which explains the "warning shot" fired at those still loyal to the previous administration.

The Psychology of Power and Diversionary Tactics

Influential liberal commentator and author Rhyu Si-min recently noted that top leaders rarely feel "lonely" in the way average citizens do; instead, they feel anger and annoyance when their will is thwarted. Lee's call to end "worship" is likely a manifestation of this frustration—a "political signaling" intended to discipline a camp that is looking elsewhere for inspiration. It is deeply ironic that the President is lecturing his base on the dangers of cult-like followings, given that his own "New Lee Jae-myung" (Nyu-Ijaemyeong) supporters are frequently criticized for their aggressive, uncompromising loyalty to him over the party's broader interests.

To regain momentum, the Lee administration and the DPK are now pushing for a "Special Prosecution for Fabricated Indictments." This move aims to investigate the actions of the prosecution during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently imprisoned. However, this looks like a classic diversionary tactic. Rather than addressing the economic concerns of the middle class, the government is retreating into partisan warfare to rouse its base. President Lee must realize that avoiding the shadow of Moon Jae-in will not hide his own administration's shortcomings. Real leadership requires self-reflection, not just the erasure of one's predecessors.
