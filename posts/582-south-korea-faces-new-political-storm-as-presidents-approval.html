South Korea Faces New Political Storm as President's Approval Plummets Over Controversial Prosecutor Nominee

October 02, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean President Yoon Suk Yeol's approval ratings have hit historic lows, driven sharply downward by public backlash over his nomination of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the newly created Serious Crime Investigation Agency (SCIA).
  • The core controversy: Kim is a career prosecutor with a record of resisting past prosecutorial reforms and protecting former President Yoon during high-profile political battles, leading critics to denounce his appointment as putting the "fox in charge of the henhouse."
  • Who is affected: The administration's core support base—particularly voters in their 30s, where disapproval ratings now exceed 70%—is fracturing, leaving the government increasingly isolated.

A Freefall in Public Trust and the Backlash from Core Supporters

Recent polling paints a grim picture for the South Korean administration, with disapproval hitting critical thresholds across multiple surveys. Rather than responding with course correction, the presidential office has doubled down on a go-it-alone approach, dismissing public outcry and warnings from allied lawmakers as obstruction. This refusal to engage in democratic dialogue risks alienating even sympathetic voters who warn that pushing through polarizing figures like Kim will only accelerate the government's political decline.

The nomination of Kim Ji-yong to lead the SCIA — an agency ostensibly created to strip unchecked powers from the traditional prosecution service — has laid bare a profound structural contradiction. Lawmakers and civic critics point out that appointing a bureaucrat known for defending prosecutorial privilege amounts to a bureaucratic whitewash rather than genuine reform. Past controversies shadowing Kim, ranging from his involvement in handling sensitive investigative cases to allegations of covering up misconduct, make his Senate-style confirmation hearings a critical flashpoint for an administration already bleeding public confidence.

Reform in Name Only: The Danger of Recycling the Past

The core grievance animating this crisis is not merely about a single personnel choice, but about the integrity of South Korea's long-sought democratic reforms. When architects of past institutional resistance are recycled into leaders of reform agencies, the exercise ceases to be about justice and becomes a cosmetic makeover for entrenched power. As public opposition mounts and approval ratings hover near historic nadirs, the administration's insistence on ramming through contested appointments threatens to deepen the nation's political polarization and permanently erode its reform mandate.
