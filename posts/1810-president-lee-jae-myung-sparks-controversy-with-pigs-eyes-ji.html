President Lee Jae-myung Sparks Controversy with “Pig’s Eyes” Jibe at Opposition

November 01, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung has triggered a fierce political standoff after using a provocative historical proverb to dismiss critics who have questioned the frequency and cost of his overseas diplomatic trips.

  • The “Pig” Remark: Defending his travel schedule, President Lee used a proverb suggesting that “pigs only see pigs,” implying his critics are too biased to recognize the national interest.
  • “Nuisance Golf” Allegations: Representative Jang Dong-hyeok of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, retaliated by labeling the first family a “nuisance family” for golfing during national tragedies.
  • Health vs. Leisure: While the President claimed his schedule is so grueling that he requires IV drips and suffers nosebleeds, the opposition pointed to his drumming and social activities in Japan as evidence of hypocrisy.
  • Eroding Decorum: The exchange highlights a deepening divide in Korean politics, where policy debate is increasingly replaced by personal insults and character assassination.

Perception Wars and the “Pig’s Eyes” Comment

The controversy began on October 31 when President Lee Jae-myung took to social media to address mounting criticism of his frequent foreign tours. He cited a famous anecdote involving the monk Muhak and King Taejo (the founder of the Joseon Dynasty), which essentially argues that the world looks like a Buddha to a Buddha and like a pig to a pig. By using this metaphor, the President effectively told his detractors that they only see his trips as a waste of money because of their own petty perspectives.

The People Power Party (PPP) immediately condemned the remark as an insult to the public, questioning whether a national leader should be comparing citizens or political rivals to swine. While the President’s office likely intended the comment to be a witty defense of diplomatic "hard work," it has instead backfired. Critics argue that instead of providing a transparent breakdown of diplomatic achievements or expenditures, the President chose to shut down dialogue by questioning the "eyes" of those tasked with holding him accountable.

“Nuisance Golf” and the Battle of Narratives

The opposition’s counterattack was led by Representative Jang Dong-hyeok, who shifted the focus from the President’s words to his family’s actions. Jang accused the first family of engaging in “nuisance golf,” claiming they were out on the links even while the nation was reeling from reports of missing soldiers and building collapses. This line of attack is particularly potent in Korea, where the conduct of a leader’s family during times of national mourning or crisis is a major barometer of moral authority.

Furthermore, Jang took aim at the President’s attempt to garner sympathy. President Lee had claimed his diplomatic efforts were so taxing that he frequently required IV drips to recover from exhaustion and nosebleeds. Jang mocked this narrative by highlighting the President’s more visible leisure activities abroad, such as playing drums with the Japanese Prime Minister and taking selfies with actors. This framing suggests that the "suffering leader" persona is merely a political show, further cooling the atmosphere between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the opposition.

A Bitter Political Climate

This spat is more than just a war of words; it reflects the "zero-sum" nature of current Korean politics. Since the imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol following his impeachment, the political landscape has remained hyper-polarized. The opposition now speaks of “judicial destruction” and “betrayal of the people,” while the President’s camp views any criticism as a biased attempt to sabotage the administration’s foreign policy successes.

When political discourse descends into labeling the opposition as “pigs” and the first family as a “nuisance,” the space for compromise disappears. For international observers and market participants, this suggests that legislative gridlock is likely to persist, as the two major parties are currently more interested in scoring rhetorical points than in collaborative governance.
