Trump's WWII Nostalgia and the Realities of Oil Hegemony

September 28, 2026

As US-China rivalry reshapes global markets, leaders from Washington to Beijing are trading sudden diplomatic pleasantries while fiercely guarding their economic interests—leaving export-reliant nations like South Korea to navigate a dangerous geopolitical tightrope.

  • What happened: US President Donald Trump raised eyebrows at a White House welcoming ceremony by invoking the "Flying Tigers"—the American volunteer pilots who fought alongside China during World War II—as part of a broader push to manage relations ahead of the US presidential election.
  • The oil connection: China remains the ultimate swing buyer of global crude, capable of swinging prices and threatening economies worldwide if US-China tensions weaponize energy markets.
  • The Korean angle: Analysts argue that Seoul's continued commitment to trilateral military drills with the US and Japan—initiated under the previous administration—has unnecessarily locked North Korea and China into a hardline stance, shrinking South Korea's own diplomatic leverage.
  • Middle East back-channels: Even isolated states like Iran are quietly using intermediaries like Qatar to signal willingness to negotiate with Washington, driven by crushing inflation and domestic political survival.

Trilateral Drills and the Cost of Rigid Alignment

Foreign policy is rarely about grand ideology; it is a brutal calculus of survival. While international panels debate Tokyo's historical revisionism, South Korea sits uncomfortably quiet, caught between avoiding friction with traditional allies and absorbing the security fallout of close-knit military exercises with Japan. Critics note that dialing back rigid trilateral military drills could offer Beijing and Pyongyang a much-needed off-ramp, opening doors for renewed diplomacy instead of locking the Korean Peninsula into a permanent cycle of confrontation.

At the same time, the broader economic ecosystem—from Mexican supply-chain realignments to Qatari back-channel diplomacy with Iran—demonstrates that nations survive by leveraging pragmatism over posture. For South Korea, clinging blindly to rigid bloc politics without demanding strategic flexibility risks turning the country into a bystander on someone else's chessboard.

Energy, Inflation, and the Necessity of Realism

Underneath every theatrical handshake between superpowers lies a raw panic over inflation and energy security. Whether it is China controlling crude imports or oil prices threatening to breach $100 a barrel, domestic wallets dictate foreign policy. If energy markets spike, ruling parties fall.

For Seoul, the lesson is stark. South Korea cannot afford to view Washington and Beijing's power struggles as a spectator sport. Navigating these turbulent waters requires dropping emotional alignments in favor of cold, calculated pragmatism—ensuring the country builds enough diplomatic flexibility to protect its own economic lifeblood before external shocks hit home.
