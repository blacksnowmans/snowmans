The Invisible Chains of South Korea’s Judiciary

October 28, 2026

South Korea’s judiciary is facing growing scrutiny as critics argue that a rigid, hierarchical structure—rather than the rule of law—continues to dictate the nation's legal outcomes. While the public has long focused on reforming the powerful prosecution service, legal experts and political analysts are now calling for a long-overdue overhaul of the court system itself, starting with the deeply entrenched power of the Supreme Court and the National Court Administration.

  • The "Patronage" Problem: Supreme Court Justices are nominated by the Chief Justice, creating a culture of deep dependency and silence among the highest-ranking judges.
  • Structural Reform: Analysts argue for the abolition of the National Court Administration, the bureaucracy that manages judge assignments and budgets, which many see as the primary tool for internal control.
  • Political Hesitation: Despite holding a majority in the National Assembly, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal party) is being criticized for its reluctance to pursue aggressive judicial reform for fear of a political backlash.
  • Calls for Participation: There is an increasing demand for the introduction of a "lay judge" or jury system to break the monopoly that career judges hold over courtroom decisions.

A Culture of Deference

The core of the issue lies in how judges reach their positions. Unlike junior judges, who operate within a standard hierarchy, Supreme Court Justices are directly nominated by the Chief Justice. This creates a "benefactor-beneficiary" relationship that discourages dissent. In the Supreme Court’s plenary sessions, it is an open secret that the Chief Justice’s stance effectively becomes the majority opinion, stifling the legal debate that is supposed to be the hallmark of the highest court in the land.

Legal analyst and lawyer Jung Pil-seung, a prominent voice on these issues, points out that while the prosecution is known for its aggressive, militaristic organizational culture, the judiciary is essentially a conservative, bureaucratic machine. Because judges are less prone to collective political "combat" than prosecutors, proponents of reform argue that a clear legislative push could dismantle these power structures much faster than many anticipate.

The Cost of Political Caution

There is mounting frustration among the ruling party's base. Despite the legislative power to initiate reforms—such as dismantling the National Court Administration—the party has instead focused on endless political maneuvering and parliamentary inquiries. Critics argue that this hesitation is a strategic error; by waiting for the opposition People Power Party (the main conservative party) to cooperate, the ruling party is losing its "golden time" for reform and alienating centrist voters who are tired of seeing legal rulings that defy common sense.

As long as the judiciary remains a "sanctuary" that is untouchable by democratic oversight, the influence of internal cliques and the weight of subjective rulings—often seen in high-profile cases involving political figures—will continue to erode public trust. The consensus among reformers is clear: the path to a fair legal system starts with breaking the internal chains that bind the judiciary to its own elite hierarchy.
