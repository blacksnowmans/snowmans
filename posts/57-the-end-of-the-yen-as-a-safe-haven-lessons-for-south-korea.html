The End of the Yen as a Safe Haven: Lessons for South Korea

September 02, 2026

The Japanese yen, once the go-to "safe haven" for investors during global economic turbulence, has transformed into a high-risk liability, signaling potential systemic fractures that South Korea must urgently heed.

  • The Myth Shattered: The yen is no longer a stable asset due to Japan's long-term decline in manufacturing competitiveness.
  • The Boomerang Effect: Japan's strategy of maintaining a weak yen to boost exports has backfired, fueling imported inflation and crushing domestic purchasing power.
  • South Korea’s Vulnerability: Like Japan, South Korea faces a structural risk where export giants keep earnings in foreign currencies abroad rather than repatriating them, leaving the won exposed.
  • The Specter of Bank Runs: Financial analysts are now stress-testing for extreme scenarios, fearing that if confidence in currency systems collapses, public panic—reminiscent of a bank run—could follow.

The Structural Collapse of the Yen

Japan’s economic woes stem from a fundamental shift in its industrial base. In decades past, Japanese products dominated global markets, forcing international demand for the yen. Today, Japanese corporations have offshored their production bases. Because these companies no longer repatriate their profits to Japan, the yen has lost its core source of strength: the domestic accumulation of national wealth.

Compounding this is the "curse" of ultra-low interest rates. In a desperate bid to stimulate domestic demand, the Bank of Japan kept rates near zero for years. However, as an import-dependent nation for energy and raw materials, this policy devalued the currency and triggered runaway inflation. Japanese households are now trapped: the government cannot raise rates without risking a sovereign debt crisis, yet failing to act allows the yen to continue its slide toward irrelevance.

Is Korea Next in Line?

South Korea’s economy is dancing near the same precipice. The won has seen increased volatility, with exchange rates frequently hovering around 1,400 won to the dollar. A critical issue is that South Korea’s major exporters have become increasingly detached from the domestic financial system. When these firms hold their profits in overseas accounts to avoid exchange rate risks, the domestic supply of dollars dries up, leaving the won vulnerable to even minor geopolitical shocks.

This is not just an academic concern; it is a matter of systemic stability. If the South Korean public loses faith in the won’t strength, the rush to convert savings into "hard" assets like gold or U.S. dollars could trigger a domestic banking crisis. We must stop viewing the exchange rate as a mere number and start seeing it as a reflection of our economic foundations—foundations that currently show signs of fatigue.

My Take: The "safe haven" narrative is a relic of the past. Relying on local currency alone is now a high-stakes gamble. As Japan’s experience proves, when a government’s economic policies fail to keep pace with global realities, the individual is left to fend for themselves. Diversifying assets globally is no longer just a strategy for growth—it has become a necessary defensive posture for survival in an era of waning national economic sovereignty.
