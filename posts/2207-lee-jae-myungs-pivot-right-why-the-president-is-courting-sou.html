Lee Jae-myung’s Pivot Right: Why the President Is Courting South Korea’s Old Guard

November 11, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea's liberal leader who built his brand on defending the vulnerable, faces fierce criticism from his own base for abandoning progressive reforms to forge alliances with conservative elites.

  • A sharp ideological shift: After winning the presidency following the impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol, President Lee has distanced himself from progressive champions and his core grassroots supporters in favor of centrist and conservative outreach.
  • Recruiting the old guard: Lee's administration is actively courting figures from the conservative establishment—including veterans of the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP)—to build what critics describe as an entrenched, Japan-style ruling coalition.
  • Scapegoating the vulnerable: To court struggling blue-collar voters amid worsening youth employment, Lee is accused of borrowing right-wing populist rhetoric that blames migrant workers for wage stagnation and job scarcity.
  • Erosion of governance: Alienating the passionate progressive base that propelled Lee to office has weakened presidential defending power, leaving the administration increasingly isolated as the conservative opposition gains ground.

From Reformist Populist to Power-Sharing Realist

Lee Jae-myung originally rose to national prominence as mayor of Seongnam and later as a defiant outsider in the liberal Democratic Party of Korea. His guiding slogan was eokgang buyak—"suppressing the strong to help the weak." Yet critics argue that this egalitarian mantle was merely an opportunistic tactic. Confronted with the harsh realities of presidential politics following his narrow 2022 loss and the subsequent political chaos that led to Yoon's ouster, Lee appears to have concluded that pure progressive ideology cannot sustain a ruling majority.

Rather than pushing structurally divisive reforms such as overhaul of the prosecution or dismantling chaebol-centric economic practices, Lee has chosen accommodation. Observers note that his pivot reflects a cynical view of statecraft: a belief that real enduring authority lies with an organized, entrenched minority rather than an unorganized mass public. This logic has driven Lee to bring conservative policy veterans into his orbit, seeking a grand, durable coalition akin to Japan's Liberal Democratic Party.

The Cost of Abandoning the Base

This calculated turn to the center has created immense friction within the ruling camp. Reformist lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae and Kim Yong-min are increasingly treated as liabilities, and dedicated grassroots activists who once fiercely defended the president online find themselves cast aside. Simultaneously, by pursuing chaebol-friendly policies even as youth jobs fall for consecutive years, Lee's administration has failed to deliver tangible economic relief to ordinary citizens.

The political gamble appears to be misfiring. Courting conservative power brokers will rarely convince conservative voters to abandon their traditional home, allowing the People Power Party to reap public discontent for free. By discarding his loyal core to chase an elusive, conservative-leaning majority, Lee risks losing both sides: alienating the passionate movement that built him while failing to win the genuine trust of the establishment he now courts.
