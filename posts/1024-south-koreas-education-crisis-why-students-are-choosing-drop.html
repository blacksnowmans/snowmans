South Korea’s Education Crisis: Why Students Are Choosing Dropout Over High School

October 12, 2026

South Korea’s hyper-competitive education system has pushed students to a breaking point, where dropping out of high school is no longer viewed as a failure, but as a strategic "reset" to focus entirely on the university entrance exam, the Suneung.

  • Record Dropouts: High school dropouts have surged from approximately 12,800 in 2021 to over 18,600 in 2025, with first-year high school students accounting for more than 10,000 of that figure for the first time.
  • The "New" Grading System: The transition to a 5-tier grade system, intended to reduce competition, has paradoxically increased pressure by making the top "1st grade" bracket feel deceptively attainable, leading students to view any mistake as catastrophic.
  • The "N-su" Phenomenon: The rise of N-su—the practice of failing and retaking the entrance exam multiple times—has turned the affluent Daechi-dong district into a massive education industrial complex, with roughly 1,600 academies feeding on parental anxiety.
  • Economic Stratification: Preparing for these exams has become a financial burden, with annual private education costs for N-su students estimated at 3 trillion won (about $2.1 billion), effectively turning top-tier university admissions into a privilege for the wealthy.

The Illusion of the "Reset"

The current educational landscape is defined by a desperate, high-stakes gamble. Students are increasingly abandoning the school system to engage in "Seon-jaesu"—dropping out to study full-time for the Suneung via private cram schools (hagwons). They view the standard curriculum as an obstacle to their singular goal: admission to medical school or a top-tier university. This is not mere ambition; it is a symptom of a system where a single B-grade can effectively disqualify a student’s lifelong prospects.

Government officials argue that these trends are fueled by the aggressive marketing of private education firms, but the reality on the ground suggests a deeper systemic failure. When students view their high school years as a "rehearsal" for a later, more perfect attempt at the exam, the school ceases to be a place of learning and becomes merely a hurdle to be bypassed. This "Golden Ticket Syndrome"—the belief that only a degree from a prestigious university guarantees a stable life—is devastating the mental health of youth and contributing to the nation's broader demographic collapse.

A Cycle of Structural Inequality

The cost of this pursuit is staggering. In Daechi-dong, "hotel-style" boarding academies charge upwards of 4 million won (about $2,800) per month, creating a environment where a student’s success is directly correlated to their family’s net worth. While some students sacrifice their health and teenage years working part-time jobs just to afford rent in small studio apartments near these centers, they are competing against peers whose paths are paved by high-spending parents.

Although political power has shifted—with the administration of President Lee Jae-myung now overseeing a landscape left behind by the disgraced, imprisoned former leader, Yoon Seok-yeol—the "education monster" remains unchanged. It has merely evolved, becoming more sophisticated and predatory. As long as academic pedigree remains the sole, rigid metric of success in South Korean society, the cycle of endless exam retakes and the commodification of education will continue to erode the potential of the next generation.
