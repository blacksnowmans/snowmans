Seoul House Prices Rise for 86 Straight Weeks: Why Government Warnings Are Failing

September 24, 2026

  • Seoul apartment prices have risen for 86 consecutive weeks despite aggressive government warnings of a potential market crash and auction crisis.
  • Historically, soaring Korean real estate has only been cooled by concrete, large-scale supply that convinced buyers they could wait for cheaper homes—such as the 1980s New Towns under the Roh Tae-woo administration or the 2000s half-price housing under Lee Myung-bak.
  • Today's market faces an extreme supply drought, with meaningful new units in Seoul unlikely until 2030 or later, rendering current government promises ineffective.
  • Rather than speculative greed, the current buying wave is driven by "survival buying"—ordinary citizens purchasing apartments around the $700,000 to $1 million mark out of fear of getting priced out or trapped in volatile rental scams.

History Shows Supply Beats Regulation, But Seoul Has None Left

Korea's property market has weathered massive bubbles before, notably during the late-1980s "three lows" economic boom and the mid-2000s pre-financial crisis peak. In both eras, skyrocketing prices only surrendered when massive, physical supply shocked buyer psychology. When the Roh Tae-woo government announced the construction of 2 million homes in new satellite cities like Bundang and Ilsan, or when the Lee Myung-bak administration later introduced "half-price" state housing, the psychological tide turned. Buyers realized they could wait for affordable units, plunging the market into years of stagnation and humbling even prime Gangnam real estate.

Today, however, that playbook is broken. Seoul is entering a severe "supply famine," with annual completions dwindling toward 10,000 units by 2028 and little relief arriving before 2030. While high interest rates—which spiked after central bank hikes following the pandemic—and fears of tenant deposit shortfalls (jeonse crises) have subdued past rallies, they cannot overcome an absolute void in physical housing stock. Government warnings and distant future projections simply carry no weight against the immediate scarcity of homes.

From Investment to Survival: The Tragic Realism of Today's Buyers

Decades ago, buying real estate in Seoul was a lucrative ladder to wealth creation. Today, for the generation currently entering the market, it has morphed into something far more basic: a defensive shield against displacement. Caught between precarious rental markets and the lingering trauma of rental fraud, ordinary people are stretching their finances to the absolute limit—so-called "soul-scraping" loans—to secure a permanent roof over their heads. Dismissing these buyers as reckless speculators misses the point entirely. They are scrambling to buy not because they expect a speculative windfall, but because they have lost faith in the rental system and see no credible long-term alternatives from policymakers.
