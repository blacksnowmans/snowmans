South Korea and Turkey Forge Strategic Economic and Defense Ties

November 04, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan met in Seoul on November 3, marking the first visit by a Turkish leader to South Korea in eight years and signaling a pivot toward pragmatic, outcome-oriented diplomacy.

  • Nuclear and Defense Focus: The two leaders prioritized cooperation on Turkey’s Sinop nuclear power project and expanded joint production and technical cooperation in defense industries.
  • Trade Liberalization: Both nations agreed to elevate their Free Trade Agreement (FTA) Joint Committee to a ministerial level to expedite necessary revisions and lower trade barriers.
  • Supply Chain Security: Four new Memorandums of Understanding (MOUs) were signed, focusing on artificial intelligence, energy, and critical mineral supply chains, including rare earth elements.
  • Geopolitical Alignment: The leaders committed to close coordination on Middle Eastern stability and reconfirmed Turkey’s support for the Lee administration’s policy on North Korean denuclearization.

A Shift from Sentiment to Substance

While President Lee emphasized the "blood-brother" historical bond formed during the Korean War, the substance of this summit was defined by cold, hard economics. Moving away from the purely symbolic diplomacy seen in previous administrations, the Lee government is clearly positioning Korea as an active participant in global supply chain restructuring. By leveraging Turkey’s unique position as a gateway between Europe, Asia, and the Middle East, Seoul is securing vital entry points for its nuclear and defense exports.

The decision to upgrade the FTA committee to a ministerial level is particularly significant. It suggests that the current government intends to bypass bureaucratic stagnation to ensure that trade frameworks keep pace with rapidly changing global markets. For South Korean exporters, this institutional backing provides the legal and structural "fence" needed to operate more confidently in the Turkish market.

Strategic Hedging in a Volatile World

The inclusion of critical minerals and AI in the bilateral MOUs highlights Korea’s urgent need to diversify its supply chains and reduce dependence on any single nation. In an era of "every nation for itself," partnering with a regional power like Turkey acts as an essential insurance policy for Korea’s energy and tech sectors.

By treating the summit as a vehicle for securing real assets—rather than just diplomatic photo opportunities—the Lee administration is demonstrating a focused "sales diplomacy." Whether these agreements successfully translate into rapid industrial results remains to be seen, but the intent to secure national interest through pragmatic, multi-vector engagement is clear.
