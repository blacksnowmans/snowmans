South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Delay Crypto Tax for the Fourth Time

October 31, 2026

The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is signaling a likely fourth delay of the country’s 20% cryptocurrency gains tax, citing a lack of administrative infrastructure and the need to protect young investors.

  • DPK leadership met with executives from the "Big 5" exchanges—including Upbit and Bithumb—and the Digital Asset eXchange Alliance (DAXA), who warned of market panic if taxation begins in January 2025 as scheduled.
  • The party has officially requested the Lee Jae-myung administration to suspend administrative notices regarding the tax until a final political consensus is reached by late November.
  • Key technical hurdles remain unresolved, specifically the inability of the National Tax Service to accurately track acquisition costs for assets moved from overseas exchanges or private "cold wallets."
  • Proponents of the delay argue that the "Phase 2" Digital Asset Basic Act—which focuses on market promotion and stablecoin regulation—must be enacted before the state begins collecting revenue from the sector.

A Strategic Retreat for Young Voters

The 2030 demographic (South Koreans in their 20s and 30s) views cryptocurrency as one of the few remaining ladders for wealth accumulation in an economy defined by stagnant wages and astronomical housing prices. For the DPK, enforcing a tax with a relatively low deduction threshold of about $1,800 (2.5 million KRW) risks a massive backlash from a core voting bloc. Critics argue that while the principle of "taxation where there is income" is sound, the current plan feels punitive toward young people who have received little state protection during previous market crashes.

Furthermore, the administrative reality is messy. Unlike the domestic stock market, which relies on decades of centralized settlement data, the decentralized nature of blockchain makes enforcement prone to errors. Industry leaders emphasized during the October 30 meeting that without a robust system to track cross-border transfers, the tax would essentially penalize law-abiding domestic users while encouraging "capital flight" to unregulated platforms. The DPK’s move to stall government notices is a pragmatic attempt to avoid "balloon effects" where liquidity drains out of the domestic market just as it is beginning to stabilize.

Correcting the Failures of the Past

The current push for a delay also reflects a shift in how the government handles digital assets compared to the previous era. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, the market was often characterized by policy flip-flops and a lack of clear vision, leading to the regulatory vacuum that the current Lee Jae-myung government is now attempting to fill. DPK lawmakers argue that it is irresponsible to slap a "sandbag" of heavy taxation on a nascent industry before the state has even finished building the "fence" of user protection laws.

The next two weeks will be a period of intense negotiation between the DPK and the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition. While the PPP has criticized the ruling party for being indecisive, the DPK appears committed to setting a final roadmap by the end of November. If a fourth delay is granted, the party will be under immense pressure to finally deliver a transparent, technology-integrated tax system that the public can actually trust, rather than another temporary patch.
