South Korea's Sweeping Prosecutor Reform Sparks Chaos and Internal Strife

October 03, 2026

    South Korea has launched a massive overhaul of its criminal justice system, splitting investigative and prosecutorial powers by creating the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA) and the Prosecution Service.
  • The rushed rollout has left over 2,000 prosecutors without formal job assignments, temporarily halting trials and causing administrative chaos.
  • President Lee Jae-myung has urged caution against reckless rhetoric, though reform-minded lawmakers fear this signals a retreat from structural change.
  • Critics within the ruling camp warn that former prosecutors are maneuvering to capture key posts in the new SCIA, threatening to recreate old institutional privileges.

The 78-year monopoly of power held by South Korea's prosecutors—who historically controlled investigations, indictments, and warrant requests all at once—is finally being dismantled. Yet the long-awaited division of labor has devolved into a bitter factional dispute. While President Lee calls for calm to avoid a conservative backlash, impatient reformers argue that procedural delays and questionable personnel appointments are watering down the entire agenda.

At the center of the dispute are fierce battles over personnel. Lawmakers like Park Eun-jung and Kim Yong-min have faced attacks from opponents framing them as radical, while controversies swirl around candidates slated to lead the new agencies. Critics argue that vetting failures are allowing figures linked to past investigative abuses to slip back into positions of power, proving that institutional rebranding means nothing without a total personnel reset.

Growing Pains or a Betrayal of Reform?

The paralysis at the courts—where trials have stalled simply because prosecutors lack formal, finalized job assignments—illustrates the dangers of hasty implementation. Despite years of planning, the administrative vacuum has given critics of the overhaul easy ammunition to label the reform a failure. If the government cannot manage basic structural transitions, public trust in the new criminal justice framework will collapse.

Ultimately, this standoff is about whether South Korea can break free from its legacy of politically motivated investigations. Reformers argue that true justice requires holding accountable those who participated in past collective insubordinations, such as the prosecutors who signed petitions against former President Yoon Suk-yeol's disciplinary actions. As this historic experiment unfolds, the administration faces a hard truth: without a genuine clearing of the old guard, any new agency risks becoming a clone of the very system it was built to replace.
