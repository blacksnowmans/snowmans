Black String Brings Its Global Korean Avant-Garde Sound Back to Seoul

November 20, 2026

After two years focused largely on European stages, the internationally acclaimed Korean ensemble Black String is returning home for a solo concert in Seoul on December 17.

  • The return: The quartet—Heo Yoon-jung on the geomungo (a traditional six-stringed Korean zither), Oh Jung-soo on electric guitar, Hwang Min-wang on percussion, and Kang Byung-ha on the daegeum (a large bamboo flute)—will stage their solo concert "Come Back Home" at Yonsei University's Centennial Hall in Seoul.
  • Global credentials: Following their 2016 debut album, Black String became the first Korean and Asian act to win at the UK's Songlines Music Awards, released records on the prominent German jazz label ACT Music, and collaborated with international musicians such as Majid Bekkas and Nguyen Lê at the Berlin Philharmonic's East-West jazz festival.
  • The performance: Blending traditional Korean music, known as gugak, with jazz improvisation and rock energy, the 90-minute show (which could stretch to 100 minutes due to live improvisation) includes pieces inspired by the Dead Man Walking soundtrack and ssitgimgut, a Korean shamanic ritual used to console the dead and comfort the living.
  • The political backdrop: According to the Korean source post and remarks from the event's host cited within it, the concert is also being framed locally as a celebratory, cathartic gathering following South Korea's recent impeachment crisis and the election of President Lee Jae-myung.

Exporting tradition before winning over home audiences

The gap between Black String's overseas reputation and its domestic profile highlights a recurring paradox in South Korea's cultural scene. While K-pop dominates global charts, experimental acts rooted in gugak often run into local prejudice that treats traditional instruments as static museum pieces or background music for official ceremonies. It took validation from European jazz institutions—and devoted fans traveling from as far as Arizona and Toronto—to spotlight what domestic audiences have largely overlooked.

A sound built for national catharsis

What makes Black String compelling is friction rather than polite fusion. On pieces like "Flowing, Floating," Heo's aggressive, percussive playing on an ancient instrument collides with Oh's sharp electric guitar, turning centuries-old heritage into something closer to psychedelic rock. Even their improvisational piece "Chilchae," opening with seven gong strikes, relies on intense, split-second interplay rather than nostalgia.

That raw intensity helps explain why commentators in the source material link the December 17 concert to South Korea's recent political transition. Whether attendees see the night as a civic celebration after President Lee Jae-myung's election or simply as an overdue musical homecoming, the band's refusal to keep Korean tradition locked in the past makes this one of Seoul's more intriguing year-end performances.
