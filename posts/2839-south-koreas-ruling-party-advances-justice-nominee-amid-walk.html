South Korea’s Ruling Party Advances Justice Nominee Amid Walkout and Judicial Turmoil

November 28, 2026

On November 27, South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party unilaterally adopted a confirmation hearing report for Justice Minister nominee Lee Yong-cheol after lawmakers from the conservative opposition People Power Party walked out in protest, according to a Korean political briefing citing domestic media including KBS News.

  • Confirmation report passed: Democratic Party members on the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee voted to classify Lee as qualified and forwarded the report to President Lee Jae-myung’s government, while People Power Party lawmakers exited before the vote, condemning what they called the "tyranny of the majority."
  • Prosecution reform dispute: People Power Party floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik labeled the nomination and additional Prosecutor General candidate recommendations an "indictment-cancellation jailbreak train," alleging an effort to drop trials tied to President Lee ahead of launching the new Indictment Office—a charge the Korean briefing characterizes as legally unfeasible for a single minister to execute.
  • Budget review stalled: Democratic Party lawmaker Park Hong-geun demanded a recess during committee budget deliberations, accusing government officials and the opposition of failing to answer inquiries in good faith and tying fiscal talks to political disputes.
  • Broader judicial friction: Away from the committee, tensions escalated over a sitting judge sharing links to an election-fraud conspiracy forum, a special counsel seeking a 10-year prison term against conservative figure Hwang Kyo-ahn for inciting insurrection in defense of imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, an opposition "law distortion" complaint against that counsel, and debate over impeaching Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae.

What the Walkout Signals for Prosecution Reform

The committee standoff underscores how deeply South Korea’s planned overhaul of prosecutorial power divides the political establishment. President Lee’s administration aims to finalize the separation of investigative and prosecutorial authority through the new Indictment Office, making the Justice Minister and the incoming Prosecutor General central figures in reshaping the country’s law enforcement structure.

By walking out rather than contesting the nominee’s legal qualifications through committee debate, the People Power Party signaled total political resistance while leaving the ruling majority to pass the report unchecked. While the Korean briefing views the opposition’s "jailbreak" rhetoric as an effort to mobilize conservative voters rather than a grounded legal critique, the deadlock also highlights the absence of bipartisan consensus over who controls prosecutorial institutions.

Courts Caught Between Politics and Polarization

Beyond the ministerial confirmation, the convergence of budget brinkmanship, special counsel prosecutions, and disputes inside the judiciary risks straining public confidence in South Korea’s courts. Each camp has accused the other of politicizing the legal system—whether through opposition complaints against investigators and impeachment threats against the Chief Justice, or controversies over sitting judges engaging with partisan conspiracy theories—leaving constitutional checks under sustained pressure as year-end budget negotiations proceed.
