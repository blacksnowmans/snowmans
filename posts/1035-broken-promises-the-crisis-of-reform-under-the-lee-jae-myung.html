Broken Promises: The Crisis of Reform Under the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 12, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is facing intense scrutiny as critics argue that its flagship "prosecution reform"—a cornerstone of the ruling Democratic Party’s 123-point national agenda—has been abandoned in favor of consolidating executive power.

  • The Broken Pledge: The administration promised to dismantle the traditional prosecution system by splitting investigative and prosecutorial powers, yet critics claim the government is instead weaponizing these powers for political ends.
  • Institutional Capture: Despite promises of "de-prosecution," key roles in the Ministry of Justice are now heavily occupied by former prosecutors, a trend critics argue is more entrenched than during the tenure of the disgraced former president, Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • Internal Schism: A faction labeled "New Lee Jae-myung" is allegedly branding internal Democratic Party critics as enemies, signaling a move toward retaliatory politics rather than the national unity once promised.
  • Structural Failure: Even with the recent reorganization of the criminal justice system—which replaced the old Prosecution Service with the new Investigation Agency and Prosecution Agency—the core issues of power concentration and legal inefficiency remain unresolved.

The Myth of "Irreversible Reform"

When Lee Jae-myung took office, he pledged an "irreversible de-prosecution" of the Ministry of Justice, promising to replace career prosecutors with legal experts to break the grip of the prosecutorial establishment. Today, that promise appears hollow. Positions ranging from public relations to strategic planning and human rights are dominated by former prosecutors. This has led to accusations that the administration is not reforming the prosecution, but rather "privatizing" its power to serve the political interests of the ruling party.

The situation is further complicated by the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction, which supporters claim has effectively "taken off their watches"—a metaphorical signal that they are ready to engage in aggressive political combat. Critics within the liberal camp argue that this group is adopting the same "dirty" tactics used by their opponents, such as manufacturing fake news to silence reasonable dissent within the Democratic Party. By turning their fire on internal critics rather than the main opposition, the People Power Party, the administration is accused of betraying the historical values of the Democratic Party.

A Deepening Crisis of Identity

The administration’s reliance on recycled political tools, such as the renewed investigation into the SillaJen financial scandal, suggests a troubling irony. The Democratic Party, which once took pride in its "courage to hit a rock with an egg" during the days of military dictatorship, now appears to be using the very prosecutorial weapons it once sought to destroy. The recent criminal justice system overhaul has reduced total personnel by 21% (from 10,700 to 8,400), yet the number of prosecutors remains unchanged at 2,292, creating a bloated, ineffective structure that struggles to handle complex financial or high-tech crimes.

For many veteran supporters of the Democratic Party, the current state of affairs is a betrayal of the legacies of Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in. The prevailing sentiment among these dissenters is clear: the "monster" they sought to defeat has been internalized within their own ranks. As the administration continues to frame internal criticism as extremism, the call for a new leadership that can reclaim the party's original spirit is growing louder. The democratic movement in Korea has always prided itself on its ability to survive adversity; whether it can survive this internal transformation remains the central question of the Lee administration.
