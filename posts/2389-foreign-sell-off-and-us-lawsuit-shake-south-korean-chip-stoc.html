Foreign Sell-Off and U.S. Lawsuit Shake South Korean Chip Stocks

November 16, 2026

South Korea’s stock market tumbled on November 16 as foreign investors dumped nearly $1.5 billion in equities following a U.S. court ruling that allows a memory-chip price-fixing lawsuit against South Korean chipmakers Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix to move forward, though the sell-off reflects deeper anxiety over an AI hardware slowdown rather than actual legal peril.

  • The market rout: South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI index sank 3.59% to close at 5,100.50, while the tech-heavy KOSDAQ plunged 4.86% to 665.64 as foreign funds net-sold roughly $1.25 billion to $1.5 billion (1.7 trillion to 2 trillion won), knocking Samsung Electronics down over 4% and SK Hynix nearly 8% alongside a 5.74% drop in Taiwan’s TAIEX.
  • The legal trigger: A federal judge in Northern California refused to dismiss a class-action suit by U.S. consumers and small PC builders accusing Samsung, SK Hynix, and U.S. rival Micron of starving the market of standard DRAM chips—sending DDR4 contract prices up 805% since October 2022—while shifting capacity to high-bandwidth memory (HBM) for AI servers.
  • What changes: Very little in the near term, as U.S. antitrust trials typically take three to four years to resolve, and a 2022 appeals ruling in a similar 2018 suit established that matching a rival’s production cuts is legal without direct proof of collusion.
  • Who is affected: Local retail investors absorbed about $1.25 billion (1.7 trillion won) in net selling without institutional support, while defensive stocks such as South Korean non-life insurers—led by a 7% jump in DB Insurance—and food exporter Samyang Foods drew capital seeking shelter.

Why the Lawsuit Is Mostly Noise

Markets flinched largely because of the 1999–2002 price-fixing scandal, when a U.S. Justice Department probe ended in criminal fines of $300 million for Samsung and $185 million for Hynix (now SK Hynix), plus prison time for executives. Yet the current case rests on a shaky premise: treating a post-downturn pivot toward higher-margin HBM as an illegal cartel. Unless plaintiffs uncover explicit communications proving an agreement, the case looks like multi-year background noise rather than an imminent financial hit.

The Real Fear: AI Peak-Out and Capital Flight

In my view, the court filing merely gave foreign funds a convenient excuse to unload shares into an exhausted market. The real pressure comes from mounting doubts over how long the AI infrastructure boom can sustain peak chip margins, as Big Tech buyers balk at soaring GPU and memory bills, AI labs like Anthropic weigh public listings to fund massive cloud commitments, and boardroom and U.S.-China export tensions cloud Nvidia’s outlook.

South Korean chipmakers also suffer from structural disadvantages when global investors turn cautious. Even as Samsung Electronics’ annual operating profit climbs toward roughly $75 billion to $95 billion (100 trillion to 130 trillion won), sharp swings in the Korean currency—between 1,150 and 1,360 won per dollar—push U.S. institutions toward dollar-denominated Micron, leaving Seoul’s chip giants exposed whenever foreign capital heads for the exit.
