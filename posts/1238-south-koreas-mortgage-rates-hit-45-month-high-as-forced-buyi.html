South Korea’s Mortgage Rates Hit 45-Month High as 'Forced Buying' Grips Housing Market

October 16, 2026

South Korea’s mortgage rates have surged to 4.66%—their highest level in three years and nine months—triggering a wave of reluctant property purchases as rental shortages leave prospective tenants with nowhere else to go.

  • Rate spike: New data from the Bank of Korea, South Korea’s central bank, shows average bank mortgage rates climbing past 4.6% in August, with some fixed-rate products testing 7% to 8%.
  • Dwindling rental supply: Severe shortages in the jeonse (lump-sum deposit lease) and monthly rental markets are triggering "forced purchases," compelling renters to buy homes rather than risk homelessness.
  • Regulatory squeeze: Financial authorities and the current Lee Jae-myung administration are tightening loan quotas and eyeing stricter caps on homes valued under 1.5 billion won (roughly $1.1 million), while subsidized policy loans for first-time buyers face shrinking funds.
  • Psychological shift: Unlike the market panic during aggressive rate hikes in 2022, today’s prospective buyers are factoring worst-case 8% borrowing costs into their household budgets to avoid being permanently priced out.

Why Rising Rates Aren't Cooling Demand

Conventional economic theory dictates that steeper borrowing costs stifle housing demand, but South Korea's real estate market is defying textbook dynamics. For end-users in Seoul and surrounding metropolitan areas, taking on a 5% loan is no longer an investment choice—it is a survival strategy. With landlords holding back rentals and supply forecasts remaining bleak, desperate tenants view entering the market under heavy financing burdens as a lesser evil than draining their capital on skyrocketing rents.

Market sentiment has fundamentally transformed since 2022. During the rate-hike shock under Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, overleveraged homeowners dumped properties in panic. Today, market power has shifted to resilient buyers who have accepted high borrowing costs as normal. With 100 million won (around $74,000) in debt yielding monthly servicing costs of roughly 600,000 to 700,000 won, buyers are stress-testing their cash flow against potential 8% interest levels rather than waiting for rate cuts that may never arrive.

Policy Contradictions and Liquidity Headwinds

The macroeconomic backdrop offers little relief. While the Bank of Korea faces headline inflation hovering near 3% against a sluggish 1.1% growth outlook, the government plans an 820 trillion won (about $600 billion) "super budget," including 92 trillion won in direct liquidity injections. This massive fiscal expansion directly clashes with monetary tightening, making sustained interest rate reductions nearly impossible in the near term.

Instead of addressing supply bottlenecks, regulators are clamping down on credit limits and paring back low-income policy loans like the Didimdol and Bogeumjari programs. By pulling up the housing ladder for lower- and middle-income families, the authorities risk concentrating market activity at artificial regulatory boundaries. As banks hit their annual lending caps toward year-end, many buyers are already queuing to execute transactions when credit quotas reset on January 1, setting the stage for another high-cost liquidity scramble.
