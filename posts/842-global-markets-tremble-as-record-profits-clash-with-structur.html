Global Markets Tremble as Record Profits Clash with Structural Reality

October 09, 2026

  • Samsung Electronics posted a staggering quarterly operating profit of roughly $77 billion (107 trillion won), yet its shares fell 2.4% as global index rebalancing triggered aggressive foreign selling.
  • The Kospi index slumped broadly, with declining stocks outnumbering gainers more than two to one, driven by heavy foreign sell-offs and rising European bond yields.
  • SK Group faced turbulence as reports of a US listing for its subsidiary Solidigm sent shares tumbling over governance and double-listing concerns.
  • LG Energy Solution bucked the trend, posting strong third-quarter results fueled by surging North American demand for energy storage systems (ESS).

Global markets are delivering a harsh lesson in the disconnect between corporate fundamentals and mechanical fund flows. While October 8 brought jaw-dropping earnings reports from South Korea's tech titans, investors were met with plunging stock prices across Europe and Asia, exposed to shifting macroeconomic tides and rigid index rules.

The absurdity was perhaps best captured by Samsung Electronics, the nation's tech flagship. Despite announcing a historic operating profit that ranks among the largest quarterly earnings in corporate history, its stock slipped as global funds mechanically trimmed positions to meet index weight caps. It is a familiar pain for Korean retail investors, where blockbuster news is routinely met with a cold "sell on the news" reality and algorithmic rebalancing.

The Battleground: Tech Giants, Governance, and AI

The turbulence extended beyond Samsung. SK Hynix and its parent company SK Square took a severe hit after reports surfaced that US-based subsidiary Solidigm plans to list on American markets. The move reignited fierce retail backlash over the "Korea discount" — the chronic undervaluation of local stocks driven by opaque governance and overseas spin-offs that hollow out parent companies. Meanwhile, Hyundai Motor lost its spot as South Korea's third-largest corporate group by market capitalization to LG Group, as traditional automakers face relentless pressure over the speed of their transition to robotics and future mobility.

Amid the market gloom, structural bright spots emerged elsewhere. LG Energy Solution reported a stellar quarter driven by explosive North American demand for energy storage systems (ESS), proving that clean energy infrastructure remains a potent growth engine despite a temporary cooling in consumer electric vehicle demand. At the same time, the artificial intelligence frontier continues to advance at a dizzying pace: OpenAI's latest models have reportedly tackled hundreds of advanced mathematical problems and made significant headway on several Millennium Prize Problems, testing the boundaries of automated reasoning and labor economics.

Power Grid Bottlenecks and the Rise of Shareholder Activism

Underpinning both South Korea's industrial ambitions and the global AI boom is a brutal physical constraint: power. Proposed AI data centers and the country's multi-billion-dollar semiconductor cluster in Yongin face severe bottlenecks. Major energy providers like GS Group have built massive thermal power generation facilities in regions like Donghae, yet face fierce local opposition and grid connection delays that threaten to stall the nation's high-tech blueprint.

To fix these systemic drag factors, South Korean authorities and market watchdogs are pushing back through regulatory pressure, introducing "naming and shaming" policies against companies that hoard cash and ignore shareholder returns. As undervalued firms are forced to transparently reassess massive land and capital assets, a quiet revolution is brewing: retail investors are finally waking up to demand their rights as true owners, pushing back against decades of dominant shareholder dismissiveness.
