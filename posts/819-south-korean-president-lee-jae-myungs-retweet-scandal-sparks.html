South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s "Retweet Scandal" Sparks Political Crisis

September 19, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing a deepening political crisis after his social media activity undermined his own public apologies, fueling accusations that his administration is increasingly detached from mainstream public opinion.

  • The Incident: Shortly after publicly apologizing for a lack of "communication and sensitivity," President Lee retweeted inflammatory posts from a supporter that attacked his own party members, a former president, and political critics.
  • Falling Approval: Recent polls, including those from the National Barometer Survey (NBS) and Gallup Korea, show the President’s approval rating hitting a "dead cross"—where disapproval ratings overtake approval—falling to around 43%.
  • Internal Divisions: Prominent progressive voices, including writer Rhyu Si-min, have warned that Lee’s intervention in party affairs and hostility toward dissenters are dismantling his own governing coalition.
  • Public Sentiment: Disapproval is driven by frustration over real estate policy and controversial cabinet nominations, with voters increasingly viewing the administration as arrogant and insular.

A Pattern of Contradiction

The controversy centers on the jarring disconnect between the President’s public persona and his private digital habits. While President Lee spent his days offering measured apologies to the nation, he spent his nights amplifying aggressive, far-right-style rhetoric directed at his own political allies. By retweeting posts that labeled his critics—including those from the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal party)—as "enemies," the President signaled that his apologies were merely performative.

Critics argue that this behavior confirms a dangerous trend: the President is isolating himself within a "filter bubble" of extreme supporters. Rather than addressing the substantive policy failures cited in polls, the administration has doubled down on blaming "external agitation" and "internal traitors." This siege mentality is causing alarm even among his traditional supporters, who fear the government is repeating the mistakes of failed past administrations.

The Risk of Self-Destruction

Political analysts and party stalwarts, including former lawmaker Rhyu Si-min, have drawn parallels to the previous conservative government’s downfall, warning that Lee is effectively dismantling his own base by demanding absolute loyalty and stifling internal debate. Rhyu specifically criticized the President’s perceived interference in party leadership, characterizing it as a return to an archaic, "aristocratic" style of governance that alienates the moderate middle.

For a leader to survive, he must accept that the public’s frustration is rooted in tangible issues—such as housing and personnel choices—rather than media conspiracy. As the President’s approval ratings continue to slide, the refusal to engage with genuine criticism suggests an administration that has lost its way. If the government continues to prioritize silencing dissent over addressing the economic and social anxieties of the electorate, the "dead cross" in the polls may just be the beginning of a long, irreversible decline.
