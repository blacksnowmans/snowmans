Donald Trump Hints at China Meeting with Kim Jong Un, Shaking Northeast Asian Diplomacy

November 18, 2026

Former U.S. President Donald Trump has sparked a fresh diplomatic whirlwind across Northeast Asia by publicly floating the possibility of meeting North Korean leader Kim Jong Un during an upcoming visit to China.

  • What happened: Speaking to reporters in the Philippines on November 17, Trump suggested Kim Jong Un would want to meet him in China, placing the responsibility on Chinese President Xi Jinping to broker the summit.
  • Regional reactions: Reports of Kim visiting the border city of Dandong suggest Pyongyang may be open to the proposal, while Moscow quickly signaled its own interest in meeting Kim to preserve Russian influence in regional affairs.
  • Beijing's reserve: China’s Foreign Ministry took a cautious stance, stating it had "no information" on the potential meeting as it calculates its strategic leverage against Washington.
  • Who is affected: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, currently attending ASEAN meetings to promote his administration's peaceful coexistence strategy, faces renewed concerns that Seoul could be sidelined by major-power bargaining.

A Political Gambit That Threatens to Bypass Seoul

Trump’s sudden proposal appears driven primarily by domestic political needs following a disappointing midterm election, as he seeks a high-profile diplomatic headline to regain momentum. By claiming that a summit depends entirely on Xi Jinping, Trump is attempting to project strong leadership while shifting any potential blame to Beijing if talks fall through. However, this transaction-focused, top-down strategy carries severe risks. In a rush to secure a quick victory, Trump could push for a "small deal" that grants sanction relief in exchange for minor concessions, leaving North Korea's core nuclear arsenal intact and undercutting South Korean security.

Navigating a Complex Great-Power Tug-of-War

For President Lee Jae-myung, this sudden maneuver threatens to distract from his diplomatic push at ASEAN to secure regional backing for sustainable inter-Korean peace. With Russia jumping into the fray to demand its own share of influence and China playing its cards close to its chest, the Korean Peninsula risks becoming a stage for great-power transactionalism. The foreign policy vulnerabilities experienced during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's past tenure underscore how easily South Korea can lose its voice when big powers take over the agenda. To avoid being marginalized, the Lee administration must aggressively coordinate with Washington while engaging Beijing and Moscow, insisting that any renewed dialogue remains anchored in genuine denuclearization.
