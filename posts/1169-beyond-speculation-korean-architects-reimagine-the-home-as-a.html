Beyond Speculation: Korean Architects Reimagine the Home as a Vessel for Living

October 15, 2026

South Korean architects Noh Eun-joo and Lim Hyoung-nam of Gaon Architects are challenging the country's fixation on rigid apartment blocks with bespoke homes that prioritize daily ritual, memory, and personal identity over resale value.

  • The "House of Chaos": Instead of maximizing square footage, the architects designed a multi-level home around a preserved 100-year-old persimmon tree, using organic paths inspired by Soswaewon, a famed 16th-century Joseon Dynasty garden in Damyang.
  • Intimate spatial details: The home features custom-built built-in furniture, column-free corner windows to foster subtle family connections, a dedicated footbath area for the client's mother, and a compact 1.5-pyeong (around 50 square feet) meditation room.
  • Live-work integration: Another project combines a neighborhood bakery with an upstairs flat, linked by an indoor net hammock and a floor aperture that lets the resident monitor the shop floor and catch the scent of baking bread.
  • A changing residential mindset: These projects reflect a cultural push to treat housing as an expressive space for life rather than an interchangeable financial commodity.

Architecture Rooted in Memory and Daily Rhythms

In South Korea's hyper-dense urban landscape, conventional homebuilding usually prioritizes maximizing floor area ratios and standardizing layouts. In contrast, Noh and Lim began the House of Chaos—originally titled "House of Prajna," a Buddhist term for wisdom—by bending the structure around an existing century-old persimmon tree. Rather than erecting concrete walls that isolate rooms, the layout draws on traditional Korean spatial philosophy: corridors meander, boundaries between terraces and interiors blur, and window frames maintain sightlines across rooms to allow family members to stay visually connected without intruding on each other's privacy.

The design also challenges the assumption that luxury requires vast floor plans. The centerpiece of quiet reflection in the house is a meditation nook measuring just 1.5 pyeong (about 5 square meters). By prioritizing tailor-made carpentry and emotional comfort—such as a quiet footbath overlooking an outdoor walking path—the architects demonstrate that personal wellness depends on how well a space accommodates human vulnerability, not how many square meters it commands.

Shifting Away from the Speculative Apartment Box

This experimental approach extends to small-scale urban revivals, such as the architects' mixed-use bakery home. Dubbed "crispy outside, soft inside" like a baguette, the building presents a robust exterior to the street while maintaining a playful, flexible interior. The second-story residential area uses a suspended hammock and viewing holes to actively engage with the ground-floor commercial bakery, creating a model for self-employed Koreans seeking to merge craft with domestic life.

These designs signal a broader reassessment of housing in South Korea. During the term of Yoon Suk-yeol, the domestic real estate market saw intense speculative fever that reduced apartments to leveraged investment vehicles. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, public discourse has increasingly shifted back toward housing dignity and livability. While standardized high-rises remain the dominant reality for most urbanites, projects like the House of Chaos offer an alternative blueprint: one where a home reflects who lives in it rather than how much it might sell for next year.
