From Michigan to Krakow: A Grassroots Upset Collides With the Global Far-Right Wave

November 09, 2026

Progressive Abdul El-Sayed has made history as the first Muslim elected to the U.S. Senate from Michigan, overcoming an onslaught of corporate and lobby spending in a rare counter-punch against Donald Trump’s resurgent far-right movement.

  • A historic upset in Michigan: El-Sayed defeated Haley Stevens despite roughly $22 million (30 billion won) poured into the race by the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC), alongside direct rally appearances by Donald Trump and JD Vance.
  • Outspent ten-to-one: Operating on just 9 to 10 percent of the opposition’s war chest, El-Sayed neutralized anti-Muslim rhetoric with kitchen-table messaging focused on the cost of living.
  • Combative Trump 2.0 personnel: Trump’s staffing moves—anchored by immigration hardliner Stephen Miller and floated roles for figures like UFC CEO Dana White—signal an aggressive White House posture defined by confrontation.
  • Rising extremist violence in Europe: Emboldened by transatlantic far-right slogans like "Make Europe Great Again," violent extremism recently struck Krakow, Poland, leaving six dead and 15 wounded in a copycat attack echoing the Christchurch mosque massacre.
  • Strains on Korean Peninsula diplomacy: Trump’s transactional "America First" foreign policy threatens to erode multilateral diplomacy and stall long-term peace initiatives across Northeast Asia.

The Limits of Lobby Cash and Identity Attacks

El-Sayed’s victory in Michigan offers a rare blueprint for beating heavily funded grievance politics. Rather than getting bogged down in ideological defense as opponents attacked his faith and ethnicity, El-Sayed pierced through the noise with a simple question to voters: "Does criticizing my faith lower the price of your groceries?" That focus on material living standards resonated with working-class voters who were exhausted by culture-war polarization.

This dynamic stands in stark contrast to Trump’s incoming personnel roster. With hardliner Stephen Miller positioned to steer internal policy and rumors swirling around high-profile combat-sports figures fronting communications, Washington appears headed toward deliberate political warfare. Rather than governance through consensus, the administration is doubling down on political pugilism.

Global Spillovers and the View From Seoul

The consequences of this ideological climate are spilling far beyond U.S. borders. The horrific attack in Krakow—a UNESCO World Heritage city previously seen as insulated from such horrors, where police also discovered the bodies of the shooter's parents and sister—demonstrates how hate crimes are increasingly adopting an online, gamified format modeled on prior extremist manifestos.

For South Korea, Trump’s ascent brings sharp diplomatic anxiety. For South Korean observers, this rise of division echoes the bitter polarization experienced domestically during Yoon Seok-yeol’s time in office—a period of intense prosecutorial politics that ultimately ended in Yoon's impeachment and the rise of the current Lee Jae-myung administration. On the foreign front, Trump’s mercantilist worldview leaves little room for structured, multilateral peacemaking, casting a long shadow over the hard-won inter-Korean engagement frameworks first pursued under former president Moon Jae-in.
