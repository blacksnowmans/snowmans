South Korea's Housing Crisis: Why Penalising Landlords Backfires on Renters

September 07, 2026

South Korea's aggressive property tax hikes and anti-landlord policies are backfiring, squeezing non-homeowners and shrinking the rental supply rather than stabilizing the market.

    What happened: South Korea's heavy-handed real estate regulations—including acquisition taxes of up to 12% for multiple-property owners and cracked-down private rental housing schemes—have effectively frozen the rental market. What it changes: Instead of flooding the market with affordable homes, these measures have driven private landlords out of business, choked off rental inventory, and made it nearly impossible for prospective buyers to find standard housing. Who is affected: Non-homeowners and everyday tenants bear the brunt of the crisis, facing skyrocketing jeonse (lump-sum deposit) and monthly rent prices, while prospective buyers scramble in a hyper-competitive market.

Korean real estate has long been a psychological battleground for ordinary salary earners desperate not to fall behind on the capitalist ladder. Recent data highlights a glaring structural anomaly: while South Korea's recurring property holding taxes remain relatively low compared to other advanced economies, its transactional levies—namely acquisition and capital gains taxes—are among the highest in the world. By weaponizing taxes to punish property owners, policymakers have effectively paralyzed housing mobility, trapping capital in a rigid system where everyday buyers feel forced into panic-buying ("yeongkkeul," meaning borrowing to the absolute limit).

The core policy failure lies in the false assumption that penalizing multiple-home owners automatically purifies the market. In reality, when the government strips away tax incentives for registered private rental providers and blocks normal market circulation, private landlords simply exit the space. Institutional or corporate leasing cannot easily fill this void with the same flexibility, leaving tenants with fewer options and higher costs. As long as Seoul's core districts remain choked by heavy red tape like land transaction permission zones while secondary regions stagnate, the government's emotional approach to taxation will continue to generate unintended distortions rather than housing stability.
