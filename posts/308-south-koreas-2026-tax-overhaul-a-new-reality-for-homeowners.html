South Korea’s 2026 Tax Overhaul: A New Reality for Homeowners

September 07, 2026

South Korea’s latest tax reform, effective September 1, fundamentally redraws the map for property owners, shifting the burden heavily toward those who do not live in the homes they own.

  • Primary homeowners: Those living in their own homes receive tax relief, with the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax deduction threshold raised to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million).
  • Non-resident homeowners: These owners remain under intense pressure, as the government continues to signal that properties should be primary residences, not investment vehicles.
  • Multi-homeowners: Face a "tax bomb" due to complex calculations and a higher 80% market value ratio applied to their assets, incentivizing them to liquidate holdings.
  • Renters: Caught in the crossfire as landlords move back into their properties to avoid taxes, further tightening the already scarce rental market.

The End of "Passive" Homeownership

The core philosophy of this reform is not to lower taxes, but to force movement in the housing market. For those holding property as a financial asset rather than a residence, the government has created a trap. By tightening capital gains tax exemptions and mandating two-year residency requirements for non-residents to qualify for tax breaks, the state is making "empty" investment homes prohibitively expensive to hold.

The market is already reacting. High-end properties in Seoul’s affluent Gangnam district are seeing price dips as multi-homeowners scramble to sell. Conversely, middle-market areas in suburban Seoul and nearby satellite cities like Gwangmyeong are experiencing surges in demand. Desperate buyers, tired of the volatile rental market, are "panic buying" in these areas to secure long-term stability, effectively driving prices higher in the very places people are moving to escape the heat of the city center.

Strategic Survival in a Shifting Landscape

For the average person, the best defense is a cold, calculated approach to their own finances rather than emotional reactions to policy news. The era of "safe" passive property investment is over; the state is effectively weaponizing tax policy to ensure that housing functions primarily as shelter.

If you are a renter, the window for complacency has closed. Many landlords are now reclaiming their properties to fulfill residency requirements or sell, leaving tenants with few options. It is no longer enough to hope for stability; renters should be monitoring market trends six months before lease expirations and preparing for the possibility of being forced to move or, if financially viable, to buy.

Ultimately, this reform acts as a nudge: the government wants liquidity in the market. Those who fail to adjust their strategy—whether by moving into their investment properties or selling off excess assets—will find their net worth steadily eroded by taxes. In this new, rigid environment, the only winners are those who prioritize clarity and long-term residency over speculative gain.
