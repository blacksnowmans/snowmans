IMF Lifts South Korea’s Growth Forecast to 3.9%, Even as Chip Peak Fears Rattle Markets

November 18, 2026

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) raised South Korea’s growth forecast this year by 0.6 percentage points to 3.9% on surging artificial intelligence chip exports, even as investors sell off top chipmakers over fears of a looming market downturn.

  • The macro upgrade: According to the IMF, booming overseas demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and DDR5 chips used in AI servers pushed South Korea’s projected growth from 3.3% to 3.9%, putting total exports within reach of $1 trillion.
  • Market pushback: Despite the rosy headline numbers, shares of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—South Korea’s dominant memory chipmakers—fell after financial news outlet Invezz warned of a potential 20% drop in chip prices, even though the two firms hold about $235 billion (329 trillion won) in cash equivalents.
  • Downgrades ahead: South Korean brokerage Kiwoom Securities cut target prices for both firms, projecting operating profits will peak in the second quarter of next year, while market tracker TrendForce lowered its demand and price outlook for standard DRAM chips used in PCs and smartphones.
  • Everyday squeeze: Domestic consumers, small businesses, and midsize firms remain left behind by the chip boom, as persistent inflation, high interest rates, and household debt keep local spending frozen.

A One-Engine Economy Running Hot

The sharp gap between South Korea’s export numbers and its stock market reflects deep anxiety over how long the AI boom can carry the entire economy. While President Lee Jae-myung’s administration aims to leverage AI chips and Korean consumer goods to make the country one of the world’s top five exporters, semiconductor manufacturing is heavily equipment-driven and creates relatively few domestic jobs. As a result, export profits are pooling on corporate balance sheets rather than flowing into household wallets.

External vulnerabilities add to that fragility. The state-run Korea Institute for International Economic Policy (KIEP), a government economic think tank, projects global growth of just 3.2% next year and warns of dark clouds over domestic demand, while South Korean business daily Hankyung notes that volatility in U.S. Big Tech stocks such as Nvidia and Apple poses a direct risk to Korean chip suppliers. Reports cited by South Korean outlet Business Post even suggest Big Tech capital spending on AI could cool rapidly starting in 2028, potentially cutting the current memory supercycle short.

The Tightrope Ahead

In my view, a 3.9% growth rate built almost entirely on one sector is a warning sign rather than a victory lap. Because the IMF paired its upgrade with a call for continued monetary and fiscal tightening to curb inflation and household debt, ordinary households cannot count on near-term interest rate relief. Unless policymakers broaden growth beyond memory chips before global supply catches up with AI demand, South Korea risks a painful landing once the chip cycle turns.
