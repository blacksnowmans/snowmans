South Korea’s Judiciary and Executive Branch in Constitutional Standoff

September 29, 2026

A prolonged dispute between South Korea’s Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the presidential office over the appointment of a Supreme Court justice has paralyzed the nation’s highest court for over six months, raising alarm over the health of its constitutional order.

  • The impasse: A Supreme Court seat has been vacant since March. Despite a candidate recommendation committee proposing four names, the Chief Justice and the President have failed to reach an agreement on a successor.
  • The trigger: Chief Justice Cho unilaterally submitted a formal request to appoint Judge Son Bong-gi. The presidential office rejected the request, citing a lack of prior consultation, leading to a 25-day standoff.
  • The fallout: The Chief Justice subsequently defied the President’s request for a re-nomination, claiming the executive branch lacked constitutional grounds. This has effectively left the court short-staffed and stalled key judicial processes.

A Clash of Constitutional Powers

The conflict centers on a fundamental disagreement regarding the limits of administrative and judicial authority. While the Constitution grants the Chief Justice the power to recommend candidates for the Supreme Court, it requires the President to appoint them. The executive branch views the Chief Justice’s unilateral, formalistic approach as an attempt to undermine the President’s constitutional appointment power. Conversely, the judiciary argues that its recommendation power must be respected to maintain judicial independence from political influence.

This is not merely a bureaucratic dispute; it is a manifestation of deep-seated friction between the current administration and a judiciary that many observers characterize as having become increasingly politicized. Critics argue that the Chief Justice is using the vacancy as political leverage, effectively holding judicial services hostage to pressure the government. By refusing to engage in meaningful dialogue, the judiciary is drawing sharp accusations that it is abandoning its role as a neutral arbiter in favor of becoming a partisan actor.

The Human Cost of Judicial Gridlock

Beyond the high-level posturing, the real-world consequences of this standoff are felt by the public. The vacancy in the Supreme Court—the final court of appeal—leads to significant delays in litigation, directly infringing upon citizens' rights to timely justice. As the Court Administration Office (the judicial branch's governing body) prioritizes defending the Chief Justice's position in the National Assembly, the efficiency of the judicial system continues to decline.

The situation has become so untenable that some are now calling for extreme measures, including potential impeachment proceedings against the Chief Justice to hold the judiciary accountable for its failure to fulfill its constitutional duties. Whether through structural reform, such as the dissolution of the powerful Court Administration Office, or a change in the appointment process, it is clear that the status quo is unsustainable. For a judiciary tasked with upholding the law, this ongoing "self-inflicted crisis" is rapidly eroding the public trust that remains the foundation of its legitimacy.
