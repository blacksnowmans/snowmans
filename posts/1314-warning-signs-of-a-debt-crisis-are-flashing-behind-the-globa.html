Warning Signs of a Debt Crisis Are Flashing Behind the Global AI Boom and South Korea’s Rate Hikes

October 19, 2026

A dangerous surge in debt during an apparent economic boom is repeating historical pre-crisis patterns across both global tech markets and South Korea’s domestic economy.

  • What happened: Unprecedented debt expansion—driven by private equity financing massive AI data center builds in the U.S. and sharp interest rate hikes in South Korea—is putting severe pressure on financial stability.
  • What it changes: South Korea's 10-year Treasury yield surged from the upper 2% range to 4.5%, while national household debt exceeding 80% of GDP has drained domestic purchasing power and forced approximately 1 million self-employed businesses to close in a single year.
  • Who is affected: Top-earning South Korean households burdened by mortgage debt, local small business owners, and global institutional investors exposed to shadow banking leverage.

Rising Leverage in the Global AI Wave

The current market euphoria surrounding artificial intelligence closely echoes the credit expansion seen before major historical downturns, including the Great Depression and the 2008 financial crisis. Unlike 2008, when subprime household mortgages triggered systemic distress, today's risk has migrated to corporate debt fueled by non-bank shadow banking. In the U.S., total gross asset value for private funds jumped 61% to $24.3 trillion by late 2024, backing astronomical commitments such as OpenAI's planned 1,040 trillion won (about $780 billion) data center buildout and Anthropic's 700 trillion won (about $525 billion) project. As central banks—including the Bank of Japan—pivot away from zero-rate policies, elevated borrowing costs pose an acute threat to heavily leveraged tech firms that have yet to generate cash flows matching their debt service costs.

South Korea's Real Estate Debt Trap

In South Korea, strong export figures driven by semiconductor giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix mask deep distress in the local economy. A primary structural flaw is that South Korea's household debt is heavily concentrated among the top 20% income bracket. Rather than driving domestic consumption, these high earners spend much of their income servicing debt incurred to purchase real estate, long viewed in South Korea as an invincible asset class. As Bank of Korea (the central bank) Governor Rhee Chang-yong documented in past research for the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), household debt exceeding 80% to 85% of GDP chokes off effective consumer demand and causes long-term economic stagnation.

This domestic deadlock is further worsened by statistical distortions. Official inflation metrics calculated by Statistics Korea significantly under-reflect actual housing cost burdens, giving policymakers cover to avoid tough structural decisions. Until capital is diverted away from non-productive real estate leverage and back into active consumer spending, South Korea's domestic economy will remain frozen even as its tech exporters log record sales overseas.
