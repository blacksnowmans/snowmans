South Korea's Ruling Camp Splinters: President Lee Jae-myung, Ally-Turned-Critics, and the Cost of Political Infighting

September 13, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung launched a furious social media attack on internal critics, including progressive intellectual Rhyu Si-min, accusing them of undermining reforms under the guise of supporting them.
  • The core contradiction: While railing against cronyism and warning that appointing close associates is "the path to failure," the administration has heavily staffed key presidential and intelligence posts with lawyers tied to Lee’s past controversial land-development scandals.
  • The political toll: Lee's approval ratings have slipped from a high of 67% earlier in the year to 61%, dragged down by a perceived obsession with partisan political fights rather than bread-and-butter economic issues.
  • Who is affected: South Korea's progressive camp is fracturing into open warfare between the presidential office and traditional movement figures, leaving centrist and swing voters alienated.

A Progressive Civil War Over "Reform"

South Korean politics are being roiled by an uncharacteristic public feud within the progressive camp. President Lee Jae-myung—who took office riding high on promises of pragmatic governance—used social media to lash out at internal dissent, warning that "the biggest obstacle to reform is blocking reform in the name of reform." The tirade quickly exposed deep factional fault lines. When loyalists branded prominent progressive writer Rhyu Si-min and other critics as "curses" trying to wreck the administration from within, President Lee amplified the attacks by retweeting them.

Rhyu, a heavy-hitter in South Korean liberal politics, hit back hard, accusing the presidential office of fostering a toxic culture where sycophancy is rewarded and warning that Lee's heavy-handed tactics resemble absolute monarchy rather than democratic leadership. For an administration that once prided itself on sweeping unity, the spectacle of high-profile figures tearing into each other on YouTube and Facebook has exhausted supporters and handed ammunition to political opponents.

Hypocrisy on Appointments and a Sinking Approval Rating

The public friction is compounded by a damaging staffing controversy. President Lee recently warned against the perils of appointing unqualified figures simply because of personal ties. Yet watchdog scrutiny has revealed that a raft of presidential secretaries, intelligence officials, and ruling-party lawmakers hail from the circle of defense attorneys who represented Lee during the explosive Daejang-dong land-development scandal.

This glaring disconnect has fueled accusations of hypocrisy. Public opinion data from Gallup Korea shows Lee's approval rating dropping from its spring peak of 67% down to 61% as of May. Analysts point a finger away from grand systemic overhauls—like the stalled prosecution reforms—and straight at the administration's drift away from everyday economic struggles. With inflation and livelihoods squeezing ordinary citizens, the sight of a powerful president engaging in bitter online turf wars threatens to squander the political capital this government worked years to build.
