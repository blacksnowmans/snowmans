South Korea's 2026 Tax Shake-Up: Penalising Passive Owners, Rewarding Real Residents

September 08, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea unveiled a sweeping property tax overhaul for 2026, targeting non-occupying homeowners and heavy multi-property owners while easing burdens for genuine single-home residents.
  • What it changes: Capital gains and comprehensive real estate holding taxes are tightening sharply. The traditional "long-term holding" tax deduction is being phased out in favor of a "long-term residence" deduction, punishing landlords who don't live in their properties. Meanwhile, the window for temporary two-home owners to sell their original house in regulated areas is halved from three years to two years.
  • Who is affected: Seoul and Gyeonggi province homeowners face mounting pressure. While primary homeowners living in their properties get higher tax deductions (with the holding exemption threshold raised from about $8.7 million to $10.1 million), non-resident single-home owners see their threshold slashed to about $6.5 million.
  • Market impact: Rental inventory is evaporating as landlords yank properties off the market to move in themselves or sell, sending lease volumes plunging and stoking fears of a rental crunch.

South Korea’s latest tax package makes one thing bluntly clear: the government is done tolerating speculative hoarding. If you own a home just to watch the asset appreciate while renting it out to someone else, your tax bills are about to spike. If you actually live in your home, you get a protective shield. But for everyone else—especially multi-property owners and landlords—the era of sitting on idle real estate is colliding with a brick wall.

The changes hit hardest through the capital gains and holding taxes. Starting in 2028, the old "long-term holding special deduction"—which rewarded people simply for keeping a property a long time—transforms into a residence-based deduction. By 2029, simply holding a house without living in it yields a 0% deduction. At the same time, heavy penalties on multi-property owners are returning in staggered phases, pushing desperate sellers to either offload properties or scramble into temporary tax-relief zones like rural areas.

For everyday buyers and small-time investors, the playbook has narrowed to razor-thin margins. With acquisition taxes remaining stubbornly high and loan regulations keeping the squeeze on leverage, passive investing is effectively dead. Only active, nimble operators—such as those executing rapid-turnaround auctions in unregulated regional markets—can navigate this new, highly punitive tax jungle without getting burned.
