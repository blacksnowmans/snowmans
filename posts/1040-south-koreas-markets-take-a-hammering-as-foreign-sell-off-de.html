South Korea's Markets Take a Hammering as Foreign Sell-Off Deepens

October 13, 2026

South Korea’s benchmark Kospi index slumped 3.65% to close at 6,384 points, while the tech-heavy Kosdaq plummeted 4.83%, driven by aggressive foreign selling and a complete drying-up of domestic buyer support.

  • What happened: The Kospi plunged to about 5,200 trillion won ($3.8 trillion) in total market capitalization, with heavyweights like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix dropping nearly 4% and 4.5% respectively. Foreign investors offloaded trillions of won in equities.
  • What it changes: Crucial technical support levels have been breached, pushing valuations down significantly—with the Kospi price-to-earnings ratio dropping from over 33 to the teens—while dividend yields in sectors like finance climb to 7% to 8%.
  • Who is affected: Retail investors caught in the broader market rout, borrowers facing ongoing monetary policy tightening under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, and chipmakers navigating heavy infrastructure costs.

Market Rout and the Valuation Paradox

The severity of the sell-off lies less in absolute price levels and more in a total collapse of buyer sentiment. With domestic retail investors largely tapped out after pouring trillions into defending positions, the market lacked any absorber for foreign capital flight. Yet beneath the panic, the math of South Korean equities has shifted dramatically. Valuations have compressed sharply, and with chipmakers trading at modest multiples of future earnings and financial stocks flashing dividend yields north of 7%, the underlying price appeal is undeniable. At the same time, the Bank of Korea faces mounting pressure to balance inflation control with debt-burdened households, keeping interest rate uncertainties front and center.

Macro Pressures and Semiconductor Resilience

Macro headwinds—ranging from global AI hardware demand shifts and high energy costs to the Biden-era fallout on tech supply chains—continue to weigh on investor psychology. Domestically, the administration's stance prioritizes preemptive rate hikes over reactive measures to keep inflation in check, adding strain to vulnerable economic sectors. Yet long-term bets remain anchored in industrial muscle: SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won’s push for a new Honam semiconductor cluster highlights a stubborn corporate commitment to future infrastructure, even as short-term market gloom tests the patience of every investor watching the boards.
