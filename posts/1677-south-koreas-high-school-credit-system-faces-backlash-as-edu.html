South Korea's High School Credit System Faces Backlash as Educators Demand Near-Total Overhaul

October 29, 2026

South Korea's ambitious High School Credit System—designed to let secondary students choose their own classes like university undergraduates—is facing intense pressure for radical reform or outright repeal as educators warn the policy is collapsing under the weight of the country's hyper-competitive college entrance race.

  • Calls for near-repeal: Gyeonggi Provincial Council member Kim Han-seul has publicly urged educational authorities to pursue reforms tantamount to abolishing the system, arguing that forcing 15-year-olds to lock in career paths creates extreme anxiety and turns elective coursework into high-stakes college prep.
  • Teacher unions demand emergency talks: Three major national teachers' associations have demanded a pan-governmental consultative body, urging the government to halt rushed secondary evaluation reforms—such as descriptive and essay-based testing—until operational chaos in schools is resolved.
  • Widening school disparities: A two-year study of 77 general high schools by the South Chungcheong Provincial Office of Education revealed severe inequities between well-resourced and underfunded campuses, showing that teachers are swamped by administrative red tape rather than mentoring students.
  • Collision with 2028 entrance exam reforms: With the first cohort governed by the credit scheme nearing their final high school year, frontline educators warn that aligning the credit system with the upcoming 2028 university admissions overhaul risks total classroom disruption.

Why the Flexible Elective Model Is Cracking

The fundamental conflict stems from transplanting a flexible, student-driven elective model into South Korea's rigid, zero-sum admissions culture. Designed to shift secondary schooling away from rote memorization toward self-directed exploration, the framework requires students to select specialized subjects as early as age 15. In practice, however, students and parents treat elective options not as opportunities to discover personal interests, but as tactical stepping stones to build competitive transcripts for university admissions.

Critics also point to policy rushed through without infrastructural preparation. Pushed aggressively during Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, the system mandated that high schools offer broad course catalogs without supplying the necessary teaching staff or standardized assessment guidelines. Instead of fostering student growth, frontline teachers report that coordinating niche electives has triggered massive administrative overhead, taking time away from actual instruction.

The Road Ahead for the Government

The findings from the South Chungcheong Provincial Office of Education highlight a geographic divide that policy rhetoric has failed to bridge. While prestigious high schools in affluent districts can easily offer diverse curricula, normal high schools in provincial areas lack the staffing and budget to sustain specialized tracks, inadvertently punishing students based on where they attend school.

For the current Democratic Party administration, the debate has shifted from technical adjustments to political accountability. Frontline educators argue that tweaking class schedules will accomplish nothing unless curriculum design is fundamentally decoupled from university entrance testing. Without a clear retreat from rigid administrative mandates and an infusion of targeted resources, South Korea's flagship educational reform risks being remembered as another policy experiment that placed institutional goals ahead of classroom reality.
