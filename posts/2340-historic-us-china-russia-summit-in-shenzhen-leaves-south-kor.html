Historic US-China-Russia Summit in Shenzhen Leaves South Korea Navigating Great-Power Diplomacy

November 15, 2026

An unprecedented three-way summit between US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin has been confirmed for November 19 in Shenzhen, while a highly anticipated meeting between Trump and North Korean leader Kim Jong-un has been officially ruled out.

  • What happened: Leaders of the United States, China, and Russia will gather on the sidelines of the APEC summit to discuss nuclear arms reduction, the conflict in Iran, and a potential $30 billion tariff reduction package between Washington and Beijing.
  • What it changes: A prospective US-North Korea summit is off the schedule, pushing direct Pyongyang-Washington talks to the background as superpowers focus on global security and trade realignments.
  • Who is affected: South Korea faces diplomatic pressure over the risk of being sidelined in regional security decisions, prompting foreign ministry officials to warn against a "21st-century Yalta Conference."
  • Diplomatic maneuvers: Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi sought to reassure Seoul that recent Beijing-Pyongyang high-level meetings remain strictly economic, while South Korea seeks a bilateral meeting between President Lee Jae-myung and Xi Jinping to safeguard its interests.

Great Power Bargaining and the 'Yalta' Shadow

The Shenzhen summit marks a dramatic shift toward trilateral superpower diplomacy, bringing together the leaders of the world's three main nuclear powers. With agendas spanning nuclear arms control, Middle Eastern instability, and trade concessions, the gathering signals an effort by Washington, Beijing, and Moscow to manage strategic rivalries. By setting aside disruptive side issues—such as intelligence scandals and tech-sector controversies surrounding Nvidia—the three powers appear focused on transactional bargains. However, the absence of a US-North Korea dialogue during the trip highlights how Korean Peninsula denuclearization has slipped down the list of global priorities.

This dynamic has raised alarm in Seoul. Senior South Korean foreign ministry officials, including Vice Foreign Minister Cho Hyun, have explicitly warned against a scenario reminiscent of the 1945 Yalta Conference, where superpowers carved up post-war spheres of influence without consulting smaller regional states. The fear is that trilateral compromises among Washington, Beijing, and Moscow could undermine South Korea's security autonomy or settle regional security architectures over Seoul's head.

Seoul's High-Stakes Balancing Act

In response, President Lee Jae-myung of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea is actively arranging a bilateral summit with Xi Jinping during his upcoming trip to APEC and the Philippines. Seoul aims to secure commitments that China will play a constructive role in maintaining stability on the peninsula. While Beijing has framed its recent diplomatic exchanges with North Korean officials as purely focused on economic cooperation rather than military alignment, South Korean policymakers remain wary of Beijing using its leverage over Pyongyang as a bargaining chip against Washington.

As the major powers negotiate trade tariffs and strategic boundaries, South Korea faces a challenging diplomatic environment. Navigating this era of superpower bargaining will require Seoul to maintain clear national security boundaries while ensuring its voice is heard in bilateral and multilateral forums alike.
