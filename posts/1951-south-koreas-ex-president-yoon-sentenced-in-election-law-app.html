South Korea's Ex-President Yoon Sentenced in Election Law Appeal, Saddling Opposition with a $30 Million Bill

November 04, 2026

  • An appellate court upheld a 18-month suspended prison sentence for former South Korean President Yoon Suk-yeol, finalizing his loss of eligibility to hold public office.
  • The ruling forces the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition, to repay about $30 million (397 billion won) in state-reimbursed campaign funds.
  • The court found Yoon guilty of spreading false information during the campaign regarding his ties to a controversial shaman, disproving his claims that he and his wife never met the figure.
  • Meanwhile, key loyalists are falling: former lawmaker Kweon Seong-dong lost his parliamentary seat after a final 2-year prison sentence for illegal political funds, while high-ranking judges face separate corruption and budget probes.

The final confirmation of Yoon's conviction marks a historic turning point for South Korea's legal and political landscape. Yoon, who was impeached and removed from office, now holds the tragic distinction of having a lower court's election law violation ruling fully upheld. The verdict squarely targets what the court termed persistent falsehoods by a former leader who tried to sway voters by denying close personal ties to a shaman known as "Geonjin Beopsa." Investigators proved that both Yoon and his wife had visited the shaman's temple and even invited him to their home, completely undermining the ex-president's pre-election alibis.

For the conservative People Power Party, the legal defeat brings an unprecedented financial shock: returning roughly $30 million to the National Election Commission within months. Yet, rather than facing immediate bankruptcy, the party is weighing asset sales—such as its prime Seoul headquarters, estimated to be worth well over $70 million—alongside member fundraising and state loan options. Party leaders are attempting to rebrand the repayment pressure as political retaliation by the ruling Democratic Party, seeking to rally their base ahead of upcoming local re-elections.

The fallout extends far beyond party finances, as the once-powerful inner circle of Yoon loyalists unravels. Beyond Kweon's departure and impending by-elections in his stronghold of Gangneung, opposition heavyweights face separate legal troubles, from Seoul Mayor Oh Sehoon's alleged interference in a public transport corruption probe to allegations of academic résumé padding involving lawmaker Han Dong-hoon's family. Even the judiciary is under fire, with the Supreme Court facing scrutiny over opaque "skill development" cash bonuses for senior justices. As Yoon languishes in prison—and his legal team attempts bizarre defenses, such as claiming officials were too drunk during late-night martial law discussions to orchestrate a rebellion—the ruling closes another chapter on a tumultuous era of South Korean politics.
