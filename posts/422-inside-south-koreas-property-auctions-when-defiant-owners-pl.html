Inside South Korea's Property Auctions: When Defiant Owners Play Hardball

September 08, 2026

South Korea's property auction market offers bargain hunters steep discounts, but winning a bid often means stepping into a psychological war with defiant former owners who refuse to leave.

  • What happened: A South Korean real estate investor detailing their fourth auction win of 2025 encountered an owner who fled the property after an official lease registration, handed the digital door password to state guarantors, and stayed put to extort moving fees from the new buyer.
  • What it changes: The case exposes how bad actors exploit legal loopholes surrounding eviction clearance certificates to stall new owners and demand cash settlements.
  • Who is affected: Auction buyers must navigate hostile occupants, potential criminal charges for unlawful entry if they act prematurely, and complex tax hurdles when claiming eviction expenses.

The Reality of Auction Evictions and Difficult Occupants

For inexperienced investors, the most terrifying part of buying a distressed property through court auctions is the eviction process, known locally as myeongdo. Yet seasoned investors point out that the system inherently favors the winning bidder—provided they pair cold financial math with strict legal procedures rather than emotional outbursts.

In this particular case, the investor faced an owner who hid behind state-backed guarantees after failing to repay a 165 million won (about $120,000) key-money deposit (jeonse). Despite not actually living in the unit, the owner demanded a 2 million won (about $1,500) moving fee while stringing the buyer along with false meeting times.

Why Legal Protections Beat Empty Threats

Many novice investors make the critical mistake of pausing formal legal actions, such as filing for an eviction order (indomyeongryung), simply because informal negotiations seem to be going well. That is a dangerous gamble. Occupants can change their tune overnight, and failing to secure a court-backed eviction order immediately can add months of costly delays.

Furthermore, investors must resist the temptation to force their way into vacant-looking properties before receiving formal legal possession. Breaking a lock without an official execution officer present can land the rightful owner in criminal trouble for trespassing and property damage. Success in South Korea's auction market ultimately belongs to those who keep their emotions in check, file the paperwork on day one, and let the courts do the heavy lifting.
