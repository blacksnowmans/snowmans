The Reality of Making $7,000 a Month with Airbnb in Korea

September 08, 2026

  • The Promise: Social media is rife with claims that hosting on Airbnb can generate 10 million won (about $7,000) in monthly passive income.
  • The Reality: High profits are often overshadowed by risks, including illegal property usage, regulatory hurdles, and poor location analysis.
  • Key Strategy: Successful hosts focus on "low-cost, high-impact" interior design using traditional Korean aesthetics to drive bookings, rather than expensive renovations.
  • Legal Warning: Authorities are cracking down on illegal rentals—specifically those operating in residential apartments without proper registration—leading to hefty fines and shutdowns.

Design over Dollars

Many novice hosts make the mistake of overspending on luxury furniture. However, experienced operators know that atmosphere is created by details, not price tags. Using items from traditional hubs like Gwangjang Market—a famous street food and textile market in Seoul—can create a unique "Korean" aesthetic that resonates with foreign tourists. Small, affordable items like 5,000 won (about $3.50) pouches or traditional coin purses serve as perfect welcome gifts. Even simple items like 2,000 won table covers can be repurposed as wall decor to change the texture of a room. This approach isn't just about saving money; it’s about creating an "Instagrammable" space that encourages guests to share photos, which functions as free, powerful marketing.

The "Survival" Checklist

Before signing a lease, prospective hosts must look beyond the potential revenue. The most critical step is verifying that the property is legally eligible for short-term lodging. Many beginners ignore property zoning laws, only to find out later that their "side hustle" is classified as an illegal business. The Seoul Metropolitan Government’s special judicial police unit has recently ramped up investigations into illegal lodging operators who rent out apartments or studios without proper registration. These illegal setups often fail to meet fire safety standards or cause noise disturbances for neighbors. While the government has recently eased some regulations for "Foreigner-designated Urban Bed & Breakfasts"—allowing even older, aging homes to register if they meet safety standards—this lower barrier to entry does not replace the need for due diligence. If you are looking at Airbnb as a "second pension," treat it like a business: verify your permits, research your location's demand outside of peak tourist seasons, and ensure your business model is sustainable rather than just a quick way to make a buck. In the competitive Korean market, preparation—not luck—is the only way to ensure your venture remains profitable for the long term.
