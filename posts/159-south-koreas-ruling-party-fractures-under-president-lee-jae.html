South Korea's Ruling Party Fractures Under President Lee Jae-myung

September 04, 2026

  • What happened:
    •  South Korea's liberal Democratic Party, which swept to power under President Lee Jae-myung, is tearing itself apart through infighting over judicial reform, party democracy, and factional loyalty.
  • What it changes:
    •  Deep rifts between party hardliners and the presidential office have broken open into public view, highlighted by the sidelining of key party figures like former Supreme Council member Jung Chung-rae.
  • Who is affected:
    •  President Lee's administration, core liberal supporters disillusioned by compromised reform promises, and the Democratic Party's internal factions as they position for future influence.

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party is eating its own. Following a hard-fought presidential victory, the party expected a unified march toward sweeping institutional reform. Instead, profound disagreements over how to wield power have exposed a widening chasm between President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the party’s traditional base. What began as subtle policy disagreements has devolved into a bitter war over who truly speaks for the president.

The rot set in early. Despite promises to overhaul South Korea's entrenched judicial system, the administration repeatedly appointed former prosecutors from elite investigative units to key civil-affairs posts. For supporters who voted for radical change, watching establishment figures placed at the helm of reform felt like a betrayal. Meanwhile, legislative efforts stalled as party hardliners demanded swift action while the government urged caution, turning policy debates into petty turf wars over political branding.

Factional tensions finally boiled over following local elections. While party leadership tried to spin the results as a victory, President Lee offered a cold assessment: failing to win winnable races is not success. That subtle rebuke signaled a ruthless realignment of power. When key party leader Jung Chung-rae was conspicuously omitted from an airport send-off for the president—replaced by rival Kim Min-seok—it served as a stark public symbol that the party's axis of power had shifted, leaving core supporters anxious that their voices are being systematically locked out.
