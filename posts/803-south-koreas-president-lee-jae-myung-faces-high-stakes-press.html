South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Faces High-Stakes Press Conference Amid Plunging Approval Ratings

September 18, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung will hold his seventh presidential press conference tomorrow morning at the Blue House, facing his lowest approval ratings since taking office, persistent staffing controversies, and mounting public frustration over economic and foreign policies.

  • Plunging Support: Recent polls show President Lee's approval rating dropping to 33.8% across nine consecutive weeks of decline, with negative ratings crossing 60% for the first time.
  • High-Stakes Format: The 90-minute, un-titled session will cover political, diplomatic, economic, and policy issues before domestic and foreign reporters, with organizers promising unfiltered questioning and real-time public comments.
  • Key Flashpoints: Public anger is fueled by recent university admissions server crashes, controversies surrounding cabinet nominees, and widespread domestic opposition to potential military deployment to the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Public Discontent: Beyond traditional politics, younger South Koreans are pushing back against shifting college entrance exam rules and foreign policy commitments that conflict with public sentiment.

The Crisis of Confidence and the Burden of Public Opinion

Approval numbers in South Korean politics rarely lie. While presidential aides often attempt to blame external factors like opposition framing or investigative security issues, the reality on the ground points to a widening chasm between the administration and everyday citizens—particularly among younger voters. History shows that previous South Korean presidents navigated similar crises either through defensive deflection—which deepened their troubles—or by meeting the public with direct, humbling accountability.

Tomorrow's briefing represents a critical test of whether the administration can pivot from defensive spin to radical candor. With major policy missteps, botched cabinet appointments, and foreign entanglement risks hanging over the government, evasive answers or scripted talking points will only accelerate the slide in public trust. The core demand from the public is not perfection, but a genuine acknowledgment of missteps and a clear roadmap for correction.

Beyond Politics: Stressed Students and Foreign Policy Backlash

The administration's vulnerabilities extend well beyond abstract political debates into concrete grievances. A recent technical collapse of university admissions servers left hundreds of thousands of student applicants facing chaotic, shifting competition rates, triggering planned student protests outside the National Assembly. Compounding this domestic frustration is the deeply unpopular prospect of deploying South Korean forces to the Strait of Hormuz. Polls indicate that clear majorities across nearly all age groups—including 50% of young adults in their twenties and 63% of those in their forties—oppose military deployment, viewing it as a foreign entanglement rather than a national interest.

As President Lee steps up to the microphone, his challenge is clear. The administration can no longer rely on loyalist framing or view criticism as a threat to be managed. To recover its footing, the government must treat tomorrow not as a public relations exercise, but as an overdue reckoning with the realities facing South Korean society.
