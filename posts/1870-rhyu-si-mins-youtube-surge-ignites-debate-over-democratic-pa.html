Rhyu Si-min’s YouTube Surge Ignites Debate Over Democratic Party Merger Timing

November 02, 2026

Progressive commentator Rhyu Si-min’s recent appearance on YouTube drew nearly two million views, reigniting a high-stakes debate over how and when the ruling Democratic Party of Korea should integrate with its key allies ahead of upcoming local elections.

  • What happened: Intellectual and author Rhyu Si-min appeared on the progressive YouTube channel Parksiyoung TV, pulling between 1.75 million and 2 million views and briefly helping the channel surpass senior lawmaker Jung Chung-rae’s subscriber count (716,000 to 714,000).
  • What it changes: Jung Chung-rae, a four-term lawmaker in the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), is using his platform to push a firm January deadline to merge with the minor allied Rebuilding Korea Party.
  • Why it matters: DPK rules require members to pay party dues for at least six months to vote in candidate primaries; a merger finalized later than January would disenfranchise incoming members for the spring primary season.
  • The broader view: Rhyu urged caution against forced uniformity, comparing modern progressive factions to different branches of Korea's historic independence movement that shared a common goal through different methods.

The Power of New Media in Korean Politics

Rhyu Si-min’s massive viewership highlights how political discourse among South Korean progressives has migrated from legacy television to YouTube. Following the turbulent period of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure—which ended in his impeachment and current imprisonment—voters under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration are demanding substantive political analysis rather than sensationalism. For ruling party politicians, engaging with independent digital platforms is essential. Jung Chung-rae himself reportedly watched Rhyu’s commentary twice to digest how his party reform proposals were being received by commentators and the party base.

Strategic Integration vs. Coalition of Differences

Jung’s deadline for merging with the Rebuilding Korea Party relies on practical internal calculations. Delaying integration into February or March would prevent former Rebuilding Korea Party members from meeting the six-month membership requirement needed to vote in primary elections, likely forcing contentious top-down candidate nominations. By pushing for a January deadline, Jung aims to keep the selection process within transparent party rules.

However, Rhyu’s historical analogy serves as a reminder that political alliance does not require absolute ideological absorption. As the DPK transitions from an opposition stance into the governing party under President Lee, progressive media commentators and political leaders face a new task: moving beyond resistance rhetoric to manage diverse opinions responsibly while preserving coalition strength.
