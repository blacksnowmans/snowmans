Civil War Erupts in South Korea’s Ruling Party as Leadership Approval Plunges

November 12, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is descending into a bitter internal feud over candidate nominations and suspicious consulting fees, threatening to stall the legislative momentum of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

  • Factional Infighting: High-ranking members are trading public insults, with Rep. Choi Min-hee labeling internal rivals "hyenas," while others accuse the leadership of purging political opponents.
  • Consulting Fee Scandal: A report revealed that a prominent pro-government political YouTuber, Park Si-young, received 279 million KRW (about $200,000) in consulting fees, sparking allegations of paid opinion-rigging during party primaries.
  • Leadership Crisis: The approval rating for DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok has plummeted to 23.7%, roughly half of President Lee Jae-myung’s 44% approval, signaling a massive disconnect between the party base and its management.
  • Nomination Power Struggle: The core of the conflict is control over the "system nomination" process for upcoming elections, with the "Chin-Chung" faction (allied with senior leader Jung Chung-rae) accusing the current leadership of favoritism.

The Price of Influence and the "Speaker" Controversy

The conflict reached a breaking point following revelations regarding Park Si-young, a digital strategist and influential "speaker" (political YouTuber) for the liberal camp. Reports that his firm received approximately $200,000 (279 million KRW) for consulting has led to accusations that the party leadership is subsidizing friendly media voices to marginalize internal critics. Rep. Kim Woo-young, a close aide to the President and former head of the party’s innovation committee, has slammed these payments as "deceptive advertising" intended to influence internal primaries.

This "speaker politics" is a unique feature of the Korean liberal landscape, where high-traffic YouTube channels often exert more influence over the party base than traditional media. If these platforms are being paid to target specific internal rivals, it undermines the DPK’s "system nomination" protocol—a set of rules designed to ensure fair, merit-based candidate selection. For many party members, this looks less like strategic consulting and more like a taxpayer-funded purge of the leadership’s political enemies.

A Leadership Disconnected from the President’s Mandate

The internal metrics are devastating for Chairman Kim Min-seok. While President Lee Jae-myung maintains a relatively stable approval rating of around 44%, the party leadership’s support has cratered to nearly 23%. This suggests that while the liberal base remains loyal to the President and his agenda of "cleansing" the legal system—centered on the courts in Seocho-dong (Seoul's legal district)—they have lost all confidence in the party’s ability to govern itself. The People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, has already begun using these internal fractures to attack the government’s stability.

The crisis is being compounded by a series of ethical lapses, including vague handling of controversies related to the Shincheonji religious group and internal harassment allegations. Rep. Jung Chung-rae, a powerful senior figure within the party, has publicly demanded that Chairman Kim apologize and provide a roadmap for reform. In my view, the DPK is at a crossroads: it must either restore the integrity of its nomination system or risk becoming a liability that drags down President Lee’s executive power. If the ruling party continues to prioritize "bread-and-butter" factional infighting over national reform, they may find themselves facing a base that has simply moved on.
