South Korea's Chip Giants Face Market Panic, But Industry Fundamentals Point Up

October 13, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean chipmakers Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are suffering steep stock drops amid broad market panic, driven by foreign sell-offs and macro anxiety rather than fundamental weakness.
  • What it changes: Despite sliding shares, the memory market is transitioning into a structural growth industry supercharged by AI infrastructure, with global DRAM revenue projected to cross $1 trillion next year.
  • Who is affected: Global tech giants relying on AI data centers, South Korean retail and foreign investors, and major memory producers navigating tightening supply chains and US policy shifts.

South Korea's stock market is currently gripped by fears of a severe semiconductor slump, dragging down heavyweights like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. However, veteran research analyst Noh Geun-chang argues that the current sell-off is a mismatch between falling share prices and a booming industry. Rather than a cyclical crash, the memory market is experiencing a structural shift driven by relentless demand for data center infrastructure, leaving supply tight and inventories healthy.

The nature of memory demand has fundamentally changed. While PC and smartphone markets once dictated the chip cycle, data centers now consume the vast majority of server DRAM, reducing historic market volatility. This structural shift means memory makers hold strong pricing power as major tech players scramble for hardware. Furthermore, heavy investments in new fabrication plants globally confirm that AI infrastructure is a durable, long-term buildout rather than a fleeting trend—even as foreign investors rapidly trim their stakes in South Korean equities due to broader macroeconomic risk aversion.

Ultimately, the disconnect between depressed stock prices and surging corporate earnings presents a classic valuation paradox. With foreign ownership in Samsung sitting near multi-year lows and corporate fundamentals remaining exceptionally strong, the current market panic looks like a temporary psychological glitch. While geopolitical risks—such as unpredictable US subsidy policies under Washington—remain a wild card, the underlying tech engine driving global AI and data infrastructure is running hotter than ever.
