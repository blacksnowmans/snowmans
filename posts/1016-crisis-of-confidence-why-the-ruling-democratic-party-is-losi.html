Crisis of Confidence: Why the Ruling Democratic Party is Losing Its Grip

October 12, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party (DP), led by President Lee Jae-myung, is facing a severe political crisis as internal infighting and a disconnection from the public lead to a sharp decline in both digital influence and approval ratings.

  • Plummeting Digital Engagement: Official DP YouTube channels, once powerful platforms, are seeing record-low engagement, with major streams failing to attract even 200 concurrent viewers.
  • Approval Rating Reversal: A recent Realmeter poll shows the Democratic Party’s support falling to 35.5%, trailing the main opposition People Power Party (the PPP, South Korea’s primary conservative bloc) at 41.3%.
  • Economic Woes: Public frustration is mounting over surging Seoul apartment prices and a stagnant stock market, which the government has struggled to address.
  • Internal Purges: Critics within the party who call for reform are being sidelined and silenced by the party’s pro-Lee mainstream, further alienating moderate supporters.

The Collapse of Digital Dominance and Public Trust

The Democratic Party’s digital footprint, once a dominant force in South Korean politics, has effectively imploded. When a channel with 600,000 subscribers struggles to pull in 200 concurrent viewers, it is no longer just a "slump"—it is a structural failure. While some pro-government channels have attempted to inflate numbers with marathon broadcasts, the content is widely criticized as lackluster and out of touch with the average voter.

This decline reflects a broader breakdown in the relationship between the party and its base. By prioritizing echo-chamber politics, the Democratic Party has alienated moderate voters and even segments of its core supporters. Instead of engaging in policy-driven communication, the party leadership appears trapped in a feedback loop that rewards extreme fandom while ignoring the economic realities of rising housing costs and market instability.

From Reformers to "Internal Enemies"

Perhaps the most concerning trend is the party’s aggressive stance against its own internal reformers. Prominent figures—including Choo Mi-ae and Im Eun-jeong—who helped define the party’s identity are now being labeled as "traitors" or targets for exclusion. This "silencing" (or iptleumak) of dissenting voices has created a toxic internal culture that mirrors the aggressive rhetoric of the far-right.

The use of derogatory terms like "jwahan-hage" (a play on words using the prefix for "left-wing" to mock "elegance") further alienates reasonable citizens. By adopting the same smear tactics once associated with far-right internet trolls, the Democratic Party is eroding its own moral authority. For a government that rose to power on promises of reform, becoming a party that punishes internal debate is a betrayal of its founding principles.

Ultimately, the government’s reliance on blaming others—whether it be the opposition or the media—for its current struggles is failing. Without a return to genuine policy solutions and an end to the culture of internal purges, these poll numbers may be the beginning of a much larger collapse for the Lee Jae-myung administration.
