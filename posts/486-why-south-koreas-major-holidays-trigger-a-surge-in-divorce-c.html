Why South Korea's Major Holidays Trigger a Surge in Divorce Consultations

September 30, 2026

South Korea's major holiday seasons like Chuseok and Lunar New Year have become infamous catalysts for marital breakdown, with legal and psychological experts reporting an immediate spike in divorce inquiries as soon as the holidays end.

  • The Trend: Legal practices and counseling centers across South Korea see a sharp surge in divorce mediation requests immediately following major family holidays.
  • The Shift: Traditional mother-in-law and daughter-in-law clashes are being overtaken by intense friction between mothers-in-law and sons-in-law.
  • The Catalyst: Deep-rooted parental interference, economic comparisons, and strict credential vetting turn family gatherings into high-stress compliance tests.

Beyond the Mother-in-Law: The Rise of Son-in-Law Stress

For decades, Korean media and daily life framed holiday stress almost exclusively around the mistreatment of wives by traditional mothers-in-law. However, legal and psychological experts—including prominent psychologist Park Sang-mi and divorce lawyer Lee In-chul—note a profound shift. Today, the flashpoint has increasingly become the relationship between mothers-in-law and sons-in-law.

As parental protection and expectations surrounding daughters shift, some mothers-in-law have stepped into a role that evaluates a son-in-law’s career, university background, and financial assets with rigid scrutiny. Even men with prestigious corporate jobs find themselves on the receiving end of pointed critiques regarding housing status, promotions, and wealth during family meals. Rather than celebrating a couple's life, these holidays frequently function as a high-pressure audit of a family's socioeconomic standing.

The Clash Between Modern Independence and Feudal Family Control

At the root of these escalating crises is a fundamental contradiction in contemporary South Korea. Younger generations increasingly embrace independent, nuclear-family lifestyles modeled after Western standards, yet older generations cling to a traditional, patriarchal belief that adult children remain an extension of the broader family unit.

When families are forced into close quarters for days at a time during holidays, this structural tension explodes. Parents often cross the line from well-meaning advice into invasive surveillance of a couple's finances, lifestyle, and private lives. Shockingly, experts report that some parents—particularly mothers—push their daughters toward divorce even when the couples themselves wish to stay together, prioritizing family pride and unmet expectations over the actual stability of the marriage.

Ultimately, these recurring crises expose an uncomfortable truth about how transactional marriage and family obligations remain in South Korea. Until older generations learn to respect their children as fully autonomous adults—and couples learn to firmly set boundaries against external interference—these historic holidays will continue to double as an annual stress test that pushes strained marriages past the breaking point.
