Can Faking a Smile Actually Cure Depression? South Korea’s Experts Weigh In

October 04, 2026

  • What happened: A recent online discussion featuring psychiatrist Kim Jong-ki and laughter therapist Oh Haeng-ja explored whether forced laughter and physical exercises can genuinely alleviate clinical depression.
  • The science: While traditional cognitive behavioral therapy does not formally prescribe fake smiling, experts point to the "facial feedback hypothesis"—suggesting that relaxing facial tension, such as smoothing out a furrowed brow with Botox, sends positive signals to the brain.
  • The physical link: Neurological pathways tie physical pain ("somatic pain") directly to emotional distress, meaning simple stretches, walks, and physical relief can actively reduce emotional suffering.
  • Who is affected: Busy urbanites and overworked professionals who suppress stress behind a permanent frown, often locking their emotions behind rigid social taboos against public silliness.

South Korea’s modern work culture is notoriously high-pressure, leaving many residents exhausted, cynical, and emotionally burnt out. When professional stress turns into chronic low moods, finding an accessible remedy can feel impossible. But can something as simple as pulling the corners of your mouth upward—or doing silly, choreographed breathing exercises—actually rewire a depressed brain?

According to the medical specialists featured in the recent online video, the mind and body are far more intertwined than we admit. We tend to believe that genuine happiness must precede a smile, but human physiology works in reverse, too. Triggering physical expressions of joy releases endorphins and drops cortisol levels, fooling the brain into recognizing safety and relaxation. Conversely, living with a permanently furrowed brow tells the brain that you are under constant threat, deepening the cycle of anxiety and despair.

Breaking the Taboo of Expressing Joy

The discussion highlights how modern social norms trap us in stoicism. Adults are taught to maintain dignity, keep a straight face, and avoid looking foolish. Yet, physical therapy sessions that force participants to clap, count out loud, act goofy, and stroke their own shoulders serve as a necessary release valve for pent-up frustration. These activities might feel embarrassing at first, but that awkwardness is just evidence of how deeply ingrained our emotional repression has become.

Ultimately, the experts argue that recovering from burnout requires breaking those self-imposed rules of seriousness. When the weight of daily life makes you want to withdraw and hide, physical movement and shared laughter offer a practical, low-barrier entry point to emotional healing. You do not need a grand spiritual breakthrough to lighten a heavy day—sometimes, simply unclenching your jaw and letting out a forced chuckle is enough to crack the armor.
