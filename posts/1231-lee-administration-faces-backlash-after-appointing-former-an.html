Lee Administration Faces Backlash After Appointing Former "Anti-Reform" Prosecutor to Lead New Investigative Body

October 16, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung has officially appointed Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO), sparking a fierce backlash from the president's own supporters and progressive allies who view the pick as a betrayal of prosecution reform.

  • The New Agency: The MCIO was established by the ruling Democratic Party to finalize the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, effectively ending the Prosecution Service’s century-long monopoly on direct criminal investigations.
  • The Controversy: Kim Ji-yong is a former high-ranking prosecutor who publicly lobbied against these very reforms in 2022 and signed a 2020 collective protest to defend Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president currently in prison following his impeachment.
  • A Split Mandate: The National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee issued a rare "split" report listing Kim as both "fit" and "unfit," yet the Blue House (the South Korean presidential office) finalized the appointment within 24 hours.
  • High-Level Criticism: Gyeonggi Governor Chu Mi-ae, a heavyweight in the Democratic Party, has publicly denounced Kim as a "mole" who prioritized organizational loyalty over judicial justice during the previous administration.

A Compromise on Reform for the Sake of "Stability"

The Lee administration’s decision to prioritize "expertise" and "organizational stability" by appointing a career prosecutor smells of the same pragmatism that has historically backfired on Korean liberals. By choosing a man who actively campaigned to keep investigative powers within the Prosecution Service, the government is placing the fox in charge of the henhouse. To many "candlelight" protesters—the citizens who fueled the movement to reform the judiciary—this isn't just a questionable hire; it is a signal that the administration is more interested in a smooth bureaucratic transition than a radical break from the "prosecutorial republic" of the past.

The internal friction within the Democratic Party highlights a growing rift between the leadership and its base. While party leaders call for "restraint" to support the President’s choice, grassroots supporters and influential voices like Governor Chu warn of a "deja vu" moment. They point specifically to 2019, when the liberal bloc rallied behind Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s appointment as Prosecutor General based on his promises of reform—promises that were discarded the moment he took office, eventually leading to the national crisis and his subsequent impeachment.

The Risk of Repeating History

For a government that rose to power on the promise of finishing prosecution reform, appointing a figure who stood on the opposite side of that battle is a dangerous gamble. Critics, including the influential group MINBYUN (Lawyers for a Democratic Society), argue that someone who fundamentally disagreed with the MCIO's founding philosophy cannot be trusted to build its institutional culture. If Kim Ji-yong uses his new authority to protect the interests of his former colleagues rather than dismantling their privileges, the political cost for President Lee will be immense.

Ultimately, this appointment suggests a worrying level of overconfidence within the Blue House. By dismissing the concerns of the party's core supporters as "excessive," the leadership risks alienating the very people who provide their political mandate. In Korean politics, when a reformist government begins to look indistinguishable from the establishment it sought to replace, its public support rarely remains intact for long.
