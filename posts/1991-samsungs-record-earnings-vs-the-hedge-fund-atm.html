Samsung’s Record Earnings vs. The "Hedge Fund ATM"

November 05, 2026

Massive short-selling by global hedge funds and outdated regulatory hurdles are decoupling Samsung Electronics’ stock price from its strong financial performance, pushing domestic investors to a breaking point.

  • Global hedge funds are executing record-breaking short positions and "degrossing" (rapidly reducing risk exposure) in the Korean market, treating the KOSPI as a source of quick liquidity.
  • The Korea Discount is being worsened by the "Act on the Structural Improvement of the Financial Industry," which prevents Samsung from aggressive share cancellations due to the 10% ownership cap on financial affiliates like Samsung Life.
  • Market valuations have reached a level of extreme pessimism, currently pricing in an irrational scenario where profits drop by 50% every year through 2030.
  • Retail investor trust has evaporated, with many "Seohak-gaemi" (Korean retail investors in U.S. stocks) fleeing the domestic market for Wall Street as indices remain stagnant despite solid corporate earnings.

The Regulatory Catch-22 and Hedge Fund Predation

The primary reason Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (Korea’s second-largest chipmaker) are struggling is not a lack of profit, but a structural supply-demand distortion. Global hedge funds are using sophisticated spot-and-futures manipulation to keep a lid on the index. As South Korea’s weight in emerging market indices grew, passive funds were forced to mechanically sell down Korean holdings. This "basket selling" created a vacuum where hedge funds could aggressively short the stock without fear of being squeezed by domestic buyers.

Internal barriers are equally damaging. While investors demand that Samsung cancel its treasury shares to boost value, the company is trapped by the "Act on the Structural Improvement of the Financial Industry." If Samsung cancels shares, the relative ownership percentage of its financial affiliates—such as Samsung Life Insurance—would rise. By law, these affiliates cannot own more than 10% of a non-financial firm. Without a legislative exception, Samsung’s most powerful tool for shareholder return remains locked behind a regulatory wall, reinforcing the "Korea Discount."

Political Scars and the Valuation Gap

The market is also grappling with the lingering economic "scars" of the previous administration. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the use of prosecutorial pressure and heavy-handed political interference in the capital markets damaged investor confidence. While the current government under President Lee Jae-myung—of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—is working to restore democratic procedures and stabilize relations with North Korea, the "negative spiral" in the markets remains difficult to break. Professional funds that took on too much leverage during the AI boom are now being forced to liquidate, creating a cascade of selling that ignores fundamental value.

Current valuations assume a catastrophic "peak-out" for the semiconductor industry that isn't supported by data. Samsung’s price-to-book ratio has fallen below 1.1x, meaning the market barely values the company’s physical assets, let alone its future earnings. This extreme pessimism suggests that investors are pricing in a decade of decline. However, with U.S. "reshoring" efforts proving slow and expensive due to a lack of skilled labor and high costs, Korean chipmakers are likely to maintain their competitive edge for much longer than the current "short play" suggests. The catalyst for a reversal will likely require a bold regulatory shift from the Democratic Party-led government to unlock shareholder value.
