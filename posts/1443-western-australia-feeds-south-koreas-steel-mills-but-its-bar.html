Western Australia Feeds South Korea's Steel Mills, but Its Barren Interior Rejects Human Settlement

October 25, 2026

South Korea relies on Western Australia for seven out of every ten tons of its iron ore imports, yet the colossal territory sustaining the nation's heavy industry remains one of the emptiest and most inhospitable expanses on Earth.

  • Massive territory, minimal settlement: Spanning 2.53 million square kilometers—more than eleven times the size of the entire Korean Peninsula—Western Australia is home to fewer than 3 million people, less than the population of Busan, South Korea's second-largest city.
  • Extreme concentration: Roughly 80 percent of the population is clustered in the southwest corner around Perth, leaving inland administrative districts the size of entire nations populated by barely 10,000 residents.
  • Vital economic engine: Despite housing just 11 percent of Australia’s population, the state generates 45 percent of the country's total exports, anchoring the global supply chain for raw commodities.
  • A forced compromise: Modern mining operations extract vast fortunes through Fly-In, Fly-Out (FIFO) shifts rather than building permanent communities, tacitly conceding to three unyielding natural barriers: hyper-aridity, ancient nutrient-depleted soil, and brutal geographic isolation.

The Geological and Climatic Prison

The total absence of inland civilization is not a failure of engineering or capital, but the result of hard physical limits. The Australian landmass sits squarely beneath a dry subtropical high-pressure belt, stripped of mountain ranges that could force moisture upward to generate rainfall. Aside from the southwestern coastline—tempered by the Leeuwin Current—the interior is a furnace, with outback outposts like Marble Bar having recorded unbroken stretches of 160 consecutive days above 37.8°C (100°F).

Worse than the absence of water is the ground itself. Western Australia rests on some of the oldest continental crust on Earth, with zircon crystals in the Jack Hills dating back 4.4 billion years. Having escaped both tectonic rejuvenation and glacial resets for hundreds of millions of years, the red earth has had vital life-supporting nutrients like phosphorus and nitrogen completely leached away by weathering. Even when torrential seasonal rains fall in regions like the Kimberley, the ground cannot sustain conventional agriculture; the soil is little more than weathered quartz dust and oxidized iron rust.

Extracted Wealth Without Roots

Every historical attempt to transplant traditional European urban settlement inland has exacted a brutal toll. When the British founded Perth along the Swan River in 1829, lured by inflated reports of fertile soils, early settlers quickly faced starvation on barren coastal sands, ultimately requiring nearly 10,000 British convicts between 1850 and 1868 just to construct basic roads and ports. Decades later, the Kalgoorlie gold rush of the 1890s drew tens of thousands into the waterless interior, where typhoid ran rampant and water cost more than beer.

That crisis spurred chief engineer C.Y. O'Connor to design an audacious 560-kilometer steel pipeline to pump water uphill from Perth into the desert—a triumph completed in 1903, only months after political vilification drove O'Connor to suicide. Today, that pipeline still stands as a testament to the land's stubborn defiance. Billions of dollars in raw iron flow steadily into East Asian blast furnaces, but human presence remains transient: an extractive outpost sustained by aircraft and pipelines, where capital can visit, but society cannot take root.
