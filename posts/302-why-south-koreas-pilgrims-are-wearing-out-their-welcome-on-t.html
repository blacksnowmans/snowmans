Why South Korea's Pilgrims Are Wearing Out Their Welcome on the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

South Korea's booming enthusiasm for the Camino de Santiago has made it one of the trail's top nationalities, but a rise in bad etiquette, tour-group congestion, and cultural tone-deafness is testing the patience of locals and fellow travelers alike.

  • The numbers: South Korea ranked 10th globally in 2024 with about 7,910 official pilgrims, representing the largest Asian contingent on the historic Spanish trail.
  • The clash: Organized tour groups are accused of monopolizing limited accommodations at albergues (pilgrim hostels), shifting luggage by bus, and ignoring trail quiet hours.
  • The friction: Independent travelers and locals report rising tensions over noisy gatherings, unsolicited boasting, religious proselytizing, and a lack of basic shared-dorm courtesy.
  • The stakes: While individual Korean travelers have long been welcomed as warm and generous guests, group behavior risks branding the entire nationality as disruptive tourists.

When Korean Group Culture Collides with Spanish Solitude

The Camino de Santiago—a roughly 800-kilometer medieval pilgrimage network ending at the shrine of apostle Saint James the Great in Santiago de Compostela—is meant to be a space for introspection, physical endurance, and quiet cross-cultural exchange. Yet as commercial tour packages bring in waves of South Korean travelers near the final 100-kilometer mark, that meditative atmosphere often fractures. Group travelers arriving by bus with pre-booked beds frequently crowd out independent hikers who have spent the day walking on foot. Incidents of groups commandeering kitchen facilities, playing guitars in town squares, and bringing high-speed competitive social dynamics into a contemplative space have led some local café owners and hosts to voice open frustration.

Beyond logistics, the trail has seen a cultural disconnect involving personal boundaries. Solo hikers seeking refuge from South Korea's high-pressure corporate and social environments—where age hierarchies and status metrics dominate daily life—find themselves cornered by fellow countrymen boasting about their children's elite university degrees or high-paying jobs at conglomerates like Samsung. Others report religious proselytizing from travelers attempting to reframe the secular journey into an evangelical mission field. Even small oversight issues in shared dorms, such as leaving chat notification sounds on through the night or coughing through severe colds without seeking private rooms, highlight a friction between individual convenience and communal responsibility.

Reframing the Journey: Speed and Status Left Behind

The core tension on the trail mirrors a broader cultural export: the relentless pace and competitive status anxiety of modern South Korean society colliding with a sanctuary designed to strip those exact markers away. With spending by South Korean visitors to Spain recovering significantly—averaging about 550,000 won (about $400) per person daily—their economic footprint is undeniable. Yet the currency that matters most on the Camino is not purchasing power, but basic consideration for others.

Ultimately, the pilgrimage remains vast enough to escape the noise. Travelers bothered by group dynamics can simply change their pace, seek alternative lodging, or lean into the solitude that drew them to Spain in the first place. But for South Korea's future pilgrims, the challenge is internal: leaving the hustle, the age-based social hierarchies, and the urge to conquer behind, and remembering that the true measure of the trail is not speed or endurance, but the quiet grace shown to the strangers walking beside you.
