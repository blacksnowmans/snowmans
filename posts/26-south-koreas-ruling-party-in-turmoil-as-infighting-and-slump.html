South Korea's Ruling Party in Turmoil as Infighting and Slumping Approval Threaten Reform Agenda

September 24, 2026

  • South Korea's ruling Democratic Party is facing a severe internal crisis, with recent polls showing its approval rating plunging 10.2 percentage points to 31.7%, falling behind the conservative People Power Party.
  • Critics, including prominent progressive figures, have lashed out at party leader Kim Min-seok for letting online political forums dictate policy and acting as a rubber stamp for the presidential office.
  • Disagreements over the pace of promised prosecution reforms and key personnel appointments have alienated core supporters, threatening the administration's legislative agenda.

A Ruling Party Adrift: Online Forums and Presidential Deference

When South Koreans voted to change power, the mandate was clear: dismantle the overreach of the country's powerful investigative agencies and push through deep-rooted reforms. Yet, months into the new administration, the ruling Democratic Party—the country's main liberal political force—looks paralyzed. Party leader Kim Min-seok has faced fierce internal backlash for defending the president's unilateral personnel choices, with critics accusing him of practicing "Femco politics," a derogatory term for shaping policy based on volatile reactions from male-dominated online discussion boards.

The friction goes beyond mere messaging. Core promises of prosecution reform, such as stripping prosecutors of supplementary investigation rights and establishing a new investigative agency, have slowed to a crawl. Lawmakers within the party have begun openly rebelling against what they call a submissive relationship with the presidential office, warning that the legislature is turning into a mere administrative sub-agency rather than an independent check on power.

Erosion of the Core Base and the Cost of Complacency

The political fallout has been swift. Prominent political commentator Rhyu Si-min publicly criticized the administration's approach to reform as befitting a monarch rather than a democrat, capturing a growing sense of betrayal among the party's traditional base. Once-reliable voting blocs in their 40s and 50s are showing signs of fracturing, and recent polling data shows the conservative opposition edging ahead for the first time in the current electoral cycle.

The core issue is a widening gulf between party leadership and the voters who put them in office. By attempting to manage public discontent with quick dismissals and light mockery instead of substantive policy arguments, the party's strategists are losing control of the narrative. Unless leadership drops its defensive posture and returns to the reformist principles that brought them to power, this slump in public support may prove to be just the beginning of a deeper political collapse.
