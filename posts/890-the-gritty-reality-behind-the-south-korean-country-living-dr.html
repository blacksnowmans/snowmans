The Gritty Reality Behind the South Korean "Country Living" Dream

October 10, 2026

Moving to a rural home in South Korea is often romanticized as an escape from the frantic pace of city life, but for many, it quickly devolves into an exhausting cycle of maintenance, isolation, and unexpected costs that leads them right back to the city.

  • The "Nature" Trap: The reality of rural life involves constant battles with insects, snakes, and rodents that far exceed what city dwellers encounter.
  • Labor-Intensive Upkeep: Large gardens and lawns are not leisure spaces; they are high-maintenance "labor prisons" that require relentless physical work.
  • Infrastructure Gaps: The lack of delivery services, reliable waste management, and school transport forces parents into becoming full-time personal drivers.
  • High Operational Costs: Heating a standalone home during a six-month-long winter and paying for emergency repairs can create a massive financial burden.

The Hidden Costs of Rural Isolation

The primary disconnect for those fleeing the city is the assumption that nature provides a peaceful sanctuary. In practice, the absence of urban infrastructure makes simple daily tasks a struggle. Without the convenience of delivery apps or nearby amenities, residents face an enforced isolation that can be psychologically draining, especially for younger people needing social connections or older residents who struggle with driving.

While the administration under President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—has implemented policies aimed at regional development and revitalizing rural areas, these top-down initiatives cannot fix the daily physical demands of home maintenance. Managing a house in the countryside is closer to caring for a living, breathing entity that constantly demands repairs, from frozen pipes to structural weathering.

Is the "Rural Life" Worth the Risk?

Data suggests that while satisfaction levels among those who move to rural areas can be high (often cited around 72%), this is typically contingent on having significant financial reserves and ample time to prepare. For the average person, the "dream" is often an unexamined ideal. Without a realistic budget and a personality suited for active, hands-on community engagement, the rural experience often results in a swift return to the city.

Ultimately, rural living is not a passive retreat; it is a lifestyle that demands high-level technical skills in home maintenance and a robust ability to endure discomfort. For those considering the move, the best advice is a trial run. If you cannot embrace the work of maintaining a home and the social effort required to integrate into a new community, it is far wiser to treat the countryside as a place to visit, not a place to anchor your life.
