South Korea's 'Assassins' Movie Row Exposes Rising Right-Wing Populism

October 06, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's conservative opposition targeted director Hur Jin-ho's upcoming film Assassins, branding it an anti-state smear against former authoritarian leader Park Chung-hee, while protesters staged hostile rallies outside lead actor Hae-jin Yoo's home.
  • Who is affected: Actor Hae-jin Yoo and film crews face direct harassment and threats, prompting government warnings, while South Korea's broader cultural sector faces mounting pressure from partisan ideologues.
  • What it changes: The uproar highlights how easily culture can be weaponized for political mobilization, accelerating worries about anti-intellectualism and confirmation bias in Korean public life.

Culture War Meets Political Grandstanding

The controversy surrounding Assassins has little to do with the actual film. Directed by Hur Jin-ho, the movie does not endorse conspiracy theories about the assassination of former First Lady Yuk Young-su, and the director has explicitly dismissed such claims. Yet, senior figures from the People Power Party—South Korea's main conservative party—visited Yuk's birthplace to publicly denounce the film as an assault on national legitimacy. By manufacturing a moral panic, conservative leaders are attempting to rally their base using fictional grievances rather than engaging with reality.

Worse, the backlash has spilled over into dangerous personal intimidation. Conservative protesters gathered outside the private residence of lead actor Hae-jin Yoo, demanding boycotts and hurling personal attacks. Such aggressive tactics crossed a line from political disagreement into outright harassment. South Korea's Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism rightly condemned the threats as an attack on democracy, while police have been urged to crack down on illegal intimidation.

A Global Wave of Reality Denial

This episode is part of a broader, worrisome international trend. With right-wing populism gaining ground across major democracies, politics is increasingly driven by "reality denial"—the rejection of facts in favor of comforting confirmation biases. When political factions treat creative works as existential threats to rally their supporters, rational debate breaks down, leaving room only for conspiracy theories and mob rule.
