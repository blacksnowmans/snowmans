Seoul’s 100-Story Yongsan Megaproject Is Heading for a Ghost Town Crisis

October 10, 2026

  • The plan: The Seoul Metropolitan Government has revived the Yongsan International Business District project, aiming to build a 100-story vertical city with 4.4 times the floor space of New York's Hudson Yards, targeting move-ins by the early 2030s.
  • The mismatch: Conceived over a decade ago, the blueprint relies heavily on traditional office towers—ignoring post-pandemic hybrid work trends and massive corporate AI automation.
  • The risk: Pouring massive commercial supply into a weakening office market risks triggering severe vacancy rates, dragging down older districts like Jongno and inflicting heavy damage on South Korea's economy.
  • The alternative: Urban experts argue Seoul should follow cities like New York and Paris by pivoting away from pure office megaprojects toward mixed-use spaces that combine residential and tech-incubation hubs.

Seoul’s ambitious push to transform the massive, undeveloped Yongsan district in the heart of the capital into a global business hub is hitting a wall of modern economic reality. While city planners envision a gleaming financial center that would generate hundreds of thousands of jobs and massive economic output, the underlying assumptions belong to a bygone era. Post-pandemic remote work habits and the aggressive integration of artificial intelligence are rapidly shrinking the demand for traditional white-collar office space worldwide.

Global tech giants offer a glimpse of where the market is heading. Major firms are shedding administrative headcount to cut fixed costs as AI handles routine corporate workflows. Pushing ahead with millions of square meters of new office space under these conditions ignores clear warning signs from major global markets, where vacancy rates are already climbing. If Seoul floods the market with towers that corporate tenants no longer need, the resulting glut threatens to cannibalize existing commercial districts and destabilize project financing.

A Rare Prime Site Desperately Needing a New Urban Blueprint

Despite the commercial risks, Yongsan remains a uniquely valuable asset. Because the site was historically occupied by foreign military garrisons—from Qing and Japanese forces to the U.S. Army—Seoul’s center boasts a rare, wide-open expanse of accessible land. In dense global capitals like New York or Tokyo, finding a massive downtown canvas to incubate the next wave of spatial AI, autonomous driving, and smart-city infrastructure is nearly impossible. Yongsan offers South Korea a golden opportunity to test the future of urban tech.

Yet sticking to a rigid, decades-old zoning blueprint of skyscraper offices squanders that potential. Insisting on a pure commercial district risks creating a zero-sum game that pulls tenants away from older downtown commercial centers like Euljiro, leaving a trail of empty desks in its wake. Instead of forcing an outdated vision, Seoul should look to global precedents: New York and France have successfully shifted prime office developments into residential conversions to combat housing shortages. By adapting Yongsan to include high-density housing, cultural spaces, and flexible startup incubators, policymakers can salvage the capital’s final great land asset before it becomes an expensive monument to bad timing.
