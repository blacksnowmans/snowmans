Micron’s AI Memory Push Puts Samsung and SK Hynix on Notice

November 20, 2026

Micron Technology is rewriting the memory industry playbook with $32 billion in five-year supply contracts and a 2028 target for next-generation 3D DRAM, raising the competitive stakes for South Korean chip giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

  • Market rebound on AI optimism: After dropping 42% from its peak, Micron’s stock rose over 4% in pre-market trading on November 19 following reports of AI startup Anthropic’s IPO plans, alongside filings showing major hedge funds such as Appaloosa and Altimeter expanded their Micron holdings in the third quarter.
  • Supply crunch and 3D DRAM race: Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra warned that memory shortages will worsen in 2027 and 2028 as chipmaking grows more complex, while Micron committed to bringing vertically stacked 3D DRAM to market by 2028.
  • New contract and supply-chain rules: Micron has locked in $32 billion under five-year agreements with strict volume conditions for buyers, while scaling advanced production in Singapore to offer supply outside geopolitically sensitive Northeast Asia.
  • Consumer slump and antitrust risk: Even as AI server memory thrives, sluggish PC and smartphone sales sent NAND flash spot prices down 20% in two weeks, and Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix all face an advancing DRAM price-fixing lawsuit after a court rejected SK Hynix’s motion to dismiss.

Korea’s fading cushion

For years, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix treated Micron as a technological follower, relying on South Korea’s manufacturing scale and engineering lead to outlast market downturns. That assumption no longer holds. Micron has already supplied 8-layer HBM3E high-bandwidth memory to Nvidia ahead of schedule, and its concrete 2028 roadmap for 3D DRAM signals an ambition to set industry standards rather than chase them.

Equally challenging for Seoul is Micron’s commercial and geographic positioning. By developing Singapore into an advanced chip hub backed by local authorities, Micron gives global tech buyers a supply alternative insulated from Northeast Asian geopolitical tensions. Meanwhile, its restrictive five-year contracts aim to tame the boom-and-bust cycles that have historically bruised memory makers—forcing Samsung and SK Hynix to find ways to lock in major customers without inviting further regulatory scrutiny.

The trap beneath the AI boom

Still, the bullish narrative around AI memory hides a fragile foundation. Memory makers remain heavily exposed to general-purpose chips for PCs and smartphones, where Bank of America expects SSD prices to keep falling until 2027. If consumer demand stays weak, eroding NAND margins could undercut the record profits earned on AI servers. Combined with ongoing price-fixing litigation—which already factored into Morgan Stanley cutting Micron’s price target in half—both American and South Korean chipmakers face a narrower margin for error than the AI rally suggests.
