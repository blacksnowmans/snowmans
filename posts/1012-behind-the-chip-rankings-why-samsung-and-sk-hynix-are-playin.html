Behind the Chip Rankings: Why Samsung and SK Hynix Are Playing a Higher-Stakes Game

October 12, 2026

    Samsung Electronics, South Korea's tech giant and the world's largest memory maker, reclaimed the top spot in global DRAM market share in the second quarter at 39.4%, narrowly beating out rival SK Hynix (South Korea's leading memory chipmaker, which had briefly surged ahead on the strength of its High Bandwidth Memory, or HBM, production). SK Hynix and U.S.-based rival Micron Technology sit closely behind at 24.9% and 23.3%, respectively, as a booming market for standard, high-performance DRAM lifts all boats. While quarterly market share tables fluctuate based on short-term price swings and production timing, the real battle is shifting toward complex multi-year supply contracts and next-generation HBM4 production partnerships. China's state-backed memory startup CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies) is also rapidly gaining ground in legacy chips, capturing a 7% market share despite U.S. sanctions.

The semiconductor industry is prone to superficial panic. When SK Hynix captured the global DRAM sales lead last year on the back of its exclusive HBM chips—essential high-performance memory used alongside AI accelerators—headlines declared a permanent changing of the guard. Yet just one year later, Samsung bounced back to the top of the second-quarter rankings. Observers quick to declare a crisis at SK Hynix misunderstand the fundamental mechanics of modern chipmaking.

The underlying reality is a high-stakes tug-of-war governed by rigid supply contracts and massive manufacturing capacity. Because HBM requires extensive upfront coordination and customization with major AI buyers like Nvidia, prices are often locked in via long-term agreements. As spot prices for standard server DRAM spiked dramatically over the past year, competitors like Samsung and Micron reaped immediate windfalls from unhedged supplies. SK Hynix, by contrast, traded away short-term pricing flexibility to secure stable, long-term HBM partnerships—a strategic trade-off rather than a technical failure.

The Shift Toward Long-Term Contracts and Take-or-Pay Rules

This dynamic reflects a permanent structural shift in how memory chips are bought and sold. Driven by the explosive demand for AI data centers, big tech companies can no longer afford the traditional "boom-and-bust" cycle where component shortages halt server assembly lines. To guarantee security of supply, memory makers are increasingly locking customers into strict Long-Term Agreements (LTAs) featuring "Take-or-Pay" clauses. Under these terms, buyers must pay for contracted volumes even if their internal AI demand cools down. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron are also securing upfront deposits of 20% to 25% to insulate their massive multi-billion-dollar factory investments against sudden order cancellations.

As the industry looks ahead to the 2027 rollout of HBM4, the competitive landscape is evolving beyond simple component manufacturing into a complex system-design war. Samsung is leveraging its unique, end-to-end vertical integration—combining advanced DRAM production with its own 4-nanometer foundry capabilities to build custom base dies. Meanwhile, SK Hynix is countering through a powerful foundry alliance with Taiwan's TSMC. With Chinese upstarts like CXMT quietly swallowing up the bottom end of the legacy market, tomorrow's winners will not be decided by a single quarter's market share, but by who controls the tightest supply chain contracts and the most sophisticated foundry partnerships.
