South Korea’s Live Commerce Grows Up—and Leaves Solo Sellers Behind

November 22, 2026

South Korean comedian Park Sung-kwang’s decision to pause his six-year run on mobile shopping app Grip marks the end of an era when individual celebrities could carry a live-commerce channel on charm alone.

  • A pioneer steps back: Park, who joined Grip in 2018 as South Korea’s e-commerce market topped $70 billion (100 trillion won), announced an indefinite hiatus on November 21, stepping away to recharge from years of regular broadcasts as solo streams lose ground to studio-grade productions.
  • Platforms build their own talent: Major Korean mobile and TV retailer CJ OnStyle outlined a push to train in-house creators rather than rely on costly outside influencers, betting that entertaining content keeps users on the app longer and leads naturally to checkout.
  • Short-form clips drive the funnel: With 92% of South Korean video viewers watching short-form content, sellers are slicing hour-long streams into 15-to-30-second highlights to hook holiday shoppers, mirroring interactive quizzes and live discount voting seen in markets such as Vietnam.
  • New entrants and stricter gatekeeping: Small merchants in North Gyeongsang Province partnered with Korean cooperative lender Saemaul Geumgo and Uiduk University for live-commerce training, while TikTok permanently banned actor Kang Ji-hwan one day after he joined over a past sex-crime conviction, underscoring stricter vetting of on-screen personalities.

The read: entertainment factories replace personality retail

South Korea’s live-shopping boom was built on intimacy. Viewers tuned into early broadcasts on apps like Grip not only for bargains, but to banter in real time with familiar hosts like Park, who helped small businesses reach national audiences. That low-budget, relationship-driven model is hitting a wall.

Ahead of the year-end shopping rush, live commerce is morphing into television-style production paired with short-form algorithms. An individual seller with modest equipment struggles to compete against CJ OnStyle’s curated content ecosystems or the viral pull of unscripted celebrity moments—such as a surprise private wedding livestream involving actress Kim Ga-yeon and TV host Hong Seok-cheon that illustrated how blurred the line between entertainment and streaming has become. Even local merchants in regional provinces now need backing from banks and universities just to clear the production bar.

For platforms, this shift brings tighter control over conversion rates and brand safety, a priority highlighted by TikTok’s swift ban of Kang. Yet professionalization also risks smoothing out what made Korean live commerce compelling in the first place: once streams are engineered for 15-second highlights and studio polish, the spontaneous rapport that drew shoppers in 2018 is much harder to recreate.
