Korean Doctors Tell Expectant Mothers to Drop the Prenatal Panic

November 21, 2026

South Korean obstetricians and public health officials are urging expectant parents to abandon fear-driven pregnancy taboos, warning that obsessive stress and misinformation are causing far greater harm than many everyday exposures.

  • Needless terminations over mild medicine: Data from South Korea’s Ministry of Health and Welfare and its Korean Mother Safe Counseling Center show women repeatedly choose abortions out of panic after taking common cold or digestive pills before realizing they were pregnant, even though most pose no significant birth-defect risk.
  • The stress trap of taegyo: Traditional Korean prenatal care (taegyo), originally meant to keep mothers calm, has hardened into rigid dietary and lifestyle policing; 30-year OB-GYN specialist Shim Sang-deok warns that maternal anxiety sends cortisol across the placenta, raising risks of ADHD and emotional delays.
  • Real hazards versus myths: Doctors draw a sharp line between harmless dead-keratin routines like nail polish and systemic exposures—warning that medicated anti-inflammatory patches (NSAIDs) and scalp-contact hair dye absorb into the bloodstream, while even a single cigarette constricts umbilical blood vessels.
  • C-sections surpass 60%: South Korea’s Cesarean delivery rate has topped 60%, among the highest globally, driven by older maternal age, intense anxiety over a single pregnancy in a low-birthrate society, and declining muscle mass from poor physical conditioning.
  • Curbing hospital visits: Medical staff are openly advising extended family and in-laws to skip visits to delivery hospitals and specialized postpartum care centers (jorwon), prioritizing physical recovery over traditional hosting etiquette.

A Culture of Perfection Meets Medical Reality

South Korea’s low-birthrate trend has turned pregnancy into a high-stakes, all-or-nothing endeavor, amplifying every rumor on parenting forums. When families and social circles treat expectant mothers as fragile vessels who must follow endless unwritten rules, taegyo stops being comforting and becomes a chronic stressor. Worse, the outdated emphasis on constant bed rest leaves women without the core strength and cardiovascular endurance needed for labor, directly feeding the country’s surging Cesarean rate.

That is why Shim’s blunt pushback against traditional postpartum visits carries real weight in Korean society. Telling grandparents and in-laws to stay away from the hospital and postpartum care centers challenges deeply ingrained family expectations, particularly the pressure on new mothers to sit up and play host while exhausted. Treating childbirth like a demanding physical marathon—rather than a social reception—marks a necessary shift toward putting maternal recovery first.

The medical takeaway is a plea for proportion. Expectant mothers do not need to panic over an early pregnancy test lag or an accidental dose of cold medicine, nor should they sacrifice essential medical treatment out of misplaced guilt. Drawing hard lines against proven vascular toxins like alcohol and nicotine, while replacing rigid lifestyle taboos with light exercise and calm, is what actually protects both mother and child.
