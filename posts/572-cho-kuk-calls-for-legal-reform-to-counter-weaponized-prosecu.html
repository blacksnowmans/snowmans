Cho Kuk Calls for Legal Reform to Counter “Weaponized” Prosecutions

September 10, 2026

Cho Kuk, former Justice Minister and current head of the Rebuilding Korea Party’s (a progressive minor party) think tank, argues that South Korea’s judiciary is being weaponized through the selective interpretation of public election laws, and that the only solution lies in legislative reform rather than legal maneuvers like dropping indictments.

  • The Core Issue: Cho argues that the inclusion of the vague term "acts" in the Public Official Election Act—added in a 2000 amendment—allows prosecutors and judges to criminalize normal political statements.
  • Proposed Solution: Instead of attempting to drop indictments (which is legally impossible for cases already in the Supreme Court), Cho urges the National Assembly to pass a pending amendment to strike the term "acts" from the law.
  • Target of Criticism: Cho asserts that recent Supreme Court rulings targeting opposition leader Lee Jae-myung are politically motivated, labeling them "judicial violence" that undermines the rule of law.
  • Systemic Reform: Beyond legislative changes, Cho is calling for a formal Ministry of Justice investigation into allegations of fabricated evidence and prosecutorial misconduct in cases like the Seongnam FC controversy.

Legislative Action Over Judicial Maneuvering

The debate surrounding the "dropping of indictments" (gongso-chwiso) has dominated recent headlines, but Cho characterizes this as a distraction. He maintains that under current law, prosecutors cannot drop an indictment once a case reaches the Supreme Court. Even for cases in lower courts, forcing such an action would likely be struck down as unconstitutional. Instead, he points to a specific amendment already passed by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee and pending in the National Assembly plenary session, which would strip away the legal ambiguity that enables these controversial prosecutions.

Cho warns that if the National Assembly fails to act, the judiciary will continue to use the term "acts" to selectively punish political rivals. By removing this phrase, he believes the legal basis for current "re-trials" and convictions would collapse, effectively granting relief to those facing what he calls politically motivated legal persecution.

The Battle for Judicial Integrity

Beyond the specific legislative fix, Cho’s commentary highlights a deepening crisis of confidence in South Korea’s legal institutions. He argues that the recent, rapid-fire Supreme Court rulings against political figures represent a departure from established legal principles, signaling that the judiciary is actively intervening in the political process.

To address this, Cho suggests that a new administration must prioritize a thorough audit of the prosecution service. He advocates for the mobilization of a "Future Prosecution Committee" under the Ministry of Justice to document and formalize findings on past prosecutorial abuses. For Cho, these reforms are not about political revenge, but about restoring the judiciary as a neutral arbiter rather than a tool for political retribution.
