The Battle to Strip South Korea's Prosecutors of All Investigative Power

October 26, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party is racing to appoint a hardline Minister of Justice to execute the final stage of prosecutorial reform: the total removal of the prosecution's investigative authority.

  • The Ministry of Justice has faced a prolonged leadership vacuum, which the administration of President Lee Jae-myung views as a critical bottleneck for its top-priority reform agenda.
  • The core goal is the absolute separation of investigation and prosecution, specifically by stripping the Prosecution Service of its "supplementary investigative power."
  • The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition, is aggressively blocking candidates by labeling them "personal shields" for the President.
  • Key figures like Representative Park Kyun-taek, a former prosecutor turned reformist lawmaker, argue that leaving even a fraction of investigative power in the hands of prosecutors allows for continued political targeting.

Closing the Loophole of "Supplementary Power"

The current political battle centers on what is known as "supplementary investigative power." Previously, even after major reforms, prosecutors retained the ability to investigate further if they found gaps in cases handed over by the police. However, the ruling Democratic Party now views this as a dangerous loophole. They argue that this remaining power has been weaponized for "targeted" or "separate-issue" investigations—tactics they claim reached a peak during the era of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi, the former leader who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment.

Representative Park Kyun-taek, a top contender for the Justice Ministry post, has become the face of this shift. Despite his background as a high-ranking prosecutor, Park has publicly hardened his stance. He recently stated that after witnessing the "unrestrained" nature of the prosecution in recent years, he believes they should not be left with "even a drop of soup" (a Korean idiom for the tiniest remains) of investigative authority. This hardline stance signals that the next Minister of Justice will not seek a compromise, but rather a total institutional overhaul that limits prosecutors strictly to courtroom litigation and indictments.

Political Warfare in the National Assembly

The People Power Party (PPP) is leveraging the confirmation process to stall this momentum. During recent audits at the National Assembly (South Korea’s parliament), opposition lawmakers like Na Kyung-won focused their attacks on the candidates' pasts as defense attorneys for President Lee Jae-myung. By framing potential ministers as "loyalist guards," the opposition hopes to damage the moral authority of the reform movement. They argue that appointing the President's former lawyers to oversee the Justice Ministry is a move to protect the administration from judicial scrutiny.

However, from the administration’s perspective, these attacks are a "smoke screen" intended to preserve the status quo of the prosecutorial elite. The Democratic Party views the PPP’s resistance as a desperate attempt to maintain a lopsided judicial system that previously favored conservative interests. For the Lee Jae-myung government, the appointment of a "resolute" minister is not just about filling a seat; it is about ensuring that the Prosecution Service can never again act as an independent political power. The coming weeks will determine whether the administration can push through the noise to finalize what it calls the "normalization" of the South Korean justice system.
