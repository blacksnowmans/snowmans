Inside South Korea's Opposition: Grassroots Frustration and In-Fighting in the Democratic Party

September 30, 2026

  • What happened: Deep divisions have erupted within the main opposition Democratic Party, sparked by controversial remarks from prominent political commentator Rhyu Si-min and a growing disconnect between party leadership and grassroots supporters.
  • Who is affected: Party leader Lee Jae-myung's core base, rank-and-file members, and senior figures like Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok are caught between external media pressure and internal factional disputes.
  • What it changes: The friction exposes a growing weariness among traditional supporters who fear that petty infighting and unaddressed leadership flaws will derail key reform goals and future electoral bids.

Grassroots Disconnect and the Trap of Media Frames

South Korea's political landscape is increasingly defined by a widening chasm between die-hard party loyalists and ordinary members on the ground. While a hardcore base remains fiercely protective of the party, everyday supporters watching the daily grind of politics report a deep sense of fatigue and quiet cynicism. Recent controversial comments by commentator Rhyu Si-min—dismissed by critics as out-of-touch—triggered an explosive backlash across social media, dragging up old factional wounds and exposing how easily the progressive camp turns on its own figures when poll numbers dip.

This internal friction is being aggressively amplified by conservative media outlets, notably the Maeil Business Newspaper, which has used controversies like Rhyu's remarks and unrelated legal tangles involving former senior officials to construct a narrative of internal chaos. Instead of uniting around policy and reform, the party is repeatedly taking the bait, allowing hostile media frames to dictate the news cycle and distracting the public from pressing legislative duties.

Leadership Friction and the Cost of Factional Politics

Compounding these external pressures are questions surrounding internal party management. Critics within the party have contrasted the relatively grounded local approach of figures like lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae with the intensely factional style attributed to Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok. For a tired and anxious base, heavy-handed factional maneuvering reads as tone-deaf and self-serving, further eroding trust at a time when discipline is most needed.

Ultimately, South Korea's opposition finds itself fighting a war on two fronts: defending against external conservative attacks while managing a bitter, self-inflicted civil war of words. If the party's leadership continues to ignore the genuine distress of its grassroots supporters in favor of tactical posturing and internal posturing, the cost will be paid not just in poll numbers, but in the enduring trust of the very people who keep the movement alive.
