Stalled Prosecution Reform, Not "Overreach," is Eroding President Lee Jae-myung’s Support

October 20, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s declining approval ratings are driven by a growing sense of betrayal among his core base, who believe the administration has hesitated on its promise to dismantle the excessive powers of the state prosecution service.

  • Approval ratings are hitting a slump as data shows a significant desertion by core liberal supporters who feel the government is yielding to the "establishment."
  • The core grievance is the "retreat" on prosecution reform, specifically the failure to fully separate the prosecution's power to investigate from its power to indict.
  • Superficial "communication events," such as press conferences intended to soften the president's image, have failed to regain the trust of voters who demand legislative action over rhetoric.
  • The ruling Democratic Party, which currently holds a parliamentary majority, is being urged to use its "golden time" to finalize reform before the political momentum vanishes.

The "Speed" Myth and the Frustration of the Base

The current slide in the polls is often framed by the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, and conservative media as a reaction to "reform fatigue" or radical overreach. However, the data suggests the opposite. The sharpest drop in support is coming from the administration’s most loyal base. These voters witnessed what they describe as the "tyranny" of the prosecution during the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was impeached and is currently imprisoned—and they expected President Lee Jae-myung to deliver an irreversible overhaul of the investigative agency.

Instead of decisive action, the administration has been accused of "reading the room" and slowing down in the face of resistance from the legal establishment. This hesitation has created a vacuum of leadership that a simple press conference cannot fill. For the core supporters, the issue isn't that the government is moving too fast, but that it has stopped moving altogether. They view the prosecution’s current ability to both investigate and prosecute as a structural anomaly that must be ended by rebranding the agency into a "Prosecutor’s Office" focused strictly on litigation.

Legislative Action as the Only Exit Strategy

To reverse the downward trend, the administration must stop treating the decline as a public relations problem and start treating it as a policy failure. The "overspeeding" narrative pushed by the opposition is a political trap designed to paralyze the government. As noted by reformist figures like former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook, supporters are not suffering from fatigue; they are suffering from a lack of progress. They didn't lose interest because the reform was too hard; they lost hope because the government appeared to lose its nerve.

The Democratic Party currently possesses the legislative numbers to pass the necessary amendments to the Criminal Procedure Act. The path forward involves a "frontal breakthrough"—ignoring the "middle-ground" rhetoric that seeks to preserve the status quo and instead delivering the specific institutional changes promised during the campaign. History shows that liberal administrations in Korea often lose power when they fail to satisfy their base’s demand for justice. For the Lee Jae-myung government, the choice is clear: complete the reform of the power institutions now, or risk a permanent "lame duck" status fueled by the silence of its own voters.

"The public did not grant this power for the government to be polite to the establishment; they granted it to ensure that the era of political prosecutions never returns."
