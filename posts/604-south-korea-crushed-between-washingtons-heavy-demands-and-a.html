South Korea Crushed Between Washington's Heavy Demands and a Brutal Economic Reality

September 10, 2026

  • Washington's steep demands: The U.S. is reportedly pressuring South Korea to increase its total investment commitments well beyond $350 billion, including economically unviable projects like the Alaska North Slope LNG development abandoned by global energy giants.
  • Military deployment pressure: The U.S. has set a November deadline for South Korea to send troops to the Strait of Hormuz to fill the gap left by scaling back American forces in the Middle East.
  • The domestic struggle: At home, young South Koreans face severe economic alienation, battling skyrocketing housing costs in Seoul while working low-wage jobs—a grim reality mirrored in the new independent film '97 Haeja, Drifting.
  • What it changes: These dual pressures—external bullying from an ally and internal socio-economic collapse—are pushing South Korea's younger generation to the breaking point, exposing deep systemic vulnerabilities.

Washington's Overreach: Economic Extortion Disguised as Alliance

South Korea is finding itself cornered by an increasingly aggressive U.S. foreign and trade policy. Beyond the relentless demands to expand financial commitments past the $350 billion mark, Washington is pushing Seoul to fund white-elephant energy ventures like the Alaska gas project—a venture rejected by top international energy companies due to poor cost-efficiency. At the same time, the U.S. is pressing South Korea to deploy troops to the Strait of Hormuz by November, effectively asking Seoul's youth to shoulder the security vacuum created by American military drawdowns in the Middle East.

This dynamic feels less like an equal partnership and more like subordination. While the government in Seoul struggles to defend its national interests on a tilted playing field, the cost is being directly passed down to ordinary citizens. Demanding billions in cash while simultaneously expecting South Korean soldiers to step into Middle Eastern flashpoints crosses the line from ally to extortionist.

Drifting Youth and the Mirror of Modern Cinema

While geopolitics squeezes the nation from above, local reality is crushing young South Koreans from below. The independent film '97 Haeja, Drifting, directed by Jung Gi-hyuk and starring Kim Joo-an and Jung Eun-ji, captures this despair with brutal accuracy. The story follows a young woman navigating the brutal economic landscape of working in affluent Gangnam while being completely priced out of living there. Her struggle to collect money due to her is a metaphor for a society that has broken its promise: work hard, and you can live a normal life.

The movie strikes a nerve because it reflects the raw truth of the MZ generation (Millennials and Gen Z) caught between stagnant wages and soaring housing costs. The film also dismantles the manufactured culture war between generations, showing how young people and their parents' generation are ultimately victims of the same predatory economic soil. When the state bends over backward to satisfy foreign superpowers while ignoring the basic survival rights of its own youth, it invites a crisis of legitimacy. Seoul needs to push back against unreasonable foreign demands and start building a real safety net at home before the nation's future drifts entirely out of reach.
