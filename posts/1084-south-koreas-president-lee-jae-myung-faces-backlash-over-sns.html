South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Faces Backlash Over SNS Outbursts and Fading Approval

October 13, 2026

  • South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing sharp criticism for using the term "garbage" (오물) on social media, with critics and commentators demanding an immediate apology for insulting the public.
  • Critics accuse the presidential office of trying to buy off conservative commentators with lavish gifts, while the administration's internal purges and pursuit of political rivals alienate core supporters.
  • Meanwhile, the ruling Democratic Party faces mockery over a youth speech contest that critics dismiss as a platform for young members to parrot hardline partisan rhetoric rather than address real issues like housing and youth unemployment.

South Korea's progressive administration under President Lee Jae-myung is hitting a wall, and the self-inflicted wounds are hard to ignore. What was supposed to be an era of sweeping reform and a clean break from the past—marked by the imprisonment of disgraced former leader Yoon Suk-yeol—is instead being bogged down by familiar political pitfalls. From late-night social media rants to heavy-handed tactics against critics, the current government is burning through its political capital at an alarming rate.

The core of the frustration lies in the disconnect between executive power and public accountability. When a leader resorts to combative online outbursts against the very citizens who elected them, it signals a dangerous siege mentality. Observers and reform-minded critics have laid out a blunt roadmap for recovery: put down the smartphone, stop attacking critics, and abandon the factional infighting. Without a drastic shift in tone and substance, this administration risks becoming its own worst enemy—proving that even a government born of high public expectations can quickly lose its way once intoxicated by power.

Tax Authorities Score Big Win as Media and Politics Clash

Away from the presidential office, South Korea's National Tax Service recently notched a historic victory, recovering about 1,000 billion won ($730 million) out of a massive 4,000 billion won ($2.9 billion) tax evasion case linked to shipping tycoon Kwon Hyuk. The 15-year pursuit, spearheaded by the high-value delinquent task force, was a rare and praised display of enforcement muscle.

Yet, the way this triumph was consumed highlights a growing fatigue with South Korea's media ecosystem. Political YouTube channels and online commentators rushed to dissect the raid with hyper-detailed commentary, often parroting the exact same talking points as if they were part of the investigative team. While the cash recovery is a genuine milestone, the shallow, sensationalized media cycle underscores a deeper problem: commentary is increasingly outpacing genuine, deep-dive analysis. As the public grows weary of echo chambers, both politicians and media creators will need to offer more than just recycled talking points if they want to regain the trust of a discerning audience.
