South Korea’s Ruling Party Hits Record Low in Polls Amid Fierce Parliamentary Audits

October 11, 2026

  • South Korea's ruling Democratic Party has hit a record low approval rating of 35% under President Lee Jae-myung, trailing the conservative main opposition People Power Party by a wide 9-percentage-point margin in recent polls.
  • The drop coincides with the first week of high-stakes parliamentary audits (Gukjeong-gamsa), where lawmakers have clashed over governance failures, welfare disparities, and foreign policy missteps.
  • Diplomatic friction is mounting after the sudden recall of Ambassador Park Ki-chang from Ukraine, while domestic tensions spike following a fatal building ceiling collapse in Daegu.
  • Voters and unaligned independents are increasingly frustrated, signaling that the public is losing patience with political bickering over past scandals rather than addressing pressing economic anxieties.

Parliamentary Grilling Meets Real-World Crises

The annual parliamentary audit season is in full swing, turning the legislature into a battleground of political posturing and substantive policy critiques. While some lawmakers have leaned into theatrical stunts like robotic demonstrations, the committee rooms have also exposed alarming systemic gaps. Investigations revealed worsening disparities in low-income student dormitories, thousands of long-distance juvenile holding facility transfers, and severe commuting burdens for special-needs students. Meanwhile, outside the audit halls, political fallout is building around high-profile legal cases, including upcoming court rulings for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon and intense opposition attacks regarding past controversies tied to former officials.

Beyond domestic politics, foreign affairs and sudden tragedies have added severe pressure to the Lee Jae-myung administration. Unification Minister Chung Dong-young is facing fierce calls for his dismissal from the People Power Party, which accuses him of projecting weakness in inter-Korean relations. Simultaneously, the abrupt recall of South Korea's ambassador to Ukraine has laid bare underlying diplomatic strains, testing Seoul's delicate balancing act in international relations. Compounding these political headaches, a structural ceiling collapse at a Daegu mart forced the government to scramble emergency response teams, throwing the chronic issue of public safety regulations back into the national spotlight.

The Danger of Fighting Yesterday's Battles

In my view, these plummeting approval ratings should serve as a stark warning to the political establishment. Despite the ouster and imprisonment of disgraced former leader Yoon Seok-yeol, the current administration and its ruling party are still trapped in the exhausting cycle of blame-shifting rather than delivering tangible results. Parliament audits are meant to build a better foundation for the next generation, not serve as a stage for empty shouting matches. When young people face relentless housing crises, stubborn job shortages, and deep economic anxiety, political bickering over historical grievances feels entirely disconnected from reality. If politicians refuse to listen—truly listen—to the quiet desperation of ordinary citizens, plunging poll numbers will be the least of their worries.
