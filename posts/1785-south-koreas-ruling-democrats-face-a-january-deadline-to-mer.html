South Korea's Ruling Democrats Face a January Deadline to Merge with Cho Kuk's Party

October 31, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is racing against a tight January deadline to absorb its minor progressive ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party, but lingering friction and rank-and-file skepticism threaten to derail the coalition ahead of critical local elections.

  • The January deadline: Consolidation must be completed by next month to guarantee incoming members the six months of active party standing required to participate and run in upcoming regional races.
  • Rank-and-file hurdle: Unlike past backroom mergers, this union hinges on direct membership ballots in both parties, making grassroots consent—not executive dictate—the ultimate gatekeeper.
  • Demand for a clean slate: Pro-merger voices are calling for a "one-point" joint national convention where figures like DPK heavyweight Kim Min-seok and Rebuilding Korea Party leader Cho Kuk would compete openly for a newly unified leadership.
  • Echoes of the 2016 Honam wipeout: The party faces quiet unrest in Honam, the traditional southwestern liberal bastion, where voters famously punished complacent incumbent lawmakers in the 2016 legislative elections.

Why Grassroots Approval Outweighs Backroom Deals

President Lee Jae-myung has actively signaled support for the merger, urging senior multi-term lawmakers to close ranks and unify the progressive camp. Yet the path forward is blocked by significant bad blood. Many within the Rebuilding Korea Party harbor deep resentment over condescending rhetoric from mainstream Democrats who treated the smaller group as a subordinate satellite rather than an equal partner. Unless Democratic leadership formally apologizes for past slights, any paper agreement risks getting voted down by the party base.

This dynamic reflects a broader shift toward "party-member sovereignty." Activists argue that top-down party management belongs to a bygone era. If the party apparatus attempts to bypass emotional reconciliation and rush through procedural steps just to beat the election calendar, it risks internal sabotage and factional paralysis rather than an electoral boost.

Consolidating Power Behind the Reform Agenda

For the ruling party, institutional unity is crucial to defending the aggressive legislative drive launched since Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was removed from office and imprisoned. With Lee's administration in power, the Democrats have pushed through structural overhauls that spent decades stalled in the National Assembly: dissolving the state prosecution service to strip prosecutors of direct investigative powers, creating criminal penalties for judicial distortion, expanding the Supreme Court bench, and passing commercial code revisions alongside punitive damages for media fabrications.

However, resting on legislative achievements alone will not insulate the ruling bloc from voter fatigue. In strongholds like Honam, voters are growing impatient with career politicians perceived as coasting on party labels rather than aggressively backing reform. Merging with Cho Kuk's party is seen by strategists as a necessary shake-up—forcing incumbents to defend their seats against fresh talent and keeping the coalition aligned with an assertive grassroots electorate.
