The Crisis of South Korea’s Judicial Reform: Lee Jae-myung’s Latest Appointment Backfires

October 02, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is facing a political firestorm after forcing through the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the newly created Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA), further alienating public opinion and deepening the administration's ongoing leadership crisis.

  • What happened: The MCIA and a separate Prosecution Service were launched on October 2, meant to finalize the "separation of investigation and prosecution." However, the appointment of Kim Ji-yong has been widely criticized as an attempt to maintain partisan control over the new investigative body.
  • The political impact: The administration is struggling with record-low approval ratings and a 11-day vacancy for the Chief of Staff position, a signal that the government is effectively paralyzed.
  • Who is affected: The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the liberal party currently in power, faces internal fracture as reform-minded members and party supporters grow increasingly disillusioned with the President’s leadership style.

A Reform That Feels Like a Retreat

The establishment of the MCIA and the Prosecution Service was intended to be a landmark achievement in Korean criminal justice reform. By stripping the traditional Prosecution Service of its investigative powers, the government aimed to curb the unchecked authority that has historically defined Korean law enforcement. Instead, the appointment of Kim Ji-yong has turned a day of supposed progress into a symbol of institutional capture.

Critics argue that instead of creating an independent watchdog, the administration is filling the agency with "骨石" (literally "bone-stones"—a term implying rigid, unhelpful figures) who are loyal to the President. This pattern extends beyond the MCIA; key positions across the government are currently operating under acting heads rather than permanent, confirmed officials. This "acting system" is being viewed by political analysts as a definitive sign that the administration’s personnel management system has collapsed.

A Government in Limbo

Perhaps the most alarming symptom of the current administration’s state is its inability to fill the post of Chief of Staff—a position often considered the "heart" of a presidency. The fact that the office has remained empty for 11 days suggests that the administration has entered a state of terminal decline, or "lame duck" status, well ahead of expectations. Potential candidates are reportedly shunning the role, signaling that the political cost of being associated with this government now outweighs the prestige of the office.

President Lee’s attempts to consolidate power by isolating critics—including members of his own party—have only served to fuel the perception that he is prioritizing his personal political survival over the nation’s democratic health. While the President seeks to frame these reforms as essential, many citizens and political observers now view them as "destruction disguised as reform."

Ultimately, the current trajectory is unsustainable. Unless the administration shifts its approach to prioritize transparency and genuine consensus, the internal rift within the DPK will likely widen. The challenge for the future of Korean democracy lies not just in changing laws, but in cleaning house within political institutions to ensure that the "reform" movement serves the public, not the interests of a single leader.
