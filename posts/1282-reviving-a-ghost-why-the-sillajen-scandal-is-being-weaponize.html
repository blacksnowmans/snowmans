Reviving a Ghost: Why the SillaJen Scandal Is Being Weaponized Against Progressive Intellectual Rhyu Si-min Again

October 18, 2026

Conservative commentators and online agitators in South Korea are attempting to resurrect long-debunked conspiracy theories tying prominent progressive author and former health minister Rhyu Si-min to the collapsed biotech venture SillaJen, despite courts and prosecutors having cleared the matter years ago.

  • The origin: SillaJen, a domestic biotech firm once hyped for its oncolytic virus cancer treatment Pex-Vec, saw its shares plummet in August 2019 when an independent monitoring board recommended halting its Phase 3 clinical trial.
  • The investigation: Prosecutors during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure as prosecutor general launched sweeping raids into company executives, but the Seoul Southern District Prosecutors' Office officially confirmed it never investigated Rhyu because there was zero evidence of any lobbying or illicit ties.
  • The court verdict: South Korea's Supreme Court ultimately acquitted SillaJen's former leadership of charges alleging they dumped stock using undisclosed clinical trial data, and the stock resumed trading in October 2022 after a lengthy suspension.
  • The current flare-up: Partisan commentators and internet channels have recently revived allegations based solely on a ceremonial speech Rhyu delivered at a 2015 company event, aiming to drag high-profile progressive figures into a fresh media storm.

Anatomy of an Old Accusation

The campaign against Rhyu hinges entirely on guilt by association. In 2015, Rhyu gave a brief congratulatory speech at a SillaJen technical briefing. Meanwhile, an early financial backer of the biotech firm, Value Invest Korea (VIK), was headed by an executive who had past ties to progressive political circles and invested roughly 45 billion won (about $35 million) in SillaJen. Opponents attempted to turn a routine guest speech into proof of a high-level bribery network connecting the former Moon Jae-in administration to financial fraud.

Yet even during the height of aggressive special investigations under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, prosecutors found no money trails, illicit accounts, or political lobbying. In fact, the case famously backfired into a prosecutorial-media collusion scandal when a conservative television reporter was accused of pressuring the imprisoned VIK chief to fabricate damaging testimony against Rhyu.

The Poisoned Chalice of Selective Prosecutions

The revival of the SillaJen narrative illustrates how failed corporate R&D can be retrofitted into political ammunition. Clinical setbacks in drug development are an inherent industry risk, not an automatic state-sponsored financial crime. By framing SillaJen's trial failure as an executive conspiracy and trying to pin it on political opponents, critics transformed a biotech loss into years of manufactured controversy.

This dynamic also explains why institutional overhaul of the prosecution remains a central priority under President Lee Jae-myung's administration. When investigative bodies possess unchecked discretion to leak leads, pursue separate tangential probes, and coordinate leaks to receptive media outlets, political battles inevitably degrade into legal witch hunts. Without fresh evidence, recycling four-year-old closed files does little to rewrite the legal record, but it serves as a stark reminder of how durable partisan smear campaigns remain in South Korea's digital public square.
