Why President Lee Picked a Former TV Anchor to Run Korea’s Police and Local Budgets

November 27, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s nomination of two-term lawmaker Han Jun-ho to lead the massive Ministry of the Interior and Safety has sparked a sharp clash over whether the pick is designed to tighten political control over new investigative agencies or steer billions in local government funds ahead of future elections.

  • The reshuffle: In a cabinet shake-up on the 23rd, President Lee tapped Han—a former anchor at South Korean broadcaster MBC and a key presidential campaign aide with no policing or local governance record—alongside nominees for the National Intelligence Service and the culture, unification, and foreign ministries.
  • The ruling camp’s defense: Presidential Chief of Staff Lim Kwang-hyun and Kim Min-seok, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, argued that seasoned career bureaucrats handle technical operations while Han brings strong execution and a shared governing philosophy.
  • The opposition’s pushback: Lawmakers from the conservative People Power Party, South Korea’s main opposition, accused Lee of planting a loyalist to shield allies from probes and compromise civil service neutrality before upcoming by-elections and the 2028 parliamentary vote.
  • Investigation oversight: Because the ministry oversees both the national police and the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—created after the prosecution service was abolished—political analysts question whether Han holds enough authority to command agency chief Kim Ji-yong.

Shielding Probes or Commanding Bureaucrats?

On the surface, the uproar centers on law enforcement and insider appointments. People Power Party lawmakers Kim Jang-kyum and Park Chung-kwon have targeted Han over an ongoing police inquiry into whether his aides pressured public broadcaster KBS over a news report, though Han’s office pushed back by citing parliamentary testimony from the head of the KBS Journalists Association denying any outside pressure. Opposition figures and political commentators also view the broader reshuffle—which includes nominating former justice minister Kang Kum-sil as the first female head of the National Intelligence Service despite her lack of intelligence experience—as a narrow "revolving door" of loyalists and alumni from past liberal administrations.

Follow the Money: Local Grants and Election Leverage

Looking past policing headlines, political analysts argue the real prize is the interior ministry’s vast power over local government budgets. With the National Assembly and executive ministries already locked in a tug-of-war over a "Future Response Fund" that commentators warn could pull core ministerial budgets under legislative control, placing a trusted, politically savvy ally in charge of local fiscal transfers gives President Lee crucial leverage with lawmakers desperate for district funding before the next general election.

That financial clout will immediately be tested in Gyeonggi Province, South Korea’s most populous region, where Han previously ran in the Democratic Party’s gubernatorial primary against current Governor Choo Mi-ae. Choo’s administration is pressing central ministries for local tax reforms projected to raise about $10 billion (14.1 trillion won) over four years, and Han—whose main fiscal track record consists of securing smaller district grants, such as about $2.1 million (2.93 billion won) for projects in Goyang—must now prove he can manage macro-level fiscal politics rather than just routine constituency favors.
