South Korea’s Media Upheaval, Foreign Policy Stumbles, and Scandals at the Top

September 23, 2026

South Korea is navigating a turbulent political landscape defined by shifting media trust, growing friction in foreign policy, heavy judicial reckonings over a failed martial law attempt, and an embarrassing expense scandal at the highest level of the judiciary.

  • Alternative Media Ascendancy: A recent 2026 media trust survey by SisaIN and Gallup Korea ranked the progressive YouTube-based political show Kim Ou-joon's Humble Opinion Is Hard as South Korea’s most trusted broadcasting program (6.3%), outranking traditional terrestrial giants like MBC and KBS.
  • Foreign Policy Passivity: Analysts warn that Seoul’s diplomatic approach to upcoming U.S.-China summits remains overly defensive and reactive, leaving South Korea sidelined while major powers negotiate complex trade, technology, and security packages.
  • Severe Sentences Demanded for Martial Law Commanders: Prosecutors have sought up to 30 years in prison for former military commanders involved in the botched martial law attempt, highlighting stark contrasts in accountability and remorse among the accused.
  • Chief Justice Expense Scandal: Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de faces intense public backlash after reports revealed that the judiciary inflated attendance numbers on expense reports to skirt spending limits while dining at high-end omakase restaurants and luxury hotels.

The rise of alternative digital platforms like Kim Ou-joon’s program signals a permanent fracture in how South Koreans consume news. Traditional broadcasters, once the undisputed gatekeepers of public discourse, are losing ground to commentators who offer direct, unfiltered analysis rather than the performative, mechanical neutrality of legacy media. This shift reflects a broader public impatience with institutions that appear more concerned with protecting their own authority than confronting political reality.

Meanwhile, the country's governance is being tested on multiple fronts. The ongoing trials of military leaders who joined the short-lived martial law attempt underscore the fragility of South Korean democracy—and the vital role ordinary citizens played in defending it. At the same time, the expense scandal enveloping the Supreme Court demonstrates that a lack of accountability is not confined to the executive branch, threatening public trust in the judiciary precisely when that trust is needed most.
