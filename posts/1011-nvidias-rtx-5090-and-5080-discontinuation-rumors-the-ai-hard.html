Nvidia's RTX 5090 and 5080 Discontinuation Rumors: The AI Hardware Rush

October 12, 2026

Wild rumors sweeping South Korea's PC hardware community suggest that Nvidia's flagship graphics cards—the RTX 5090 and 5080—may face discontinuation as the company pivots to feed skyrocketing corporate demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure.

  • The Rumor: Unconfirmed reports from social media claim Nvidia is shelving the RTX 5090 and the 16GB VRAM version of the RTX 5080, potentially replacing the latter with a beefed-up 24GB "Super" variant.
  • The Driver: Chinese tech giants and AI startups are aggressively hoarding consumer-grade RTX 5090 cards—paying upwards of $6,500 (about 9 million won) per unit—to run local large language and video-generation models like ByteDance's "SeeDance."
  • The Strategy: By shifting chip supplies toward lucrative enterprise-focused variants like the new RTX PRO 5500, Nvidia and regional distributors are effectively solving supply shortages at the top while cross-subsidizing losses on budget consumer cards.

Why Corporate AI Buyers Are Cleaning Out Retail Shelves

At the heart of the hardware shortage is video memory (VRAM). The RTX 5090 packs a massive 32GB of VRAM, making it a cost-effective alternative to expensive enterprise servers for startups running local AI models. In China, strict U.S. export controls bar the sale of the original 5090, leaving buyers to aggressively snap up available inventory—including imported units from South Korea—at extreme markups.

This insatiable enterprise demand creates a mathematical dilemma for hardware buyers. Three RTX 5090 cards combined deliver 96GB of VRAM and triple the core computing power of a single workstation-grade RTX PRO 6000, for a fraction of the price. Retailers in South Korea are quietly riding this wave, using the massive profit margins squeezed from high-end 5090 sales to offset the thin margins on slower-moving, budget-friendly consumer GPUs.

The Master Plan Behind the Production Shift

Nvidia's alleged move to scale back consumer flagship production aligns with the introduction of enterprise-focused alternatives like the RTX PRO 5500, built on the 5090 chipset. When a chip can be sold in bulk to corporate buyers at scale, factory allocations naturally follow the money. It is a classic corporate pivot disguised as supply chain volatility.

For individual consumers, the rumored arrival of a 24GB RTX 5080 Super is designed to plug the gap left by the vanishing 5090. However, this upgrade comes with a catch. To successfully market an aggressively priced new model without inviting consumer backlash over older 16GB inventory, older cards must quietly disappear from shelves. It is a masterclass in hardware lifecycle management from Nvidia leadership—keeping enterprise coffers full while squeezing every last dollar out of the consumer market.
