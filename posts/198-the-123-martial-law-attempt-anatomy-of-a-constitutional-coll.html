The 12/3 Martial Law Attempt: Anatomy of a Constitutional Collapse

September 05, 2026

Recent courtroom testimonies regarding the events of December 3, 2024, have laid bare the chaotic and legally baseless nature of the emergency martial law declaration that nearly crippled South Korean democracy.

  • The Incident: Former President Yoon Suk Yeol declared emergency martial law, citing "internal enemies" and political gridlock, but the move lacked the necessary constitutional justification.
  • The Process: Court testimonies confirm that the martial law decree was drafted and implemented without proper Cabinet review or legal consultation, bypassing established democratic procedures.
  • The Fallout: The Constitutional Court ultimately removed Yoon from office in April 2025, branding the act an unconstitutional attempt to dismantle the nation's democratic order.
  • Key Figures: The hearings have cast a harsh spotlight on the alleged involvement of former First Lady Kim Keon Hee and the convenient memory lapses of key officials like former Minister of Justice Park Sung-jae.

A Recipe for Constitutional Crisis

The martial law declaration was framed by its architects as a desperate measure to save a "failing nation" from political obstructionism. However, the legal reality is stark: under Article 77 of the South Korean Constitution, martial law is a tool reserved for genuine national emergencies, not a button for a president to press when they grow weary of legislative opposition. The court records reveal that the "Proclamation No. 1" was essentially a vanity project, handled with such amateurish negligence that it bordered on farce—a move that effectively privatized state power.

The Shadows of Power

Perhaps the most damning aspect of the testimonies is the recurring theme of "unofficial" channels of power. Witnesses described a government where crucial decisions—such as the declaration of martial law—were made via informal communication, bypassing the Cabinet entirely. The persistent rumors of former First Lady Kim Keon Hee’s involvement, coupled with vague denials and "I don't remember" responses from high-ranking officials, suggest that the official state machinery was being ignored in favor of a shadow cabinet. This culture of non-accountability reveals that the true threat to South Korea’s stability wasn't the "internal enemy" the president claimed to be fighting, but a breakdown in the ethical and legal foundations of the presidency itself.

Ultimately, the December 3 incident was not just a political blunder; it was a traumatic reminder of how fragile democratic institutions can be when held by leaders who view the constitution as an obstacle rather than a mandate. The "convenient forgetting" currently on display in courtrooms is a final, cynical attempt to evade responsibility for a betrayal of the public trust.
