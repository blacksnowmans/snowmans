The Harsh Reality of Spain's Camino de Santiago: What Korean Trekkers Are Learning the Hard Way

September 07, 2026

  • The completion rate is under 50%: Despite its romantic portrayal on social media, fewer than half of all travelers finish the iconic 800-kilometer (approx. 500-mile) pilgrimage across Spain.
  • Preparation takes a full year: Experts advise starting physical conditioning, gear testing, and route simulation 6 to 12 months in advance to push success rates above 90%.
  • Safety is a growing concern: Recent medical emergencies and traffic accidents involving Korean travelers have prompted strict warnings from South Korea's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and embassy in Spain.

The Camino de Santiago is experiencing a massive wave of renewed popularity among South Koreans, driven by social media feeds filled with picturesque landscapes. Yet behind the idyllic snapshots lies a grueling physical ordeal that catches many unprepared travelers off guard. Korean trekking experts warn that the route is far from a casual walking tour; it demands serious physical conditioning, careful season selection, and a respect for local safety hazards that claim lives and derail journeys every year.

For international readers eyeing the trail, the core takeaway from recent Korean advisories is to drop the romanticized notion of an impromptu backpacking trip. With completion rates hovering below 50 percent, success relies entirely on pacing, proper gear—such as double-socking to prevent debilitating blisters and utilizing trekking poles to save knee joints—and knowing when to drop heavy luggage using local baggage delivery services (costing about €5 to €7 per section). Overtourism has also strained local infrastructure, making off-season planning or spring and autumn windows essential to avoiding punishing heat waves and packed accommodations.

Prioritizing Survival Over Speed

Perhaps the most sobering shift in the conversation surrounding the pilgrimage is the emphasis on safety over mere completion. Recent tragic incidents—including Korean nationals suffering fatal heart attacks and strokes on the trail, as well as pedestrian accidents along vehicle roads—have turned the spotlight onto personal health limits. South Korea's diplomatic missions in Spain now actively urge travelers to avoid starting out in the dark without proper reflectors and headlamps, and to heed early warning signs like chest tightness or slurred speech immediately.

Ultimately, the consensus among seasoned Korean trekkers is that no certificate of completion is worth your life. Pacing yourself according to your own body's signals rather than matching younger travelers, utilizing Spain's universal 112 emergency line, and accepting a bus ride or a rest day when exhaustion sets in are the true hallmarks of a successful journey. The 800-kilometer path is meant to be a test of self-reflection, not a race against burnout.
