Musk Explores Tesla-SpaceX Merger as Big Tech AI Splurge Collides with Global Fragility

October 22, 2026

The prospect of a merger between Tesla and SpaceX has advanced from speculative market chatter to formal evaluation by an exploratory committee, injecting fresh momentum into tech valuations even as physical infrastructure constraints and geopolitical risks mount.

  • Corporate consolidation on the table: Elon Musk has established a special committee to review merging Tesla with SpaceX, capitalizing on strong SpaceX data center earnings and positioning Tesla’s software ecosystem—notably its Full Self-Driving (FSD) fleet—as a broader technology platform.
  • Big Tech’s high-stakes CapEx race: Meta is signaling annual capital expenditures reaching up to $145 billion by 2026, betting heavily on AI data centers despite quarterly losses exceeding $4.1 billion in its Reality Labs hardware unit.
  • Physical and geopolitical friction: Surging power and water consumption are triggering regulatory pushback on new data centers globally, while Middle East tensions and French 10-year bond yields piercing 5% signal underlying macro stress.
  • Seoul's balancing act: The Bank of Korea remains constrained between maintaining hawkish rhetoric to anchor inflation expectations and holding policy rates steady to support domestic recovery under President Lee Jae-myung.

The Shift from Hardware Manufacturing to Platform Moats

Tesla’s third-quarter earnings capture a company caught in transition. While deliveries reached a four-year quarterly high of 486,532 vehicles, automotive revenue slipped 3% and operating margins remained compressed at 2.6%. The valuation story is instead shifting toward high-margin recurring income: services revenue climbed 33% to $4.6 billion, bolstered by FSD subscriptions surging 67% quarter-over-quarter to 1.74 million. By exploring a formal tie-up with SpaceX, Musk is attempting to cement Tesla not as an automaker subject to cyclical manufacturing margins, but as an indispensable physical-AI and aerospace platform.

This strategy mirrors Meta’s willingness to burn cash for structural independence. Meta’s advertising engine—generating $63 billion in quarterly revenue across 3.64 billion daily active users—comfortably funds staggering infrastructure bets. Building dedicated AI computing clusters is treated less as an operating expense and more as acquiring productive capital assets, insulating the company from third-party operating systems.

Macro Decoupling Meets Physical Grid Limits

A striking dynamic in current markets is the divergence between U.S. tech-fueled equity resilience and sovereign bond turbulence in Europe, where French borrowing costs have spiked without dragging U.S. Treasuries along. Global capital continues to treat American AI infrastructure as the premier destination for yield. Yet this concentration faces hard physical constraints: worldwide data center power demand is projected to reach 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, sparking grid moratoriums in key Western hubs and highlighting efficiency gaps among Korean tech firms whose Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) metrics trail global peers.

For South Korea, maintaining macroeconomic stability amidst external shocks remains central. Following the turbulence and economic strain of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, the Lee Jae-myung administration has prioritized pragmatism and grid-scale energy expansion. With currency pressures keeping the won volatile, policymakers in Seoul know that sustaining domestic tech competitiveness requires both diplomatic de-risking and securing the dense power grids that modern AI infrastructure demands.
