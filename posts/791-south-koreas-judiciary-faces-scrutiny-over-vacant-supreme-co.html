South Korea's Judiciary Faces Scrutiny Over Vacant Supreme Court Seats and Cash Bonuses

October 07, 2026

Recent parliamentary audits have exposed South Korea's Supreme Court to intense criticism over a months-long vacancy crisis and untracked cash bonuses distributed to top judges, contrasting sharply with the judiciary's harsh treatment of petty offenses by ordinary citizens.

    What happened: Lawmakers grilled court officials during a parliamentary audit over unbudgeted cash bonuses given to Supreme Court justices (about $1,500 monthly) and the National Court Administration chief (about $2,200 monthly), alongside a prolonged deadlock over filling vacant Supreme Court seats. Who is affected: Ordinary litigants face paralyzed full bench rulings due to the court vacancies, while the judiciary's internal financial practices have drawn public outrage. What it changes: The revelations pierce the armor of judicial independence, fueling calls for democratic oversight and systemic reform of court administration budgets.

A Tale of Two Standards: Petty Offenses vs. Judicial Perks

The audit highlighted a glaring double standard in how South Korea's justice system operates. Lawmakers pointed to past rulings where ordinary citizens faced severe consequences for minor slips—such as a bus driver fired over a disputed 800-won (about $0.60) snack purchase, or security guards prosecuted over 1,060 won (about $0.80) worth of snacks (though later acquitted on appeal). Meanwhile, an audit by the Board of Audit and Inspection revealed that top judges quietly pocketed millions of won in regular cash bonuses without proper documentation.

Compounding the financial scandal is a prolonged constitutional standoff. Supreme Court vacancies have lingered for over half a year due to deadlocks between Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the executive branch over nominee appointments. Critics argue that leaving the top court half-empty paralyzes high-level rulings and harms everyday citizens under the guise of protecting judicial independence, while treating public funds like petty cash.

Time to Tear Down the Ivory Tower

The judiciary has long wrapped itself in the noble mantle of independence to shield its internal workings from public view. But revelations of untracked cash payouts, lavish wine purchases on official cards without receipts, and stockpiled excess IT equipment make a mockery of those claims. When the nation's highest legal minds demand strict adherence to the law from the public while treating themselves to unvouched perks, the social contract breaks down.

Judicial power ultimately derives from the public and must serve to protect citizens' basic rights, not act as a playground for a privileged elite. Resolving the current vacancy crisis and cleaning up opaque budgets are no longer optional—they are the minimum requirements to restore public faith in South Korea's rule of law.
