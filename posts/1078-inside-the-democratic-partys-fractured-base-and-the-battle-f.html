Inside the Democratic Party's Fractured Base and the Battle for Reform Ahead of South Korea's Next General Election

October 13, 2026

A heated debate over a presidential social media post and fierce internal finger-pointing have laid bare deep factional rifts within South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party, exposing a dangerous disconnect between party leadership and its core supporters.

    A presidential warning sets off alarms: A recent social media post by President Lee Jae-myung criticizing self-serving elites ("living in luxury") sparked widespread speculation about who was being targeted, drawing a sharp denial from senior party figure Choo Mi-ae. Internal backstabbing laid bare: Lawmakers and political commentators have openly criticized the prevalence of *haedanghaengwi* (harmful party activities) within the Democratic Party, exposing a culture where self-preservation trumps reform. The trap of the moderate pivot: While the party leadership under figures leaning toward a broad-tent, moderate strategy has attempted to appeal to wider electorates, it has alienated the party's fiercely loyal, reform-minded base, dragging down poll numbers. An inevitable purge on the horizon: Political analysts argue that upcoming general elections will demand a sweeping generational shift and wholesale elimination of entrenched incumbents to prevent total voter backlash.

The Politics of Interpretation and the Media Backlash

When a South Korean president drops cryptic language online, the shockwaves ripple instantly through the National Assembly. President Lee's jab at "luxurious high-ranking officials" put the ruling Democratic Party on edge, prompting lawmaker Joo Yeong to press senior figure Choo Mi-ae on whether the description applied to her. Choo flatly denied the insinuation, recalling her humble roots as the daughter of a dry cleaner to deflect accusations of elitism. Yet the episode underscored a draining reality of South Korean politics: every word is parsed for its implications on future power dynamics and nomination rights.

Simultaneously, the party's relationship with the press continues to fuel a strange political dynamic. Politicians who proudly adopt an adversarial stance against media outlets—much like Choo and former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook—enjoy an ardent following. For voters exhausted by cautious, poll-watching establishment politicians, this unfiltered defiance offers a potent catharsis. It creates a feedback loop where open confrontation with critics cements a politician's status among the base, even as it deepens partisan polarization.

Factional Warfare and the Push for a Sweeping Primary Purge

The greater threat to the Democratic Party, however, is not the opposition, but the enemy within. Commentators have pointed to rampant internal betrayal—colleagues undermining each other's legislative initiatives and stalling reforms to protect their own political survival. During the turbulent years of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol, obstructionists within the party frequently slowed down resistance efforts, and that institutional habit persists.

As the next general election approaches, analysts argue that a massive cull of incumbent lawmakers is no longer optional. The traditional power broker cartels and opportunistic politicians who drift wherever power flows have rendered the current establishment vulnerable. If the party's leadership fails to wield a heavy knife against those who compromise democratic values and stall reform, they risk facing swift and unforgiving punishment from disillusioned voters.

Ultimately, the Democratic Party stands at a crossroads. The friction between chasing moderate swing voters and satisfying the core, reform-driven base has left the party's approval ratings sagging. To survive, the party must abandon cautious compromises and purge the dead weight within its ranks, returning to the grassroots principles that keep it alive.
