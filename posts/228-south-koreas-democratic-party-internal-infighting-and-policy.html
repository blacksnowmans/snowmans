South Korea’s Democratic Party: Internal Infighting and Policy Retreat

September 07, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s main liberal opposition, is facing a crisis of identity as internal factionalism and regressive housing policies alienate its traditional base.

  • Factional Infighting: Leaked messages and reports of "line-drawing"—where loyalty to specific power brokers outweighs party values—have exposed a dysfunctional, vertical power structure.
  • Policy Reversal: The DPK has recently signaled a retreat on the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax, essentially offering tax breaks to owners of high-value homes, a move seen by many as a betrayal of their working-class supporters.
  • Youth Disillusionment: With Seoul apartment prices often exceeding $750,000 (1 billion won) and interest rates squeezing household budgets, the party's failure to address housing instability leaves younger voters feeling abandoned.

The Collapse of Democratic Standards

The DPK was once the standard-bearer for judicial reform and social justice, but these ideals are losing credibility due to the party's opaque internal decision-making. Observers note that the party has shifted from a coalition of shared values to a survival-of-the-fittest environment where "who you know" matters more than policy conviction. This shift has turned the party into a venue for power struggles rather than a vehicle for public service, causing supporters to turn away in frustration.

By framing internal power plays as "political pluralism," the DPK leadership is attempting to mask what is essentially cronyism. True democratic reform within the party would require dismantling these rigid, exclusionary structures and returning to the transparent dialogue that the party’s historical figures, such as former presidents Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun, once championed.

Housing Policy and the Loss of Identity

The party’s recent flirtation with relaxing real estate taxes on expensive, non-primary residences is particularly damaging. While the leadership frames this as necessary economic maneuvering, it strikes a deep chord of hypocrisy among citizens struggling with high-interest debt and unaffordable rent. The data is clear: the number of people subject to these wealth-based real estate taxes has surged fivefold in recent years, highlighting a growing wealth gap that the DPK was elected to help bridge.

For the youth, the transition from Korea’s unique jeonse (a massive lump-sum deposit lease) system to monthly rent is being treated by the party as a mere logistical shift, while the reality is a looming affordability crisis. By failing to offer a coherent alternative to speculative, debt-fueled housing markets, the DPK risks being viewed as a party that protects the assets of the wealthy while offering nothing but empty rhetoric to the next generation. If the party continues to prioritize the optics of power over the concrete needs of its constituents, it risks losing its relevance—and its voters—for good.
