South Korea’s Rental Costs Set to Surge Double the Speed of Home Prices Next Year

November 13, 2026

South Korea’s nationwide jeonse (lump-sum rental deposit) prices are projected to surge 4.5% next year—more than double the forecasted 2.0% rise in home purchase prices—as tight mortgage regulations force prospective buyers to remain tenants.

  • What happened: The Construction Economy Research Institute of Korea (CERIK), a leading industry think tank, announced at its outlook seminar that national home sales growth will slow to 2.0%, while jeonse rental costs will jump 4.5%.
  • The capital divide: Home prices in the Greater Seoul area are expected to outpace the national average with a 3.0% gain, deepening real estate polarization against stagnant provincial markets.
  • Root causes: Stricter mortgage lending caps and tax uncertainties are dampening buyer sentiment, while soaring construction material costs have severely reduced new apartment completions.
  • Who is affected: Middle-class tenants face severe cash squeezes upon lease renewals, while long-term construction orders are projected to grow by just 4.1% in 2027, signaling persistent housing shortages.

Lending Restrictions Push Buyers into a Squeezed Rental Market

The stark divide between sluggish home sales and skyrocketing rental prices highlights a growing structural bottleneck in South Korea’s property market. As financial regulators tighten household loan limits to curb debt, many buyers who intended to purchase homes have been priced out of taking on mortgages. Unable to buy, these households are choosing to remain in the jeonse system—Korea’s unique lease structure where tenants deposit a large lump sum returned at the end of the contract instead of paying monthly rent. This shift has flooded the rental market with demand at a time when fresh housing supply is drying up.

For tenants, the financial burden will be substantial. Under a 4.5% average increase, a household residing in an apartment with a 500 million won deposit (about $360,000) will need to secure over 20 million won (around $14,500) in additional cash simply to renew their lease. Compounding the issue, high prices for key construction materials like cement and rebar continue to delay redevelopment projects, leading to a drought of new apartment handovers needed to relieve rental demand.

Regional Polarization and Multi-Year Supply Bottlenecks

CERIK’s forecast also underscores a widening regional divide across the country. While nationwide property growth stalls at 2.0%, Greater Seoul is projected to see a 3.0% rise due to persistent demand for premium metropolitan infrastructure. In contrast, provincial housing markets remain entirely cold, fueling growing asset inequality between capital-region homeowners and provincial residents.

Looking further ahead, CERIK projects construction orders to grow by a modest 4.1% in 2027. Because order growth remains sluggish, new housing starts over the next three to four years will remain constrained. Unless tax policies and credit caps are adjusted to unblock market transactions, South Korean renters waiting for home prices to drop will instead face an increasingly expensive rental market.
