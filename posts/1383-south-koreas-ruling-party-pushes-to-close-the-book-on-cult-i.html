South Korea’s Ruling Party Pushes to Close the Book on Cult Infiltration Rumors

October 22, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing mounting internal pressure to release the results of an internal probe and decisively shut down allegations of infiltration by the controversial Shincheonji religious sect.

  • The internal demand: Senior figures including Supreme Council member Lee Sung-yoon are calling on the party leadership to immediately disclose its investigation findings to halt internal division and defuse rumors of a factional conspiracy.
  • The official verdict: An eight-month joint police-prosecution task force of 47 investigators concluded that while organized, collective enrollment occurred within the conservative opposition People Power Party, no systematic infiltration took place inside the Democratic Party.
  • Local lobbying, not a coup: While some individual Shincheonji adherents registered as Democratic Party members in cities like Goyang and Gwacheon to lobby for church zoning permits—leading to indictments in one mayoral primary—investigators confirmed no top-down directive existed from sect leader Lee Man-hee.
  • Opposition counterattack: The People Power Party has cried foul during legislative audits, accusing law enforcement of bias for not subjecting the ruling party to the same aggressive raids conservatives faced.

Behind the Factional Distraction

For more than three months, whispered allegations of a secret alliance between Shincheonji and anti-leadership factions have sowed distrust within the ruling party. Rather than focusing on economic policy under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, party officials have found themselves bogged down in defensive skirmishes. Leaving the internal probe's findings in the dark has only allowed speculation to metastasize, gifting the opposition an easy weapon to dilute scrutiny over its own documented sect ties.

The distinction between isolated misconduct and institutional collusion is vital. In suburban hubs across Gyeonggi Province, sect members attempted to gain political leverage to resolve long-standing real estate and permit disputes after facing neighborhood backlash against their assembly halls. Treating these localized municipal grievances as a centralized takeover of the ruling party distorts reality, but lingering secrecy from Democratic Party headquarters has prolonged the narrative.

The Privacy Dilemma and the Path Ahead

Democratic Party leaders now face a delicate legal balancing act. South Korean privacy and political party statutes strictly protect members' personal information and sensitive religious beliefs. Releasing individual member rosters would violate the law, meaning leadership must present anonymized, statistical proof to clear the air without triggering legal liability.

With prosecutors having already executed dozens of search warrants and confirmed the lack of organized Democratic Party involvement, the controversy has outlived its substance. The ruling party's challenge now is to draw a clean line under the episode, fulfill the demands of its rank-and-file for transparency, and pivot back to pressing state affairs.
