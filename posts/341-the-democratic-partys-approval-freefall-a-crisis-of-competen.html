The Democratic Party’s Approval Freefall: A Crisis of Competence

September 07, 2026

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the main liberal opposition, is facing a deepening crisis as both the party’s approval ratings and the performance assessment of President Lee Jae-myung continue a synchronized decline, signaling a total collapse of public trust.

  • What happened: Realmeter’s latest data shows President Lee’s approval rating has plummeted to 37.4%. Even more concerning, the DPK is losing support in lockstep with the President, ending any "honeymoon period" or political decoupling.
  • What it changes: The gap between the DPK and the ruling People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative party—has narrowed to within 10%, putting the opposition’s dominance at risk.
  • Who is affected: The DPK’s leadership is under fire for ignoring economic hardships, while the party is struggling to gain traction with younger voters who view their outreach efforts as performative and out-of-touch.

A Party Out of Touch with Reality

The DPK’s current tailspin isn't just about economic metrics; it is the result of self-inflicted wounds and a lack of political intuition. While the PPP is gaining ground, it is not because they are performing exceptionally well, but because the DPK has repeatedly failed to address the core concerns of the electorate. Instead of focusing on policy refinements to fix the cost-of-living crisis, the party remains mired in internal factional infighting. This "brakeless" decline reflects a fundamental disconnect between the political elite and the reality of voters struggling with inflation and housing instability.

The frustration is particularly acute among younger voters (the 20s-30s demographic). When the DPK attempts to court this group, it often resorts to superficial optics—such as forced seating arrangements for photo-ops—which voters perceive as patronizing. The consensus among these demographics is that the party cares more about political theater than meaningful systemic change. As one critic noted, a politician’s most vital instinct is the ability to read the room (nunchi), yet the DPK seems to have lost this survival mechanism entirely.

Misplaced Priorities and "Creative" Wordplay

Nothing highlights the DPK’s detachment from reality more than lawmaker Kim Min-seok’s recent push for constitutional reform. While proposals like adding historical protest movements (the May 18th Democratization Movement and the Bu-Ma Democratic Protests) to the constitution carry symbolic weight, proposing them while the public is battling an economic downturn is a massive strategic blunder. The electorate sees this as a "luxury" debate, leading to widespread speculation that the party is merely attempting to shift the power structure to protect its own interests.

This is compounded by the party’s tendency to hide its incompetence behind buzzwords. Kim’s call for a "creative horizontal relationship" between the party and the government is widely viewed as empty jargon. It is a classic political tactic: adding an adjective to a concept to mask the fact that no actual change is occurring. True reform requires the party to hold the administration accountable, not merely to change the branding of their cooperation. If the DPK cannot pivot from internal squabbles and performative politics to delivering tangible solutions for everyday citizens, the upcoming Seoul mayoral by-election could be the final nail in the coffin for their electoral prospects.
