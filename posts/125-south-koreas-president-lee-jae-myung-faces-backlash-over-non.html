South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Faces Backlash Over Non-Stop Social Media Attacks

September 29, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s relentless use of X (formerly Twitter) to lash out at critics and rally supporters has transformed his official account into an online weapon, raising serious concerns about his leadership, the erosion of governance standards, and his handling of critical foreign policy.

  • What happened: President Lee has posted hundreds of messages on X, frequently bypassing his own administration's briefers and cabinet members to direct supporters, label critics as malicious actors, and fight daily public relations battles.
  • What it changes: State agencies have begun scrambling to echo the President’s personal social media pronouncements, threatening the separation of powers and creating a system where a presidential tweet overrides institutional checks.
  • Who is affected: The South Korean public, civil servants, and the nation's foreign policy apparatus, all of whom face the fallout of an erratic leader distracted by online comment sections during high-stakes international tours.

A Presidency Run by Timeline Refreshes

For an outside observer, watching a head of state spend an overseas diplomatic trip refreshing an app to argue with internet commentators sounds like satire. But for South Korea, it is an increasingly alarming reality under President Lee Jae-myung. Rather than functioning as an open forum for policy debate, Lee's social media feed operates as a bullhorn demanding loyalty and directing his base to push back against unfavorable news. When a leader dismisses reasonable policy dissent as organized subversion, the space for actual governance vanishes.

This personal grievance-driven style is bleeding directly into the state machinery. When a major business lobby report regarding wealthy citizens leaving the country drew controversy, cabinet ministers rushed onto social media to parrot the President's branding of the report as "fake news," sidelining bureaucratic autonomy. Furthermore, critics worry that Lee's psychological inability to withstand criticism—manifested in his near-constant online sparring—could lead to catastrophic miscalculations on major looming issues, such as foreign troop deployment pressures from Washington.

The Erosion of Democratic Norms

What makes this political strategy so damaging is its populist veneer. By framing everyday political participation as an urgent crusade against shadowy opponents, the administration avoids genuine self-reflection on economic struggles, high taxes, and public anxiety. Statecraft requires institutional deliberation, not immediate reactive venting.

When the leader of a democratic nation treats public critique as an unpardonable personal offense and recruits online followers to drown it out, the casualty is not just the administration's credibility, but the health of South Korea's democracy. Lee was elected to manage state affairs and protect the national interest—not to command a digital army from a smartphone screen.
