South Korea's Presidential Chief of Staff Resigns Amid Internal Power Struggle

September 24, 2026

  • Kang Hoon-sik, the presidential chief of staff, has offered to resign, triggering intense political fallout and exposing deep factional rifts within the administration of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung.
  • The resignation highlights a reported power struggle between Kang's camp and the influential "Seongnam line"—staffers aligned with key presidential aide Kim Hyun-ji—over who bears responsibility for recent cabinet nominee failures and plunging approval ratings.
  • The main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, is capitalizing on the turmoil by demanding parliamentary audit testimonies from Kim and former Democratic Party official Jeong Jin-sang, intensifying accusations of shadow power politics.
  • The crisis threatens the administration's core political backing, turning traditional progressive supporters into critics as the government struggles with policy direction and internal finger-pointing.

The sudden departure of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik is officially being framed as standard accountability for a string of political missteps and sliding public support. But in Seoul's political circles, the exit is widely read as the boiling over of a long-simmering civil war inside the Blue House. The core grievance circulating among insiders is simple: why does Kang take the fall when personnel recommendations and vetting are heavily influenced by the secretive Seongnam faction? By exposing these internal fault lines, the resignation has dragged the eternal question of who truly wields power in the administration uncomfortably into the open.

This is more than a standard personnel shuffle; it points to a deeper institutional rot. When a government enjoys strong political health, minor policy errors are absorbed by a loyal core base. Today, however, the Lee administration's political reserves are nearly exhausted. Rather than addressing the root causes of policy disconnects—such as controversies over prosecutorial reform and a widening gap with progressive ideals—the administration's inner circle appears trapped in a politics of subtraction, hunting for internal scapegoats rather than listening to critics. Blaming individual aides without fixing a broken system means the cycle of crisis will only repeat, leaving the administration increasingly isolated from the very public that brought it to power.
