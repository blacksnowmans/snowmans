President Lee Jae-myung Faces Progressive Backlash Over Appointments and Top-Down Rule

October 15, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is pushing ahead with polarizing personnel picks and a micromanaged governing style, triggering unprecedented pushback from within his own progressive camp and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

  • Controversial agency chief: Lee is moving to formally appoint Kim Ji-yong to lead the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, despite sharp public opposition and confirmation hearing revelations that Kim dropped charges in a past legal corruption case.
  • Loyalty over candor: The appointment of former National Tax Service chief Lim Gwang-hyun as presidential chief of staff has deepened criticism that the administration favors unquestioning yes-men over independent counselors.
  • Cabinet pushback: Around ten cabinet ministers reportedly petitioned Prime Minister Han Seong-sook to scale back live broadcasts of cabinet meetings, complaining that camera-ready micromanagement silences real policy debate.
  • Progressive divide: Rifts are widening among liberal lawmakers, civic groups, and influential commentators over whether the administration is repeating the authoritarian missteps of past governments.

Controversial Appointments Spark Accusations of Yes-Men Politics

The appointment of Kim Ji-yong to head the newly established Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—a core pillar of South Korea's institutional prosecution reforms—has drawn intense scrutiny. During his parliamentary confirmation hearings, ruling and opposition lawmakers alike confronted Kim with his prosecutorial past, including an episode where his office dropped sentencing demands against a defendant connected to a former prosecutor general. Critics argue that Kim was complicit in political prosecutions by refusing to register internal objections, yet the presidential office appears determined to finalize his nomination regardless of majority public disapproval.

This pattern extends to the presidential secretariat. The selection of Chief of Staff Lim Gwang-hyun has fueled skepticism over presidential nepotism and loyalty tests. Lim, who had sought high office during the period of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol before Yoon was impeached and imprisoned, has personal ties to the president's legal defense circle. To critics within the ruling party, these appointments reflect a deliberate strategy to surround the president with aides who will execute orders without offering difficult policy dissent.

Stifled Debate and Growing Fatigue in the Ruling Base

Frustration is also surfacing inside the government's daily operations. The administration's hallmark practice of live-streaming cabinet meetings was meant to showcase transparency, but ministers report it has reduced deliberative governance to a reality show. Policies debated for months—such as lowering the age of criminal responsibility or setting standards for the contentious Yellow Envelope labor bill—have been abruptly upended on live television by spontaneous remarks from the president, leaving ministers unable to push back without publicly defying their boss.

For the ruling Democratic Party, this growing centralization of power poses a dangerous dilemma. Influential progressive voices are warning that suppressing candid advice will alienate the public and isolate the Blue House. While the administration continues to brush aside warnings from media and civil society, the simmering discontent among its own coalition signals that President Lee's top-down approach may soon exhaust its political capital.
