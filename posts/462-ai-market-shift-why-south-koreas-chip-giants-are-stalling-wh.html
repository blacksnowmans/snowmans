AI Market Shift: Why South Korea’s Chip Giants Are Stalling While Big Tech Rallies

September 30, 2026

  • The shift: Market leadership in the artificial intelligence sector has pivoted away from infrastructure and hardware toward software distribution and user-facing AI agents.
  • The losers: South Korean memory chip giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix have seen their stock prices slump since July, weighed down by skepticism over how long the current chip boom will last.
  • The winners: US tech giants like Microsoft and Meta are surging as investors bet on their control over the "front door" of AI through apps, routing, and user interfaces.
  • The stakes: While data centers will keep buying chips regardless of who wins the software war, the market is currently starving hardware makers of high valuations in favor of high-growth platforms.

The Great Pivot: From Infrastructure to Distribution

For the first half of the year, the AI trade was simple: spend massive sums on infrastructure, and buy the chipmakers supplying the essential hardware. SK Hynix and Samsung soared on soaring demand for high-bandwidth memory, while major cloud providers lagged. Since July, however, that playbook has reversed entirely. Wall Street giants like Microsoft and Meta have rallied, while Korean chip stocks have stumbled in a bizarre inversion that signals a deeper change in market psychology.

Wall Street analysts have begun questioning the economic returns on multi-billion-dollar AI capital expenditures, warning that monetization is failing to keep pace with spending. Fear of missing out (FOMO) keeps tech companies spending heavily, but investors are increasingly asking when those costs will be recovered. As capital flows toward consumer-facing AI agents—such as Microsoft's Copilot Home or Meta's routing models—that control user traffic and software entry points, hardware suppliers risk being relegated to low-margin subcontractors.

The Valuation Trap Facing Memory Makers

Despite posting record profits driven by unprecedented AI demand, memory chipmakers are stuck trading at low price-to-earnings (P/E) multiples of 4 to 5. The market views their current earnings as a cyclical peak rather than a sustainable baseline, assuming a downturn will arrive by 2028. To break out of this valuation trap, companies like Samsung and SK Hynix need more than strong short-term earnings reports; they need to convince global investors of long-term structural growth through advanced technologies like 3D DRAM and more aggressive shareholder return policies.

Ultimately, the fierce battle between infrastructure and distribution is a reminder that the AI boom is an evolving ecosystem rather than a one-sided trade. While software platforms fight for user attention, the physical data centers powering them must keep expanding. For investors watching from the sidelines, navigating this volatility requires looking past short-term market noise and focusing on the underlying fundamentals of the technology driving the future.
