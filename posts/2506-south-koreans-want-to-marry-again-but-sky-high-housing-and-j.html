South Koreans Want to Marry Again, but Sky-High Housing and Job Anxiety Stand in the Way

November 19, 2026

While positive attitudes toward marriage in South Korea reached a 12-year high of 55 percent, young adults remain largely paralyzed by steep financial burdens, expensive housing, and persistent employment insecurity.

  • Shift in sentiment: National survey data shows 55 percent of respondents now view marriage positively, though a stark gender gap persists: 43.9 percent of unmarried men hold a positive view compared to just 29.9 percent of unmarried women.
  • Financial barriers lead: A lack of marriage funds was cited as the top deterrent, fueled by Seoul housing prices averaging 11.7 times annual income and forcing newlyweds to spend a fifth of their earnings on housing costs.
  • Evolving policy demands: Public demand for tackling South Korea's record-low birth rate is rapidly shifting away from temporary government cash giveaways toward permanent job creation and employment stability.
  • Push for structural reform: Lawmakers have introduced a bill to abolish the legal requirement forcing children to automatically take their father's surname, signaling broader friction between traditional family laws and modern social values.

Economic Realities Outweigh Social Desire

The latest survey figures present a striking paradox for South Korean policymakers. While social stigma against marriage appears to be fading, the financial hurdle of establishing a household has grown nearly insurmountable for young workers facing high interest rates on housing loans. The 14 percentage point sentiment gap between unmarried men and women also underscores how women continue to anticipate a heavier burden under conventional family expectations and career interruptions.

Beyond Cash Giveaways: The Shift to Structural Fixes

Local governments are trying to plug the gap with regional incentives. Wanju County in North Jeolla Province has expanded settlement packages to maintain its 100,000 population, while Seoul’s Gangseo District budgeted a 10 million won (about $7,200) bonus for first-born children. However, temporary subsidies fail to address the core issue: precarious contract work and runaway housing costs. Without durable job creation and legal modernization—such as enabling parents to mutually choose a child's surname—cash handouts will remain a costly, temporary bandage on a systemic crisis.
