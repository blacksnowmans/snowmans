South Korea's Politics Plunges Into Crisis Over Impeachment Talk and Party Dissolution Threats

November 11, 2026

South Korea's National Assembly ground to a halt on November 10 after the conservative opposition took to the streets hinting at presidential impeachment, prompting the ruling party to retaliate with threats to petition for the opposition's legal dissolution.

  • Street revolt at the presidential gates: Roughly 80 lawmakers from the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition, boycotted legislative sessions to protest outside the presidential office, demanding a veto against a special counsel bill targeting alleged prosecutorial abuses.
  • Nuclear rhetoric: The standoff exploded when a senior PPP leader floated "a new president in the new spring," prompting the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) to brand the remark a "second insurrection plot" and threaten to seek a constitutional order to dissolve the PPP.
  • Legislative paralysis: Committee hearings and reviews of next year's national budget were completely abandoned as opposition lawmakers vacated the assembly to wage outdoor rallies.
  • Nationwide escalation: The PPP announced plans to expand its street protests into a nationwide tour, dimming any prospects for dialogue between the rival camps.

From Street Protests to Existential Threats

The immediate spark for the showdown was a controversial special counsel bill investigating alleged fabricated indictments, a measure passed by the majority DPK that the PPP has labeled a "prison-break bill" tailored to protect President Lee Jae-myung. Outnumbered on the assembly floor, the PPP opted for open-air confrontation outside the presidential compound at Cheong Wa Dae, demanding a presidential veto. However, the nature of the protest shifted radically when PPP senior supreme council member Jang Dong-hyeok took the stage to declare "a new president in the new spring"—a thinly veiled call for early presidential removal.

The DPK responded with unprecedented fury. Floor leader Han Byung-do immediately convened an emergency party meeting, denouncing the speech as an unconstitutional attempt to topple the elected government. Han warned that the PPP's behavior justified initiating proceedings with the Constitutional Court to disband the conservative party altogether—the most severe legal weapon available against a political organization in South Korea.

A Total Breakdown of Parliamentary Restraint

When the country's two dominant factions trade threats of presidential impeachment and party annihilation in a single day, it signals that the safety buffers of South Korean democracy have worn dangerously thin. The confrontation has reduced the legislature to an empty shell at the exact moment lawmakers should be scrutinizing next year's fiscal budget amid rising living costs and a sluggish economy.

Both sides bear responsibility for this race to the extremes. Abandoning parliamentary committee rooms to chant street slogans damages the opposition's credibility as a responsible governing alternative. At the same time, the ruling party's counter-threat to outlaw its political rival mimics the authoritarian tactics democratic politics is designed to prevent. With the opposition preparing to take its campaign across major cities, South Korea's legislative politics has effectively devolved into total factional warfare.
