South Korea’s Judiciary Under Fire: Chief Justice Faces Criminal Complaints and Delay Allegations

October 14, 2026

South Korea’s top judicial leadership is locked in an unprecedented constitutional standoff with parliament, facing sharp accusations of misusing nomination powers, embezzling public funds, and deliberately stalling sensitive trials tied to figures from the deposed administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

  • Constitutional friction: Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae refused to take an oath at a parliamentary audit while asserting recommendation powers that lawmakers argue unconstitutionally encroach on President Lee Jae-myung’s presidential appointment authority.
  • Criminal allegations filed: The progressive minor opposition Rebuilding Korea Party accused Cho of seven criminal offenses, including misallocating between 610 million and 650 million won (about $450,000 to $480,000) in judicial incentive funds and falsifying official entertainment receipts.
  • Suspicious trial stall: The Supreme Court has sat on an appeal regarding a judge-recusal motion in the trial of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo for over ten months, sparking allegations of a tactical delay until scheduled judicial rotations in February.

Embezzlement Charges and High-Level Gridlock

The confrontation escalated dramatically in the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee. Democratic Party lawmakers pressed judicial administrators over Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae's refusal to swear an oath and his stance that the president must strictly defer to the Chief Justice's recommendations for vacant judicial seats. Critics, including legal scholars, argue that the constitution grants the Chief Justice a duty to recommend candidates to President Lee, not a de facto veto to dictate appointments or leave benches vacant.

Compounding this standoff, the Rebuilding Korea Party, represented by lawmaker Shin Jang-sik, escalated the fight by submitting criminal complaints against Cho to the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO). The allegations detail systemic misuse of official operational budgets, ranging from improperly distributed incentive funds to doctoring attendance counts on dinner receipts with legal figures to circumvent South Korea's strict anti-graft statutes. For an institution that demands uncompromising ethical compliance from ordinary citizens, the documented discrepancies represent a severe reputational blow.

Judicial Stalling and Political Calculations

The broader political flashpoint centers on accusations that conservative-leaning Supreme Court justices are intentionally running out the clock on high-profile trials. Following the prosecution of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo late last year, defense efforts to recuse presiding Judge Lee Jin-gwan were rejected by both the district and appellate courts. Yet the matter has languished without a ruling in the Supreme Court under Justice Seo Kyung-hwan for nearly a year, reportedly stalled by internal divisions between conservative justices and moderate members like Justice Cheon Dae-yeop.

By sitting on the file, the apex court provides temporary shielding to figures from Mr. Yoon’s disgraced tenure, including former economic deputy Choi Sang-mok, who risk immediate legal fallout if lower-court trials proceed to verdict. With routine judicial reassignments scheduled for February, a prolonged delay could replace the trial bench entirely, disrupting proceedings and diffusing institutional accountability.

What is playing out is not an ordinary jurisdictional disagreement, but an overt battle over the boundary between judicial independence and self-serving immunity. With the CIO under scrutiny for past reluctance to confront entrenched legal networks, pressure is mounting from outside the courts. If top judges use procedural inertia to protect old allies and insulate their own offices from scrutiny, public faith in South Korea’s rule of law faces its sharpest test yet.
