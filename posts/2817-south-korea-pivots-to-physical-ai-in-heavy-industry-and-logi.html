South Korea Pivots to 'Physical AI' in Heavy Industry and Logistics

November 28, 2026

South Korea is aggressively shifting its AI strategy from digital software to "Physical AI," integrating autonomous intelligence into the hardware of shipyards, urban delivery routes, and manufacturing lines.

  • Network Breakthrough: LG Uplus, a major Korean telecommunications provider, and the Hyundai-Kia automotive group successfully tested 5G Standalone (SA) networks to ensure delivery robots operate without lag in crowded urban zones.
  • Autonomous Manufacturing: Robotics software firm Clobot and MIT have launched a 11.7 billion won (about $8.4 million) R&D project to develop robots that can self-correct and handle unexpected variables on factory floors.
  • Industrial AX: The Lee Jae-myung administration is prioritizing "AI Transformation" (AX) in heavy industries, recently visiting the HD Hyundai Samho shipyard—a major subsidiary of the HD Hyundai conglomerate—to oversee the integration of robotics into shipbuilding.
  • Strategic Roadmap: The government has set 2027 as the target for a national manufacturing and logistics overhaul, supported by new regional tech hubs like the proposed "Urban Convergence Special Zone" in the historic city of Jeonju.

From Digital Screens to Physical Reality

The recent surge in Physical AI developments signals a shift in how South Korea views its industrial competitive edge. For years, AI was confined to chatbots and data analysis. Now, through 5G Standalone technology, the Lee Jae-myung government and private sector giants like Hyundai are building the digital infrastructure—the "robot highways"—necessary for autonomous machines to navigate the physical world reliably. This is not just about automation; it is about "spatial intelligence," where machines like those equipped with SOS Lab’s LiDAR technology can understand 3D environments as accurately as humans.

The collaboration between Clobot and MIT highlights a move toward "autonomous manufacturing." Traditional factory robots are brittle; they stop the moment a part is slightly out of place. The new goal is a "brain" for machines that can troubleshoot in real-time. By investing heavily in this software-hardware hybrid, South Korea aims to solve chronic labor shortages in its manufacturing and shipbuilding sectors while maintaining its position as a global hardware powerhouse against competitors like China's Xpeng, which is already pivoting from EVs to humanoids.

Policy Ambition vs. Regulatory Reality

The 2027 roadmap released by the Ministry of Science and ICT reflects a "total war" approach to AI leadership. By transforming heavy industries like shipbuilding—traditionally dangerous and labor-intensive—into high-tech testing grounds, the Lee administration is attempting to revitalize regional economies outside of Seoul. The designation of Jeonju as a candidate for a Physical AI hub suggests a decentralized strategy where local universities like KAIST and Hanyang ERICA collaborate with industry to create "robot-friendly" cities.

"While the technology is arriving in the field at light speed, the legal framework for liability and robot status remains fragmented and outdated."

However, a significant bottleneck remains: legislation. While the government is pouring billions into R&D and pilot programs, South Korea still lacks clear legal standards for robot-related accidents and safety responsibilities. For the Physical AI revolution to move from the shipyard to the sidewalk, the National Assembly must provide the regulatory certainty that private investors need. Without a clear legal status for autonomous robots, the transition to a robot-driven economy may face significant friction regardless of how advanced the hardware becomes.
