How to Age Well: Lessons from South Korean Psychologist Lee Ho-sun

September 03, 2026

South Korean veteran psychologist Lee Ho-sun argues that people fear aging not because of the years passing, but because they were never taught the practical mechanics of how to grow older.

    What happened: In a widely discussed commentary drawing on 27 years of counseling experience, Lee broke down the mental and physical framework required for a fulfilling second half of life.
  • What it changes: It shifts the view of aging from an inevitable decline to a skill-based transition requiring active physical and psychological maintenance.
  • Who is affected: Anyone navigating mid-life transitions, long-term marriage, or looking ahead to later life without a clear roadmap.
  • Core framework: Lee summarizes the approach with the Korean mnemonic "Mak-gong-na-man"—focusing on physical health, mental curiosity, social engagement, and learning from younger generations.

The Four Pillars of Modern Aging

The biggest trap people fall into during mid-life is trying to update their mental software while completely neglecting their physical hardware. Lee stresses that high-minded philosophy means nothing when the body gives out. The prescription is straightforward: build core physical strength, because physical stamina directly underpins mental resilience. Alongside physical care, the brain requires active stimulation—not for academic degrees, but to maintain curiosity and keep up with a fast-moving world.

Isolation is another major hazard of aging. Lee advises deliberately stepping out of one's comfort zone and engaging with younger generations, treating their energy as fuel rather than a threat. In relationships, long-term couples often fall into bitter familiarity. Rather than rushing into divorce over minor frictions—barring abuse or addiction—Lee suggests treating relationships like a diet: introduce healthy boundaries, cut out toxic habits, and use simple gestures of appreciation, like a kind word in the morning, to keep the bond intact.

Choosing Attitude Over Circumstance

Growing older does not automatically make people wiser or more generous; without deliberate effort, aging often just makes people more stubborn. Drawing on decades of counseling, Lee notes that 90% of human happiness depends entirely on perspective rather than external circumstances. Shifting from a mindset of lack—constantly comparing oneself to others—to an intentional practice of counting what one already has is the single most effective tool for emotional survival.

As actor Kwon Sang-woo recently reflected when discussing his career at age 50, reaching middle age should not mean slowing down or settling into complacency. Instead, it should serve as a deadline that sharpens one's focus, turning the remaining years into an urgent opportunity to live with genuine conviction. Aging well isn't about luck; it is a discipline of tending to one's own mindset and refusing to let envy dictate the value of a life well-lived.
