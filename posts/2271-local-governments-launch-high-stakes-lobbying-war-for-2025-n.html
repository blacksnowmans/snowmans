Local Governments Launch High-Stakes Lobbying War for 2025 National Budget

November 13, 2026

South Korea’s regional administrations have shifted their operations to the National Assembly to fight for a share of the 2025 budget, with Gyeonggi Province leading an aggressive campaign to secure nearly $4 billion in funding.

  • Gyeonggi Province has established a 24-hour "war room" across from the National Assembly to monitor the review of its 5.435 trillion won (approx. $3.9 billion) funding request.
  • Namyangju City is pushing for a legislative change to have the central government cover 80% of elderly care costs, which would save the city about $30 million (40.8 billion won) in local funds.
  • The National Assembly’s Budget Subcommittee—the final and most rigorous "filter" for government spending—is set to begin intensive reviews where local projects are often cut or expanded based on political leverage.
  • Conservative strongholds like Daegu and Gyeongbuk are taking the unusual step of forming a "United Front" to lobby the leadership of the Democratic Party, South Korea’s current ruling party, to protect their regional infrastructure.

The Fight for Regional Survival in a Tight Economy

The intensity of this year's lobbying reflects a deepening crisis in regional South Korea. As the "metropolitan concentration" in Seoul continues to drain the provinces of people and resources, local governments view the 2025 budget as their final bulwark against regional extinction. Gyeonggi Province, the nation's most populous region, has even stationed its Vice Governor near the legislature to personally oversee the budget push. This level of executive presence highlights that for these regions, securing Social Overhead Capital (SOC)—the Korean term for major infrastructure like roads and rails—is no longer just about development, but about basic economic survival.

The political landscape adds another layer of complexity. With President Lee Jae-myung prioritizing universal welfare and balanced regional growth, local leaders are framing their requests to align with the administration's "national responsibility" model. This is a sharp departure from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose tenure was marked by austerity measures that many local officials say left regional economies in a "slump." Now, municipalities are demanding that the central government reclaim the financial burden of welfare programs, such as long-term care for the elderly, which have historically drained local coffers.

Beyond Infrastructure: The Rise of Ethical Budgeting

While massive projects like the Baengnyeong Island airport in Incheon or energy clusters in Jeonnam remain priorities, a new focus on "ethical budgeting" has emerged. Lawmakers are increasingly under pressure to fund social safety nets that were neglected in previous years. For example, advocates in Daejeon are calling for urgent national support for "shantytowns" (jjokbangchon), arguing that basic human dignity should take precedence over flashy construction projects. This shift reflects a broader mandate from the electorate to prioritize the immediate quality of life over long-term industrial speculation.

However, the process remains fraught with political risk. The People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, continues to scrutinize these requests as potential "populism." As the Budget Subcommittee begins its work, the real test will be whether the ruling Democratic Party can deliver on its welfare promises without triggering a fiscal backlash. For the local officials currently sleeping in "war rooms" near the National Assembly, the next few weeks will determine whether their regions see a spring of revitalization or another year of managed decline.
