Acquittal and Scandal Deepen Crisis Inside South Korea’s Conservative Opposition

November 20, 2026

South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), is sliding into open factional conflict after a court acquitted a senior lawmaker of insurrection-related charges and another accused party leader Jang Dong-hyuk of rigging internal discipline, according to Korean media reports and political commentary from November 19.

  • Court reversal sparks backlash: Lawmaker Choo Kyung-ho’s first-instance acquittal undercut the party leadership’s internal purge campaign, prompting non-mainstream lawmakers to accuse Jang and floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik of falsely branding colleagues as insurrectionists.
  • Walkout over rigging claims: Broadcaster JTBC reported that Jang abruptly ended a party meeting without answering when lawmaker Bae Hyun-jin alleged that past disciplinary proceedings against her had been deliberately fabricated.
  • Leadership ratings sink: A poll by agency Realmeter for the third week of November showed Jang’s job approval falling 1.6 percentage points to 32.9%, even though 63.4% of respondents negatively rated the livelihood policies of President Lee Jae-myung’s ruling Democratic Party government.
  • Blocked rivals and local splits: Former party committee chair Kim Jong-in told news agency Newsis that Jang is opposing rival Han Dong-hoon’s return to the party out of fear of losing a leadership convention, while local outlet Taehee reported that factional infighting has fractured regional chapters such as Cheongju Sangdang.

Hardline Rhetoric Over Internal Reform

Rather than addressing the internal turmoil, the PPP leadership has pivoted toward aggressive attacks on President Lee’s administration. On November 19, party officials blasted the government’s management of the Suneung—South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam—calling for the education minister’s dismissal, while Jang denounced a ruling-party bill supporting democracy activists and condemned moves toward impeaching the Supreme Court Chief Justice.

As Korean political commentators and outlets such as BreakNews point out, however, this confrontational strategy appears to be backfiring as a deflection tactic. Accusing the Lee administration of undermining judicial independence carries little weight with voters when the opposition leadership simultaneously questions a court’s acquittal of its own member and shuts down debate over alleged internal manipulation.

The polling numbers suggest that the PPP’s core weakness is structural rather than tactical. With public dissatisfaction over the government’s livelihood performance running above 60%, an effective opposition would normally be gaining ground. Instead, lingering resentment dating back to Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, combined with perceived gatekeeping against competitors like Han Dong-hoon, has left the party fragmented. Unless Jang’s leadership restores transparent internal procedures and allows open competition, observers warn the PPP risks losing both its ability to challenge the ruling party and its credibility as a democratic organization.
