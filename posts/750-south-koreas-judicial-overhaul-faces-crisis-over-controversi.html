South Korea’s Judicial Overhaul Faces Crisis Over Controversial Appointment

September 14, 2026

South Korea is set for a historic transformation of its criminal justice system on October 2, as the Prosecution Service—long criticized for its unchecked power—is replaced by two new bodies: the Prosecution Office (focusing solely on indictment) and the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA). While this split between investigation and prosecution marks a landmark goal of the current administration, the nomination of Kim Yong-min as the first head of the SCIA has sparked intense public backlash and accusations of institutional hypocrisy.

  • The Reform: The Prosecution Service will be abolished, with its investigative powers transferred to the new SCIA, effectively separating the roles of "investigator" and "prosecutor."
  • The Controversy: Kim Yong-min, the nominee for the new agency’s chief, spent his career as a prosecutor vehemently opposing the very separation of powers he is now expected to oversee.
  • The Cost: The government has allocated over $1.1 billion (1.5 trillion KRW) in combined budgets for these new agencies, raising the stakes for their successful implementation.
  • The Risk: Critics fear that appointing a former "political prosecutor" who protected the old guard will result in the SCIA becoming "a second Prosecution Service," rather than a tool for reform.

A Contradictory Choice

The nomination of Kim Yong-min is being viewed by many as a betrayal of the reforms the administration promised. Throughout his career, Kim was a vocal opponent of limiting prosecutorial powers. Notably, in 2022, while serving as a senior official at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, he publicly fought against the legislative efforts to decouple investigation and indictment. Entrusting the leadership of an agency built specifically to curb prosecutorial power to one of its most staunch defenders is a move that many citizens are calling a mockery of the democratic process.

Furthermore, Kim’s history is marred by allegations of "political investigations." He was involved in several high-profile cases—including the controversial indictment of a fellow prosecutor during the Channel A scandal and the prosecution of officials involved in the Lee Kwang-jae/Kim Hak-ui exit ban case—both of which were later cleared by courts. His past alignment with conservative political interests, particularly his ties to former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon, makes his appointment appear more like a preservation of old power structures than a step toward a new, transparent system.

High Stakes, Low Transparency

With an massive budget exceeding $1.1 billion (1.5 trillion KRW) and a staff of nearly 3,000, the SCIA is a gargantuan new entity. Its reach will extend across six regional offices, targeting seven types of "serious crimes," including corruption, defense industry fraud, and cybercrime. Given this immense authority and the public funds committed to the transition, the lack of transparency from the nominee is alarming.

When pressed on his stance regarding the separation of powers or his past opposition to these reforms, Kim has consistently deflected, stating he will "explain at his confirmation hearing." For an official tasked with leading a new pillar of the nation's judicial architecture, this silence is being interpreted as a fundamental lack of qualification. If he cannot articulate a commitment to the new system, he arguably lacks the mandate to lead it. The tragedy of this situation is clear: the administration risks wasting years of public struggle and massive taxpayer investment by placing the wrong person at the helm of a vital, yet fragile, new institution.
