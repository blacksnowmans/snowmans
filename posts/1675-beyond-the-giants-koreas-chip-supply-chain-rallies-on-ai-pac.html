Beyond the Giants: Korea’s Chip Supply Chain Rallies on AI Packaging Demand

October 29, 2026

South Korea’s semiconductor rally is expanding beyond chipmakers Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, as booming artificial intelligence demand channels fresh capital and orders into the country’s domestic materials, parts, and equipment suppliers.

  • Major order wins and localization: Chip substrate inspection specialist GigaVis secured a 30.6 billion won (about $23 million) supply contract, while testing firm QRT achieved domestic substitution that cut reliance on foreign evaluation equipment by roughly 70%.
  • Global toolmakers expand local sourcing: US semiconductor equipment giant Lam Research committed to expanding its procurement of South Korean-made components to 2 trillion won (about $1.5 billion), turning the country into an export hub for global tools.
  • Substrate earnings surge: Component giant Samsung Electro-Mechanics reported a 165% year-on-year surge in operating profit, fueled by soaring demand for high-value flip-chip ball grid array (FC-BGA) substrates used in AI servers.
  • Retail capital shifts downstream: Shares in equipment vendors like Wonik IPS gained nearly 5%, while retail investors poured hundreds of billions of won into exchange-traded funds focused on advanced packaging and back-end testing.

From Import Reliance to Global Integration

For years, South Korea’s sobujang—the shorthand for its domestic materials, parts, and equipment ecosystem—struggled to escape the shadow of foreign suppliers from Japan and the United States. The AI boom has upended that dynamic. Because artificial intelligence hardware depends heavily on high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and dense advanced packaging, the physical process of stacking, connecting, and testing chips has become the industry’s most critical bottleneck. As a result, domestic back-end toolmakers are no longer treated as lower-margin subcontractors, but as essential partners in yield optimization.

Lam Research’s decision to source $1.5 billion in parts locally illustrates this structural shift. Multinational toolmakers now see Korean equipment firms not just as local service providers, but as qualified vendors capable of feeding global production lines. While Chinese competitors like CXMT continue aggressive capacity expansions, South Korean equipment leaders maintain an edge rooted in deep process integration with leading memory fabricators.

Capital Gaps Outside the Metro Core

Despite the market euphoria across Seoul’s tech-heavy Kosdaq exchange, structural vulnerabilities persist beneath the surface. South Korea’s semiconductor strength remains overwhelmingly clustered in the Seoul metropolitan area. A domestic foundry venture in the southern port city of Busan—which chose to repatriate operations rather than remain in China—now faces the risk of collapse due to chronic deficits in regional power, specialized labor, and local supply chain networks.

Stock rallies for advanced packaging specialists demonstrate real commercial progress, but long-term resilience requires a balanced domestic base. If South Korea is to defend its manufacturing moat against subsidized Chinese competition, industrial policy must extend beyond celebrated Kosdaq listings to address the severe infrastructure and financing gaps threatening foundational suppliers outside the capital.
