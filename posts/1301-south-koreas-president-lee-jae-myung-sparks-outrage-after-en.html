South Korea’s President Lee Jae-myung Sparks Outrage After Endorsing Hardline YouTube Attack Channel

October 19, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has sparked a political crisis within his own progressive camp after publicly endorsing a hardline YouTube channel that has been waging smear campaigns against prominent internal critics.

  • What happened: President Lee gave an online shoutout to "Minju is a Police Station," a radical pro-government YouTube channel associated with the loyalist "New Lee" faction, which has been attempting to resurrect old financial corruption allegations against prominent progressive writer Rhyu Si-min.
  • Who is affected: Dissenting intellectuals and figures within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, alongside allies such as Cho Kuk, leader of the minor progressive Cho Kuk Rebuilding Party, who condemned the attacks as a dangerous tactic that crosses political boundaries.
  • What it changes: Internal strategy leaks from the ruling bloc reveal a calculated effort by pro-Lee strategists to provoke non-mainstream members into exiting the party, locking South Korean politics into a stark "Pro-Lee versus Anti-Lee" polarization.

Presidents, Cyberbullying, and Fandom Warfare

President Lee’s open engagement with fringe online influencers marks a troubling shift in South Korean governance. By publicly highlighting aggressive YouTubers, the chief executive is leveraging his massive social media platform to implicitly direct fanatical online mobs against internal party critics. Commentators, including progressive economist Choi Bae-keun, have described this approach as a new form of state-adjacent cyberbullying. Because moral integrity is the primary currency for South Korean progressive intellectuals, attempting to frame figures like Rhyu as corrupt or hypocritical aims to strip critics of their moral standing and permanently silence constructive debate.

The Strategic Trap of Radical Factionalism

This tactic of enforcing strict orthodoxy threatens to fracture the broader progressive alliance that came together following the impeachment and imprisonment of former president Yoon Suk-yeol. Rather than building a broad-tent coalition, Lee’s inner circle appears to be borrowing from older authoritarian playbooks by branding principled dissenters as "dangerous radicals" or unloyal opportunists. While this strategy may consolidate factional control over the ruling party in the short term, it severely degrades the democratic quality of public discourse. If President Lee continues to rely on smartphone warfare and fandom loyalty over systematic governance, he risks alienating moderate voters and crippling his political mandate.
