South Korea’s Semiconductor Boom Masks a Brutal Collapse in Ordinary Livelihoods

November 07, 2026

  • Despite record-breaking semiconductor exports, South Korea's ordinary households and domestic small businesses are enduring a deep economic slump.
  • Youth employment has hit a wall, while job growth for prime working-age adults and small-business regular workers has plummeted to a fraction of previous levels.
  • Real wages have retreated to levels seen a decade ago, and self-employed earnings have dropped below pandemic-era lows, fueling widespread public anger.
  • Critics argue that the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung has neglected domestic economic reforms while prioritizing corporate handouts to heavy exporters.

South Korea’s macroeconomic dashboard is flashing green, but the country's street-level economy is flashing red. Powered by a global artificial intelligence and memory chip super-cycle, national exports have shattered records. Yet behind these glossy trade statistics lies a harsh reality: ordinary citizens, young job-seekers, and neighborhood merchants are suffering through a severe economic contraction that feels more like a recession than a boom.

When former conservative leader Yoon Seok-yeol was impeached and removed from office, the public placed immense hope in the progressive administration that followed. Voters expected a renewed focus on Main Street, better protection for working-class labor, and a narrowing of wealth inequality. Instead, the current government has leaned heavily on old conservative playbooks, channeling state capital and massive deregulation into semiconductor giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix while leaving the domestic economy to starve.

The Illusion of Tech-Led Growth and the Employment Cliff

The core contradiction of South Korea's current economy is that high-tech manufacturing creates very few jobs relative to the capital it absorbs. While the government rolls out grand blueprints—such as a multi-decade, 700 trillion-won (about $500 billion) investment strategy for regional semiconductor clusters—the domestic job market is freezing over. In the third quarter, overall job growth slumped to just 134,000 new positions, less than half the rate seen under the Moon Jae-in administration. Meanwhile, prime working-age employment (ages 25 to 54) shrank by 130,000, leaving young adults trapped in a grueling employment ice age.

This export-first model offers little trickle-down relief. High-tech fabrication plants rely heavily on imported advanced machinery, meaning export revenues rarely circulate down to domestic small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). For the millions of young people stuck scrolling through job boards or drifting between part-time gigs, government boasts of trillion-dollar export milestones feel less like national success and more like a cruel taunt.

Crushed Purchasing Power and the Collapse of Main Street

The pain in the export sector has spilled directly into the domestic economy. SMEs—which account for roughly 75% of all jobs in South Korea—have seen regular job growth crash from a monthly average of 350,000 under past administrations to just 50,000 under President Lee's watch. Compounding this, real wages for SME workers have fallen below the levels recorded during the tumultuous final years of the Yoon Seok-yeol era. With purchasing power sliding backward by a decade, household consumption has cratered.

Nowhere is this suffering more acute than in the nation's alleyways and neighborhood markets. Average monthly sales for self-employed small business owners have plunged to roughly 8 million won (about $5,800), falling below the bleakest quarters of the COVID-19 pandemic when social distancing was strictly enforced. Unlike the pandemic era, however, store owners today receive no safety nets to cushion the blow of high inflation and soaring interest rates.

Ultimately, a government cannot build long-term stability on export figures alone while the domestic roots of society rot. If the administration continues to ignore the structural plight of precarious workers, struggling youth, and corner-shop owners in favor of corporate giants, it risks squandering the democratic mandate that brought it to power.
