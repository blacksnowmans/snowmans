South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Crisis as President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Plunges

October 28, 2026

Disapproval of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has officially overtaken approval for the first time in his presidency, signaling a sharp end to his administration's honeymoon phase and sparking deep structural anxiety within the ruling bloc.

  • What happened: President Lee Jae-myung’s polling numbers hit a "dead cross"—where negative ratings outpace positive ones—with approval dropping into the 35–40% range while disapproval surged to between 50% and 62% in recent Realmeter and Gallup surveys.
  • What it changes: Support for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is eroding rapidly among moderate and core progressive voters, who are increasingly shifting their alignment toward alternative political outlets.
  • Who is affected: President Lee’s executive agenda, party leadership—including Democratic Party Supreme Council Member Kim Min-seok—and the broader ruling coalition face mounting legislative inertia and internal factional pressure.

From Honeymoon to 'Dead Cross': The Drag on Executive Power

After early post-election approval ratings hovered comfortably in the 60% to 70% range, President Lee’s administration is now confronting a sobering reality. According to Gallup Korea, Lee’s approval rating stood at 46% entering the first quarter of his second year—a steep drop compared to former President Moon Jae-in’s 75% rating at the exact same juncture. Further Realmeter survey data showed negative assessments climbing as high as 62% against a 35% positive mark, leaving the administration dangerously close to a prolonged political paralysis. In South Korea's hyper-competitive landscape, a entrenched low approval rating severely weakens bureaucratic compliance, emboldens critical media, and invites internal dissension. Political commentators cite the tragic precedent of Yoon Suk-yeol, whose approval ratings collapsed to 35% early in his term before his eventual unconstitutional attempt at martial law resulted in his impeachment and imprisonment.

Core Base Fractures and the Rise of Voter Mobility

The rating drop is not merely an issue for President Lee personally; it has spread directly to the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Polling reveals a sharp drop in party support alongside a notable surge for the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive opposition party that is serving as an emergency release valve for frustrated liberal voters. Between August and October, Democratic Party support among self-identified core progressives fell from 74.4% to 60.6%, while the Rebuilding Korea Party’s share among the same group more than doubled from 10.5% to 22.4%. Meanwhile, Democratic Party support among political moderates dropped from 49% to 41.9%.

Critics attribute this shift to heavy-handed leadership within the Democratic Party, including Supreme Council Member Kim Min-seok, whose aggressive rhetoric targeting internal party critics has alienated swing voters rather than displaying inclusive governance. With South Korean voters demonstrating increasingly high "voter mobility"—unwilling to grant blind loyalty to any single party—the Lee administration faces an urgent imperative to reform its policy stance and political communication before its legislative momentum stalls completely.
