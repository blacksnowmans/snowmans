Why South Koreans are pivoting from e-commerce to Airbnb hosting

September 10, 2026

As traditional side hustles like e-commerce and blogging become increasingly saturated and labor-intensive, a growing number of South Koreans are turning to "space leasing"—specifically, co-operating Airbnb properties—to build more reliable, automated cash flow.

  • What happened: Many side hustlers are abandoning "Red Ocean" markets like Naver Smart Store (South Korea's most popular e-commerce platform) due to thin margins and algorithmic burnout.
  • The shift: Airbnb hosting offers daily, consistent settlements and flexible hours, appealing to workers and stay-at-home parents alike.
  • Financial impact: Operators are leveraging government policy loans (up to about $22,000 or 30 million won) to cover initial setup costs, turning low-cost villas into profitable, managed lodging.

The Shift from E-commerce to Real Estate

For many Koreans, the dream of a side hustle has hit a wall. Between rising costs of living and stagnant wages, thousands flocked to platforms like Smart Store, only to find themselves shackled to their computers, fighting for algorithmic visibility for negligible returns. Unlike e-commerce, which is plagued by long settlement cycles and endless customer service disputes, Airbnb offers a more immediate financial reward: payouts are processed almost as soon as a guest checks in, providing a steady daily cash flow.

Professional operators, such as the brand "Sohaeng" (a play on words meaning "to execute"), have scaled this model to manage dozens of properties across Seoul, Busan, and Jeju. The strategy relies on converting older, undervalued residential units—typically villas or multi-family homes—into branded, short-term rentals. By focusing on "space branding," hosts aren't just renting out rooms; they are curating a travel experience for tourists.

Legal Hurdles and Strategic Growth

Success in this field requires navigating strict South Korean regulations. Foreigner-designated urban bed-and-breakfast permits (oeguk-in dosi minbak-eop) are the only legal path for this type of business. Critically, these permits require properties to have at least two rooms. Operators often avoid apartments and office-tels, as these are generally off-limits for legal short-term lodging and prone to neighbor complaints that trigger shutdowns by local authorities.

The barrier to entry isn't just bureaucratic; it’s relational. Experienced hosts emphasize that the best way to secure properties is through "footwork"—visiting real estate agents and landlords in person to build trust. By renovating one unit in a building to a high standard, an operator can often persuade a landlord to lease additional units, effectively turning one successful contract into a multi-property portfolio.

Success in space leasing is less about having capital and more about "execution"—the willingness to knock on doors, negotiate for hours, and manage physical spaces rather than chasing clicks behind a screen.

While the prospect of automated income is attractive, it is not a "get rich quick" scheme. The risk of running afoul of the law or facing a crackdown from local authorities is real. However, for those willing to do the legwork and adhere to the strict permit requirements, Airbnb co-operation offers a rare alternative to the high-competition, low-reward digital economy.
