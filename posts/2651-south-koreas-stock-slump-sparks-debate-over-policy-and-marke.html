South Korea’s Stock Slump Sparks Debate Over Policy and Market Confidence

November 23, 2026

A critical commentary on South Korea’s capital markets argues that a 42% plunge in the benchmark KOSPI stock index from its 2026 peak—driven by a 31.2% foreign investor exodus—reflects domestic policy missteps under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration rather than global economic headwinds alone.

  • Stabilization gap: While Taiwan limited its market pullback to 8.5% by deploying its NT$500 billion (about $15.5 billion) National Financial Stabilization Fund, the commentary notes that Seoul left its own stabilization fund unused even as volatility surged during Middle East conflict shocks.
  • Leveraged products and tax proposals: Critics point to the May 27 rollout of single-stock 2x leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs)—which they say amplified forced selling and cost individual investors roughly 3 trillion won (about $2.2 billion)—alongside windfall tax proposals on banks and energy firms that unsettled foreign capital.
  • Spillover into households: With foreign ownership in South Korea’s five largest companies by market capitalization falling by more than 15%, the downturn has hit the nation’s 14 million retail investors and coincided with a 1.2% quarterly drop in second-quarter household spending and a more than 40% decline in Seoul apartment transactions between July and September.
  • Rhetorical backlash: Lee, of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, drew sharp criticism after telling a capital markets forum that "the higher the mountain, the deeper the valley," a remark detractors viewed as brushing aside structural issues such as short-selling rules and wide ±30% daily price limits.

Policy Friction Over Global Tailwinds

The core argument presented in the analysis is that industry structure alone cannot explain Seoul’s underperformance. Both South Korea and Taiwan rely heavily on semiconductors, which account for more than 20% of each market, yet Taiwan’s index posted 191% cumulative gains since late 2022 and held near its highs while South Korea gave back much of its 113% rally.

The commentary also links market anxiety to broader fiscal policy. South Korean economist Choi Bae-geun contends in the piece that channeling a 45 trillion won (about $32.5 billion) chip-driven tax revenue bump into consumption coupons and welfare handouts repeats approaches seen in earlier administrations—including those of Lee Myung-bak, Park Geun-hye, and Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—rather than tackling structural reform as fiscal deficits reach 32 trillion won (about $23 billion).

The Credibility Test Ahead

Seen from outside Korea, the dispute highlights the tension between populist political signaling and institutional predictability. Proposals like windfall taxes or headline-grabbing initiatives such as a "KOSPI 5000" special committee may play well domestically, yet global funds tend to reprice regulatory risk rapidly—especially in a market where ±30% daily trading bands and heavy short-selling already magnify swings. Restoring investor confidence will likely depend less on reassuring speeches and more on whether policymakers address market mechanics and policy consistency.
