The Myth of Reform: Why South Korea’s Prosecution Remains Untouchable

September 23, 2026

Prosecutorial reform in South Korea has become a perpetual, exhausting cycle of political theater rather than a genuine effort to fix a broken system.

  • What happened: Prominent progressive intellectual Rhyu Si-min has reignited the debate, criticizing both the ruling and opposition parties for weaponizing judicial processes for political survival.
  • The core issue: The South Korean prosecution holds a near-total monopoly on both investigative and indictment powers, creating a structure that resists checks and balances.
  • The impact: The public’s trust in the judiciary is at an all-time low, as high-profile investigations—most notably against Democratic Party leader Lee Jae-myung—are viewed by supporters as political persecution and by critics as standard law enforcement.
  • The takeaway: Reform is currently stalled because both sides of the aisle view the prosecution as a tool to either destroy their enemies or protect their own interests.

A Stalled Reform Agenda

The structural problem is that the prosecution acts as an unchecked cartel. Because prosecutors decide both who to investigate and who to charge, they hold immense power that shifts with every change in government. Previous attempts at reform—such as the creation of the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO)—have largely failed to curb this, instead becoming caught in inter-agency power struggles and political compromises.

Rhyu Si-min’s recent critique highlights a uncomfortable truth: even the politicians who campaign on “reform” are often eager to wield this power once they have it. The current discourse is less about decentralizing authority and more about a survival war between the People Power Party (the main conservative party) and the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition party). As long as judicial outcomes are treated as trophies in a partisan fight, the actual quality of justice for the average citizen remains a secondary concern.

The Trap of Partisan Justice

The investigations surrounding opposition leader Lee Jae-myung have become a litmus test for the state of South Korean democracy. While the prosecution maintains that they are conducting standard anti-corruption investigations, the aggressive nature of these probes—frequent raids and public disclosures of investigation details—has drawn intense scrutiny. Critics argue these tactics mimic the authoritarian policing of the past, deepening the divide between those who believe the law is being applied fairly and those who see it as a targeted strike against political rivals.

The irony is that the more the political class treats the judiciary as an extension of their power, the more they delegitimize the very institutions they seek to control. True reform requires moving beyond the current "us vs. them" framework. Until South Korean society demands procedural legitimacy—where investigations are transparent and shielded from political timing—the push for reform will remain a hollow slogan, leaving the prosecution's power untouched and the public increasingly cynical.
