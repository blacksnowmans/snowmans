South Korea's Personnel Crisis: A Government in Retreat

September 21, 2026

  • What happened: Two key cabinet nominees under President Lee Jae-myung—Yong Hye-in (Ministry of Gender Equality and Family) and Kim Seung-won (Ministry of Justice)—have resigned in quick succession amid intense public backlash.
  • The controversy: Yong faced intense scrutiny over holding both a parliamentary seat and a cabinet post simultaneously, while Kim was hit by a series of scandals involving past behavior and ethical questions.
  • The result: The resignations leave the administration's personnel policy in tatters, highlighting a deep disconnect between the Blue House (the presidential office) and the ruling Democratic Party.

The South Korean political landscape is reeling from a self-inflicted personnel crisis. In mid-September 2026, the administration’s attempt to fill two critical cabinet spots collapsed under the weight of poor vetting and shifting public sentiment. Instead of decisive leadership, we are witnessing a recurring "resignation theater" where nominees hang on until the political cost becomes unbearable, only to step down in a manner that leaves the government looking disorganized and defensive.

The Failure of "Fitting People to Seats"

The core issue is the administration’s penchant for "fitting people to seats" rather than selecting the best candidate for the mission. Yong Hye-in, a lawmaker from the minor progressive Basic Income Party, was tapped for her symbolic value as a young, female leader. However, the attempt to have her serve as both a minister and a sitting member of the National Assembly—South Korea's parliament—sparked immediate public outcry. By the time she resigned, the administration had already burned through precious political capital.

Similarly, Kim Seung-won’s downfall as a Justice Minister nominee was fueled by a "scandal-a-day" cycle—ranging from past drunk driving to ethical questions regarding lobbying for pharmaceutical clinical trials. When the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal party—pressured these nominees to quit to protect its own polling numbers, it exposed a structural rift. While the Blue House wanted to power through, the party had to act as a shield against public anger, revealing a dysfunctional relationship between the president and his political base.

A Distrust in the System

The administration’s strategy of allowing nominees to "voluntarily resign" is a calculated but failing tactic. It is intended to shield the president from direct responsibility, but it instead paints a picture of a government that lacks a coherent vetting process. For the public, this is not a sign of accountability; it is a sign of incompetence.

Furthermore, the media has played a significant role, framing these departures as the result of specific press conferences or individual stubbornness. While the nominees were certainly defensive, the media's focus on "gotcha" journalism has polarized the debate, masking the deeper issue: the lack of clear policy goals behind these appointments. Unless the Lee administration reforms how it recruits and vets its leaders, this cycle of scandal will continue to paralyze its reform agenda, leaving the public to wonder if anyone is truly at the helm.
