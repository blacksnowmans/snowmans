South Korea's Ruling Party Fractures as President Lee Faces New Factional Infighting

October 13, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae are locked in a bitter political and budgetary dispute, exposing deep fractures within the ruling Democratic Party.
  • The core conflict: A new wave of hardline, online-based supporters—dubbed the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction—has launched aggressive attacks and spread false claims accusing Governor Choo of slashing welfare and childcare budgets.
  • Who is affected: The Democratic Party is suffering severe internal division, undermining its reform agenda and alienating moderate voters as personal grievances and factional loyalty overshadow policy debates.
  • Broader stakes: Critics warn that the toxic online discourse and factional purges within the progressive camp risk reversing decades of democratic progress and playing into the hands of political opponents.

Factional Warfare and Fake News Threaten the Democratic Party

South Korean politics is rarely just about policy; it is a high-stakes ecosystem of shifting alliances and loyalties. The escalating feud between President Lee Jae-myung—head of the liberal Democratic Party—and Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae reveals a ruling camp at war with itself. What appears on the surface to be a disagreement over local budgets is actually a proxy war driven by newly emerged, highly vocal online factions and deep-seated political complexes.

The trouble stems from a shift in the party's grassroots support base. As older supporter networks fractured, a new, aggressive online community rallied around the president, often resorting to personal insults and vitriol against anyone seen as disloyal. When President Lee himself amplified a harsh attack post targeting critics, it blew the public discourse wide open. Rather than engaging in rational debate, these hardline factions have targeted Governor Choo—a veteran progressive heavy-hitter—with baseless accusations that she cut essential livelihood and childcare budgets. In reality, Choo successfully protected crucial welfare funds by slashing unnecessary event spending and passing a supplementary budget worth about $260 million (354.3 billion won) to cover shortfalls left by her predecessor.

Personal Grievances Overshadow Reform and Judicial Independence

Observers note that President Lee's friction with Governor Choo may also stem from a long-standing power dynamic within the party. Long before Lee reached the presidency, Choo was a towering figure in progressive politics, and her continued independence—including outspoken criticism of judicial and prosecutorial appointments—appears to chafe the current presidential office. This personal friction has trickled down to the grassroots, where internal critics and minor allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party's lawmaker Park Eun-jung are unfairly branded as traitors for demanding transparency over past investigative hurdles, such as blocked forensic examinations during the administration of jailed former leader Yoon Seok-yeol.

The stakes extend far beyond internal party bickering. As speculation simores over potential judicial moves to reopen legal cases against President Lee, some radical supporters have cheered the prospect out of short-term partisan spite. Analysts warn that treating the judiciary as a political weapon for immediate gratification is a dangerous gamble that could set the democratic movement back decades. To survive and govern effectively, the Democratic Party must step back from populist outrage politics, stop the witch hunts against internal dissenters, and return to the principles of procedural democracy and common-sense governance championed by former presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in.
