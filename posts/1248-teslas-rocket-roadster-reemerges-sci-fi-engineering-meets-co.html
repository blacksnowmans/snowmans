Tesla’s Rocket Roadster Reemerges: Sci-Fi Engineering Meets Cold Reality

October 17, 2026

Tesla has unveiled astonishing demonstrations of its long-delayed second-generation Roadster, showing off SpaceX cold gas thrusters and ground-effect fans that theoretically launch the supercar from 0 to 60 mph in a blistering 0.81 seconds.

  • The Tech: The experimental vehicle integrates cold gas thrusters fed by a 10,000-psi composite carbon-wrapped pressure vessel (COPV) and an underbody vacuum fan system generating downforce equal to twice the car’s weight—enough to cling upside down to an overhead ramp.
  • The Reality: Production is now slated for 2028—nine years after its initial 2017 reveal and eight years past its original 2020 delivery target—with planned production capped at just 2,000 units annually.
  • The Cost to Buyers: An estimated $250 million (roughly 330 billion won) in reservation deposits worldwide—including 55 million won (about $40,000) per reservation in South Korea—remains held without interest or escrow protections.
  • The Roadblock: Aviation-grade gas expulsion, extreme G-forces, and fan debris propulsion make street-legal approval from agencies like the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) or South Korea’s Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport virtually impossible in its current form.

SpaceX Rockets on Four Wheels

By transplanting the Falcon 9 rocket’s attitude-control mechanics into a passenger chassis, Tesla has essentially broken past the physical limits of tire friction. Standard supercars cannot push past the mechanical traction limit of roughly 3.4G, but roughly ten compressed-gas nozzles eliminate reliance on road grip by pushing the vehicle directly. Meanwhile, reviving the banned 1978 Brabham BT46B Formula One "fan car" concept gives the vehicle stationary suction, pulling out floor-pan air to use the atmosphere’s natural pressure like a massive aerodynamic clamp.

Yet this sheer technological bravado masks severe engineering liabilities. Storing nitrogen at 10,000 psi—equivalent to the pressure 7,000 meters beneath the ocean—carries inherent blast risks, echoing the 2016 Falcon 9 launchpad anomaly involving COPV canisters. Furthermore, discharging pressurized thrust near urban curbs poses direct blast hazards to bystanders, leaving even Elon Musk admitting that the extreme G-force thrills might be hazardous for drivers with health conditions.

High-Tech Stunt or Regulatory Non-Starter?

For reservation holders who put down substantial sums nearly a decade ago, these spectacles taste bitter. Original promises of a 1,000-kilometer range and four-passenger seating have quietly shrunk toward 480 kilometers and a two-seat configuration, all without independent third-party verification of the publicized figures. While design publications marvel at the absurdity of the machine, practical observers see a recurring playbook: reviving the Roadster to generate brand buzz and project technical supremacy while prioritizing autonomy and robotics projects like Cybercab and Optimus.

Ultimately, a vehicle that vents high-pressure gas and behaves like a missile will find few regulators willing to sign off on municipal license plates. In Seoul, regulators under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung are focused on combining pragmatic innovation with rigorous safety baselines—a stark departure from the uncritical deregulation and vanity gestures seen during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. Without clearing rigorous road safety certifications, Tesla's rocket-boosted machine will remain confined to private tracks and collector garages: an undeniably brilliant engineering stunt, but an undeliverable road car.
