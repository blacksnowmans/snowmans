Nvidia's Geopolitical Squeeze Catches South Korean Investors in the Crossfire

November 28, 2026

Nvidia faces escalating regulatory pressure in Washington after its cutting-edge Blackwell chips were discovered operating inside a Shanghai data center, triggering export halts to Southeast Asia and leaving aggressive South Korean retail buyers nursing billions in paper losses.

  • What happened: Unlawful deployments of Blackwell hardware in China prompted the US government to freeze Nvidia server shipments to four Southeast Asian nations, alongside revelations that Chinese entities secretly owned Silicon Valley front companies purchasing advanced hardware.
  • The market blowback: South Korean retail investors—known locally as Seohak Gaemi, or "Western Ants"—poured roughly $600 million (818.7 billion won) into Nvidia during recent price dips, yet their total holdings saw estimated losses reach nearly $4.4 billion (6 trillion won) due to deep technological corrections.
  • Shifting tech alliances: Big Tech firms such as Amazon are expediting custom silicon to bypass Nvidia altogether, while Japan's Kioxia is testing high-performance SSD architectures with Nvidia that could eventually substitute parts of the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) supply chain currently dominated by South Korean manufacturers.
  • Domestic policy tensions: As chip-related technology leaks surge inside South Korea, industry giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are pressing lawmakers to grant exemptions from the country's statutory 52-hour workweek to maintain pace in the global AI race.

Sanctions Leaks and the Retail Hangover

Washington’s containment strategy around advanced artificial intelligence hardware is facing severe friction. The discovery of Nvidia’s flagship Blackwell systems within Chinese infrastructure demonstrates the limitations of current export controls and guarantees intense scrutiny during upcoming US Senate hearings. Although Moody’s reaffirmed Nvidia’s credit rating following CEO Jensen Huang’s active re-engagement, the prospect of tighter controls across transshipment hubs in Southeast Asia creates immediate headwind for global server shipments.

This turbulence has battered South Korean retail traders who treated recent pullbacks as a buying opportunity. Despite pouring substantial capital into the dip, many portfolios remain deeply underwater after purchasing at earlier peaks. In response to extreme single-stock swings, the Korea Exchange is rolling out three new diversified AI semiconductor ETFs to provide local investors with broader industry exposure.

Memory Dominance and Broader Structural Risks

The geopolitical rift is accelerating technological workarounds that pose direct challenges to South Korea’s memory champions. Kioxia’s push to test petabyte-scale SSD memory expansion alongside Nvidia signals that the industry is exploring alternatives to costly HBM architectures, threatening a core profit driver for South Korean producers. Meanwhile, domestic industry analysts caution that heavy macroeconomic concentration on chips alone risks cannibalizing other sectors, potentially dragging South Korea’s economic expansion down toward 2.1% by 2028 if export routes and technological moats narrow.
