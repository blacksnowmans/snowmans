South Korea's Ruling Party Grapples with Falling Ratings and Internal Fractures

September 10, 2026

South Korea's liberal administration is facing a sharp political reckoning, marked by sliding approval ratings, bitter infighting among core supporters, and mounting pressure to reform both its policy approach and its public posture.

    Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party) and the presidential administration of President Lee Jae-myung.
  • What happened: In recent parliamentary speeches and media appearances, senior ruling party figures and presidential officials candidly addressed a confluence of crises, including plummeting poll numbers, economic policy missteps, and toxic internal divisions.
  • What it changes: The rare public admissions highlight a growing vulnerability for the government, forcing party leadership to pivot away from grand rhetoric toward tangible reforms and self-reflection.

The Ruling Party's Dual Fronts: Policy Ambitions and Plummeting Polls

Recent high-profile appearances on a popular political broadcast shed light on the administration's deep-seated vulnerabilities roughly eight months into its term. Kim Min-seok, a senior figure in the Democratic Party, used a parliamentary negotiation group speech to argue that South Korea must move beyond its industrialization and democratization phases to build a "dignified society." He championed the concept of a "basic society"—a framework intended to guarantee minimum standards of human dignity and welfare rather than just incremental budget boosts—while also calling for a pragmatic approach to constitutional reform and a more independent foreign policy that steps away from relying solely on the United States amid US-China tensions.

Yet, those grand visions clash sharply with the gritty reality on the ground. Hong Ik-pyo, the presidential senior secretary for political affairs, addressed the administration's sliding approval ratings head-on. He attributed the decline to a toxic mix of political friction, economic policy missteps—specifically pointing to confusion surrounding real estate tax policies and stock market regulations—and a tone-deaf governing style that alienated the public. Hong also condemned the vicious name-calling and internal purging habits festering within the liberal camp, warning that mutual backstabbing and extreme rhetoric by vocal supporters online are actively undermining the government.

An Exercise in Damage Control or a Genuine Turning Point?

What makes these admissions striking is their unusual candor. Rather than deflecting blame or projecting absolute confidence, senior members of South Korea's ruling establishment are publicly diagnosing their own failures. The acknowledgment that economic fumbles and an arrogant "tone and manner" drove voters away suggests that the presidential office recognizes the severity of the public's warning signals.

However, diagnosing the disease does not guarantee the right medicine. While figures like Kim Min-seok propose innovative ideas—such as randomly mixing parliamentary seating arrangements by alphabetical order to break up partisan hostility—the administration still struggles to bridge the gap between lofty legislative goals and everyday public anxiety over the economy. If the ruling bloc continues to allow internal factionalism and aggressive partisan loyalists to dictate its culture, the idealistic talk of a "dignified society" will quickly fade into political irrelevance. Power is fleeting, and voter patience in South Korea's hyper-engaged electorate is wearing thin.
