South Korean Fathers Say They Do Enough Childcare. Mothers Disagree.

November 16, 2026

Seven in ten South Korean fathers with young children believe they do their fair share of parenting, even as mothers remain saddled with nearly all routine daily care.

  • The time-versus-task divide: A recent survey found that half of fathers with children in elementary school or younger spend one to three hours a day on childcare, yet meal preparation, packing school supplies, and managing clothes remain overwhelmingly mothers' work.
  • High-needs families exposed: A broadcast report featuring a mother raising a child with a congenital disability—so overwhelmed she could not clear dishes piled up for a year—highlighted how heavily specialized care relies on personal sacrifice rather than public infrastructure.
  • Grassroots patches step in: Municipalities are expanding local support networks, including a cooperative care group of 120 families in Sacheon, one-on-one mentoring for multicultural mothers in Mungyeong, and daycare reuse flea markets donating about $360 to $720 (500,000 to 1 million won) to local charities.
  • Burnout in the daycare sector: With South Korea's falling birth rate forcing daycare closures and raising workloads for remaining staff, North Gyeongsang Province gathered roughly 1,500 childcare workers—alongside smaller county award ceremonies—to shore up flagging morale.

The illusion of shared parenting

On paper, South Korean men are more involved in parenting than previous generations, yet raw hours hide a lopsided division of labor. Because paternal time largely goes toward playtime and weekend outings, the repetitive chores that keep a household running still fall to women. Unless men shift from acting as occasional helpers to taking ownership of daily routines, domestic friction will persist—and for families facing severe challenges like childhood disability, that imbalance leaves primary caregivers dangerously isolated.

Community fixes cannot replace state support

Across the country, local governments, charities, and firms like South Korean tobacco and consumer goods giant KT&G are trying to soften parenting stress through neighborhood networks, family events, and support for employees with exam-age children. Daycare centers are even weaving themselves into local welfare systems by teaching children charity through community flea markets.

These initiatives build welcome neighborhood solidarity, especially for isolated parents of infants and toddlers. Yet community classes, mentoring networks, and appreciation awards for stressed daycare teachers are only Band-Aids. Without stronger public care infrastructure, better pay and working conditions for daycare staff, and real structural relief for high-needs households, South Korea's local safety nets will remain stretched thin.
