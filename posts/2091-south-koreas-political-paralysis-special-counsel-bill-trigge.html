South Korea’s Political Paralysis: Special Counsel Bill Triggers Opposition Walkout

November 07, 2026

South Korean politics has entered a period of intense confrontation following the passage of the "Fabricated Prosecution Special Counsel Bill" on November 6, which led the main opposition People Power Party (PPP)—the primary conservative political force—to abandon the National Assembly in favor of street protests.

  • What happened: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), currently holding the presidency under Lee Jae-myung, forced the passage of a bill intended to investigate alleged "fabricated prosecutions" by the judiciary.
  • Opposition reaction: The conservative People Power Party boycotted the vote, labeling the legislation a "shielding law" designed to protect the current administration from accountability.
  • Political shift: The PPP has declared a state of "street struggle," marking a total breakdown in parliamentary dialogue and signaling a long, volatile winter for domestic politics.

The Crisis of Parliamentary Politics

The passage of this bill is being framed in two diametrically opposed ways. The ruling DPK asserts that the special counsel investigation is a necessary tool to correct the excesses of "political prosecutors" who allegedly targeted them during the tenure of the now-imprisoned former leader, Yoon Seok-youl. Conversely, the opposition PPP claims the bill is an attempt to undermine the rule of law and neutralize the judicial process to serve the current administration’s political interests.

This is not merely a legislative dispute; it is a fundamental clash over the legitimacy of the legal system. As the PPP takes its grievances from the floor of the National Assembly to the streets of Gwanghwamun, the space for compromise is rapidly shrinking. When a country’s primary opposition party deems the legislature ineffective and chooses to protest outdoors, it suggests that the institutions designed to resolve conflict are failing to function.

A Path Toward Gridlock

The PPP’s move to push for the total cancellation of the "Future Response Fund"—a key government initiative—alongside their outdoor rallies, indicates they are moving toward an all-out, multi-front offensive against the Lee Jae-myung administration. By framing the current government as a threat to the rule of law, the PPP aims to consolidate its base and pressure the administration, but such tactics carry significant risks. For the average citizen, this constant state of "political warfare" means that critical national economic and social issues are once again being relegated to the background.

Ultimately, the events of November 6 reflect a deeply polarized political landscape where the "other side" is viewed as an enemy to be defeated rather than a partner for negotiation. Unless both parties return to the National Assembly and move past the performative nature of these public standoffs, the structural paralysis of South Korean governance is likely to persist through the end of the year.
