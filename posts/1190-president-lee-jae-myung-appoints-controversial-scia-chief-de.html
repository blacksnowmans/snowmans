President Lee Jae-myung Appoints Controversial SCIA Chief Despite Mixed Parliamentary Review

October 16, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung has officially appointed Kim Ji-yong as the first head of the Serious Crime Investigative Agency (SCIA), a new body central to his administration’s prosecution reform, despite a divided parliamentary report and public skepticism.

  • Political Gridlock: The National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee adopted a confirmation report listing Kim as both "qualified" and "unqualified," effectively passing the final decision—and the political risk—to the President.
  • A Question of Character: Kim, a former prosecutor, faced intense criticism during his hearing for "self-denial," renouncing his past involvement in prosecutorial abuses to align himself with the current government’s reform agenda.
  • Public Dissent: Only 24.8% of the public supports the appointment, while 35% oppose it, leaving the SCIA to begin its mission with a significant trust deficit.
  • Opposition Pivot: The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition, has seized on the moment not just to criticize Kim, but to attack the President’s "National Interest First" foreign policy as a threat to the U.S.-ROK alliance.

The Chameleon of Prosecution Reform

The appointment of Kim Ji-yong is a pragmatic, yet risky, move by the Lee Jae-myung administration to stabilize the SCIA just 13 days after its launch. The agency was created to strip the prosecution of its investigative powers, a cornerstone of the Democratic Party’s platform. However, Kim’s background is problematic. During his confirmation hearing, he was accused of being an opportunist who once participated in the very "targeted investigations" he now vows to abolish. Critics from both the Rebuilding Korea Party (a minor liberal party) and the PPP argue that a leader who erases his own past to suit the current power cannot be trusted to lead a neutral investigative body.

The PPP’s strategy has been notably two-faced. While they labeled Kim’s hearing a "tearful show of self-denial," they did not block the adoption of the committee report. This allowed them to avoid being blamed for obstructing a new agency while simultaneously framing the President’s personnel choices as failures. The President’s Office countered that the hearing proved Kim's qualifications, arguing that establishing the SCIA is more urgent than finding a "perfect" candidate.

Geopolitics and the "Omelet" Legacy

The political battle has spilled over into foreign policy. Jang Dong-hyeok, leader of the PPP, recently claimed the Lee administration is focused on "dismantling the U.S.-ROK alliance." This rhetoric appears to be a diversionary tactic to weaken the President’s domestic standing. President Lee has maintained that while the alliance is vital, Korea’s own national interests must come first—especially as the U.S. government under Donald Trump exerts economic pressure and leverages military issues, such as the transfer of Wartime Operational Control (OPCON), for its own gain.

Meanwhile, the appointment has revived discussions on the "collusion between power and the press" that characterized the previous era. Under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former president currently imprisoned after impeachment), the administration reportedly courted journalists with "omelet dinners" and a massive spike in overseas training budgets. One report by Newstapa, an independent investigative outlet, found that overseas training funds for journalists tripled in 2025, with individuals receiving about $29,000 (40 million KRW) for what often amounted to lackluster, "copy-paste" reports. This legacy of media bribery continues to haunt the current political landscape, as the public remains wary of any institution—investigative or journalistic—that seems too cozy with the blue-roofed halls of power.
