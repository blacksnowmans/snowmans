South Korea's Mental Health Taboo: Why People Visit Fortune Tellers Before Psychiatrists

September 10, 2026

  • What happened: Psychiatrist Dr. Choi Myung-ki appeared on a prominent YouTube discussion panel alongside health experts to dissect South Korea’s deeply ingrained social stigmas surrounding mental illness, drug therapy, and emotional burnout.
  • What it changes: It challenges the cultural view that psychological distress is a spiritual failure, framing mental health instead as a physical issue of neurotransmitter imbalance that responds reliably to modern medicine.
  • Who is affected: Millions of South Koreans suffering in silence, who frequently delay professional psychiatric care by turning instead to alcohol, folk shamans, and fortune tellers.

The Brain Is Part of the Body: Debunking Chemical Imbalance Stigma

In South Korea, physical ailments prompt immediate medical attention, yet emotional distress is routinely dismissed as a lack of willpower, a spiritual crisis, or bad luck. During the broadcast, Dr. Choi Myung-ki, head of the Cheongdam Harvard Psychology Center, bluntly dismantled this false divide. He explained that mental health is quite simply brain health—an interconnected system where the mind and body function as a single biological unit. When the body breaks down, chemical signals fail; when the brain exhausts itself, physical agony follows.

A major barrier to recovery is the widespread, unfounded fear of psychiatric medication. Many patients worry about permanent brain control or chemical dependency. Yet, Dr. Choi noted that most modern psychiatric drugs boast high success rates within a month, and even psychiatrists themselves take daily medications to manage their own brain chemistry—a reality he illustrated by openly sharing his own prescription regimen of bupropion, desvenlafaxine, and escitalopram. Depression, he stressed, is not a failure of character, but a temporary neurological slump with a predictable statistical lifecycle. Holding on through the darkest weeks often brings natural relief, making early medical intervention essential rather than something to be ashamed of.

Fortune Tellers Outrank Doctors in High-Tech South Korea

Despite South Korea's world-class medical infrastructure, the social stigma attached to psychiatric clinics remains uniquely punishing. According to healthcare statistics and expert testimony, before an ordinary South Korean finally visits a psychiatrist, they typically cycle through three alternative coping mechanisms: fortune tellers, drinking establishments, and religious leaders. When anxiety flares, citizens lean heavily on folk shamans and alcohol to numb the pressure, often exhausting their finances and energy before reluctantly seeking clinical help as an absolute last resort.

This reliance on superstition and avoidance stems from a culture that views psychological distress through a moral or superstitious lens. Treating mental health as a personal failing rather than a biological reality keeps people trapped in unnecessary suffering. When citizens finally bypass the fortune tellers and embrace evidence-based psychiatric care, they discover a fast, economical path to recovery. Breaking the social wall around mental health requires society to normalize vulnerability, treating emotional burnout with the same matter-of-fact urgency as a common cold.
