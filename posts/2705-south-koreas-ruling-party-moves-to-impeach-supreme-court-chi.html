South Korea's Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae

November 24, 2026

Lawmakers from South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea are finalizing an impeachment motion against Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae over alleged procedural abuses and trial delays, according to statements from party figures cited in a report by Korean political blog Blacksnowman.

  • The impeachment push: Democratic Party lawmakers—including Yang Bu-nam, Park Ju-min, and Noh Jong-myeon—say they plan to complete the motion this week and aim for a vote in the National Assembly, South Korea's parliament, before the end of the year.
  • Procedural focus: According to the party's impeachment task force, the motion deliberately avoids challenging the substance of court verdicts to respect judicial independence, focusing instead on alleged violations of trial-duration rules (known in Korea as the "633 rule") and improper scheduling of hearings and sentencing dates.
  • Internal court friction: Dissent has also emerged within the judiciary itself, as a presiding judge with 25 years of experience sparked debate on the courts' internal network by criticizing a ruling involving Han Duck-soo and alleging that parts of the decision selectively cleared charges.
  • Warnings of disruption: Some critics and conservative media outlets warn that impeaching the Chief Justice could disrupt governance and stall Supreme Court proceedings, noting that two of the 11 justices were classmates of Yoon Suk-yeol at South Korea's judicial training institute and might recuse themselves—a claim Democratic Party lawmakers dismiss as a pretext to delay Yoon Suk-yeol's insurrection trial.

A Clash Over Judicial Accountability

The escalating confrontation underscores deep divisions over how South Korea's courts have handled politically sensitive cases. Supporters of the impeachment drive, including Democratic Party lawmakers and the Blacksnowman blog commentary, argue that public trust in the judiciary has eroded due to perceived bias and delays in insurrection-related trials stemming from Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office. From this perspective, removing Chief Justice Jo is framed as a necessary check on judicial misconduct rather than a partisan attack.

Conversely, conservative media and critics of the move contend that pursuing impeachment risks undermining the independence of the courts and paralyzing Supreme Court operations. They warn that leadership turmoil and potential recusals could bog down high-profile appeals. Democratic Party lawmakers counter that similar classmate ties did not halt past major proceedings, such as President Lee Jae-myung's election law violation case, suggesting that fears of institutional paralysis are overstated.

In analytical terms, the ruling party's decision to base its motion strictly on trial timelines rather than verdict outcomes reflects a careful legal strategy. Attacking judicial rulings directly would invite sharper criticism over violating the constitutional separation of powers, whereas focusing on administrative delays offers a narrower target. Still, whether the National Assembly passes the motion by year-end will test both the Democratic Party's legal arguments and the judiciary's ability to defend its credibility amid intense political scrutiny.
