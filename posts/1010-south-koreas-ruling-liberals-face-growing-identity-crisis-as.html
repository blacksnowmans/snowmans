South Korea's Ruling Liberals Face Growing Identity Crisis as President Lee Invokes Roh Moo-hyun

October 12, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung is facing mounting backlash from within his own progressive base for invoking the legacy of the late President Roh Moo-hyun to justify combative social media conduct and controversial policy retreats.

  • Weaponizing legacy: President Lee has frequently cited former President Roh on social media, framing his contentious online engagement and blocking of critics—whom he likened to people throwing "filth" into a private yard—as Roh-style direct democracy.
  • Internal "cuckoo" revolt: Mainstream loyalists of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) accuse Lee's faction of acting like "cuckoos" hijacking the party's nest, abandoning the democratic principles established by Roh and former President Moon Jae-in.
  • Prosecutorial reform retreat: Disillusionment has deepened over Justice Minister Yun Ho-jung's order to inspect whistleblower prosecutor Im Eun-jung and the administration's continued reliance on career prosecutors for key posts, breaking pledges to dismantle prosecutorial overreach.
  • Voter erosion: Despite holding a commanding 160-seat parliamentary majority under DPK leader Kim Min-seok, stagnant approval ratings are driving frustrated progressives toward third parties such as the minor liberal Rebuilding Korea Party.

A Shield of Past Icons to Mask Growing Fractures

President Lee’s defense of his aggressive social media presence—arguing that "bad money drives out good" and that abusive comments must be scrubbed to keep public discourse clean—has backfired among traditional liberals. Rather than projecting the humility of the late Roh Moo-hyun, who embraced unfiltered language to challenge elitist authority, Lee’s habit of personal blocking and dismissing substantive policy criticism as toxic waste suggests a leader hypersensitive to dissent. To traditional party stalwarts, borrowing Roh’s memory whenever the administration stumbles feels less like genuine homage and more like a political shield against sagging poll numbers.

The rift exposes deep factional animosity within the ruling DPK. Factions loyal to Roh and Moon increasingly view the dominant "New Lee" leadership as an alien force prioritizing self-preservation over core progressive values. The administration's perceived hypocrisy—epitomized by its shifting rhetoric around ethical controversies, reminiscent of rationalizations offered during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office—has driven young voters away and left the broader liberal coalition fractured.

Compromised Reforms and Parliamentary Paralysis

The deepest crisis of trust stems from the administration's faltering pledge to overhaul South Korea’s powerful prosecution service. The DPK long campaigned on stripping prosecutors of investigative powers and establishing an independent Serious Crimes Investigation Bureau. Instead, Justice Minister Yun Ho-jung is placing career prosecutors back into ministry posts and targeting internal reformist figures like Im Eun-jung. Meanwhile, controversial appointments, such as Kim Ji-yong to head the new investigation bureau, have fueled suspicions that the administration is merely co-opting prosecutorial leverage for its own political survival.

With an overwhelming 160 seats in the National Assembly, the DPK cannot blame opposition obstruction for its policy drift. The party's passivity under Kim Min-seok's leadership has hollowed out its reputation as a champion of institutional accountability. Unless the administration abandons selective justice and engages candidly with public dissatisfaction, the ruling party risks discovering that a massive legislative majority is meaningless once the electorate concludes it has lost its soul.
