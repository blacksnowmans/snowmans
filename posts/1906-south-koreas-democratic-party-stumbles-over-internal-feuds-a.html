South Korea’s Democratic Party Stumbles Over Internal Feuds and Cult Allegations

November 03, 2026

Internal factional battles and unfounded allegations of fringe cult infiltration are damaging South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea, eroding core supporter trust and stalling President Lee Jae-myung's political momentum.

  • What happened: Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) leadership under party leader Kim Min-seok pushed baseless claims that the Shincheonji religious group secretly infiltrated party ranks, using the narrative as a weapon against internal political rivals.
  • What it changes: The party’s strategy to pivot toward moderate voters while sidelining core reform values has backfired, leaving President Lee Jae-myung with stagnant approval ratings despite his presidential victory following Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's impeachment.
  • Who is affected: DPK rank-and-file members feel alienated by internal witch-hunts, while prospective merger talks with Cho Kuk's allied Rebuilding Korea Party have ground to a halt over leadership self-preservation.

A Self-Inflicted Crisis Over Cult Infiltration Claims

The push by DPK leadership to investigate alleged infiltration by Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious movement in South Korea—has turned into a costly political blunder. Statistical estimates show that even if members of the 350,000-strong group joined the 1.5 million-strong DPK, their actual voting impact in internal party elections would sit below 0.5 percent. Despite a lack of concrete evidence, party leadership established task forces and threatened law enforcement referrals, effectively using cult accusations as a proxy battle to purge non-mainstream factions and protect their own influence ahead of upcoming candidate nominations.

This internal friction comes alongside a failed push for "structural reconstruction." By chasing moderate voters and softening core reform pledges on media and prosecutorial oversight, the party aimed to build a broader coalition. Instead, it alienated its passionate base without winning over swing voters. President Lee Jae-myung's stagnant public support reflects a growing voter disillusionment with tactical political maneuvering over substantive reform.

Stalled Mergers and Lingering Judicial Frustrations

The internal infighting has directly sabotaged broader unification efforts within South Korea's progressive camp. Proposed merger talks between the ruling DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive ally led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, have broken down amid mounting distrust. Insiders suggest DPK leadership fears that integrating Cho’s party would disrupt the current executive board's power balance. In response, Rebuilding Korea Party leadership publicly rejected serving as a distraction for the DPK’s internal turmoil, leaving the progressive alliance divided.

Underlying these political games is persistent anger toward the judiciary among progressive voters. Critics argue that conservative networks established during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency remain entrenched in the court system, pointing to recent controversial rulings regarding military figures involved in the Dec. 3 martial law incident. For the ruling party to regain public trust, it must abandon internal factional witch-hunts and refocus on unity and institutional reform.
