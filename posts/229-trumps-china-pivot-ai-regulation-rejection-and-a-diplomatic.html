Trump's China Pivot, AI Regulation Rejection, and a Diplomatic Fallout with Ukraine

September 29, 2026

South Korea faces a volatile mix of shifting US foreign policy, a busted diplomatic pact with Kyiv, and Donald Trump's refusal to slow down the artificial intelligence race.

    U.S. Foreign Policy Shift: Donald Trump's recent diplomatic moves toward China, driven by domestic inflation concerns and a push to lower oil prices, treat traditional alliances as transactional bargaining chips. Seoul-Kyiv Diplomatic Clash: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy publicly announced the repatriation of North Korean prisoners of war to South Korea at the UN General Assembly, violating a bilateral confidentiality agreement and triggering a strong rebuke from the South Korean presidential office (the Blue House). AI Regulation Rejection: Trump rejected international oversight and speed limits for artificial intelligence during talks with AI firm Anthropic, raising global concerns over tech monopolies and U.S. control of potential "kill switches." Strategic Implications for Seoul: South Korea must abandon passive reliance on traditional allies and establish its own independent diplomatic and regulatory standards to avoid being marginalized in great power conflicts.

The convergence of transactional US leadership and sudden diplomatic breaches signals the end of comfortable assumptions in Seoul. When long-standing allies view security frameworks purely through the lens of domestic commodity prices and political leverage, middle powers like South Korea can no longer rely on shared values alone. The bitter fallout with Kyiv over the repatriation of North Korean prisoners demonstrates how quickly sensitive security cooperation can be upended by a partner's unilateral political messaging.

At the same time, the high-stakes battle over artificial intelligence governance highlights the dangers of technological dependency. As Washington dismisses global oversight frameworks to maintain its competitive edge, the prospect of a dominant superpower monopolizing emergency control mechanisms over critical systems creates a profound vulnerability for nations on the outside. Rather than reacting with alarm, Seoul should use this moment of uncertainty to assert its own regulatory standards and independent foreign policy, ensuring national interests dictate strategy rather than the shifting whims of global superpowers.
