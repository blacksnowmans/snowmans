Poll: Majority of South Koreans Back Progressive Pundit Rhyu Si-min’s Criticism of President Lee Jae-myung

November 06, 2026

A majority of South Koreans view prominent liberal intellectual Rhyu Si-min’s recent criticism of President Lee Jae-myung as warranted, exposing a deep factional rift within the country's ruling progressive bloc.

  • The numbers: A survey conducted by Korea Information Research found that 55.2% of respondents considered Rhyu's sharp critique of the administration appropriate, compared to 36.4% who deemed it inappropriate.
  • The progressive split: Supporters of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea fiercely rejected Rhyu's remarks, with 71.3% calling them improper, whereas 94.0% of supporters of the aligned, minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party backed Rhyu.
  • Broad demographic backing: Approval of Rhyu's critique cleared 50% across every age bracket—reaching 59.6% among voters in their twenties—and led in nearly all regions except the traditional liberal stronghold of Honam (49.8% against versus 45.2% in favor).
  • The partisan backlash: Hardline supporters of the Democratic Party have accused Rhyu of betrayal and "friendly fire," attacking his personal motives rather than addressing the substance of his policy critiques.

A Deepening Schism on the Left

Rhyu Si-min is not an ordinary commentator; he is a veteran progressive intellectual and former cabinet minister whose voice carries immense authority among liberal voters. When a figure of his stature openly challenges President Lee Jae-myung, it is impossible for the ruling camp to dismiss it as a standard conservative attack. Yet the survey data reveals a striking divergence: while the broader public and allied progressives welcome a critical check on power, the core loyalist base of the Democratic Party has circled the wagons, viewing any public dissent from within as an existential threat.

This dynamic highlights the growing friction between two camps on the South Korean left. On one side are party loyalists who demand absolute unity behind President Lee, wary of any vulnerability following the chaotic political turmoil that led to the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. On the other are reform-minded progressives, typified by Rebuilding Korea Party voters, who believe that uncritical loyalty blinds the government to early governance errors and alienates moderate voters.

The Peril of Silencing Internal Dissent

The immediate reaction from Democratic Party hardliners—labeling Rhyu an attention-seeker driven by jealousy rather than debating his points—reveals a troubling decline in democratic discourse. When a political movement begins policing motives instead of engaging with logic, it risks shutting down the very feedback loops required for competent governance. Rhyu's supporters argue that enduring short-term discomfort from "bitter medicine" is preferable to letting blind allegiance drive the administration into arrogance.

With public approval for Rhyu’s critique crossing 55% nationwide and standing strong among younger generations, the ruling party faces an uncomfortable reality. Treating internal criticism as apostasy may preserve internal party discipline, but it threatens to detach the administration from the wider public whose trust it needs to maintain.
