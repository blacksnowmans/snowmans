South Korea’s Ruling Party Pledges to Close the "Meal Gap" in Daycare Centers

November 21, 2026

Kim Min-seok, the leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, has committed to incorporating increased meal and snack subsidies for daycare centers into the national budget to resolve long-standing funding disparities between early childhood institutions.

  • Budget Commitment: During a visit to a daycare center on November 20, Kim Min-seok inspected kitchen facilities and promised that the ruling party would push for higher meal unit prices to combat the impact of high food inflation.
  • The "Snack Gap": Childcare advocacy groups held nationwide rallies to protest the funding divide between daycares (managed by local governments) and kindergartens (managed by education offices), which results in different meal qualities for children of the same age.
  • Infrastructure Crisis: Local councilors are calling for emergency funding to remove hazardous asbestos from aging facilities and to replace failing CCTV systems, which are legally mandated but currently lack maintenance subsidies.
  • Public Expansion: The government is highlighting "Public-type Daycares"—private centers that receive state subsidies to operate at public standards—as a primary model to stabilize childcare quality in the private sector.

Administrative Red Tape and the Cost of Lunch

The current friction in South Korea’s childcare sector stems from a structural divide. Daycare centers (eorinijip), which typically care for infants and toddlers, fall under the jurisdiction of local governments and the Ministry of Health and Welfare. In contrast, kindergartens (yuchiwon) are overseen by regional offices of education. Because these two systems draw from different pots of money, the daily budget for a child’s lunch or snack can vary wildly depending on which institution they attend.

Kim Min-seok’s visit to the front lines of childcare signals that the Democratic Party is treating meal parity as a high-priority budget item. With food prices rising sharply, daycare directors argue that the current fixed subsidies make it nearly impossible to provide nutritionally balanced meals. For the ruling party, addressing this "tray discrimination" is a strategic move to appeal to young parents who are increasingly frustrated by the inconsistent quality of state-supported childcare.

Safety Hazards and the Burden of Compliance

Beyond the kitchen, more serious threats are emerging in the form of aging infrastructure. In cities like Cheonan, local lawmakers are warning that asbestos—a known carcinogen—remains in many centers because the budget for its removal has been repeatedly sidelined. This is not merely a maintenance issue but a fundamental safety failure that puts vulnerable children at risk. The government’s delay in funding these removals has left many parents in a state of chronic anxiety regarding the air quality in their children’s classrooms.

A similar crisis is unfolding with security technology. While South Korean law strictly mandates CCTVs in all daycares to prevent child abuse, there is no standardized support for replacing units that break down due to age. This creates a legal trap: daycare directors can face administrative penalties if their cameras fail, even if they lack the funds to buy new ones. As the government moves toward a total integration of the daycare and kindergarten systems, standardizing these safety and nutrition "floors" will be the true test of whether the policy is about child welfare or mere administrative reshuffling.
