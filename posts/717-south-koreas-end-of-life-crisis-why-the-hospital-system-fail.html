South Korea’s End-of-Life Crisis: Why the Hospital System Fails the Dying

October 05, 2026

  • The core issue: South Korea's heavy reliance on major general hospitals for end-of-life care often strips patients of dignity, trapping them in intensive care units (ICUs) on life support rather than providing comfort.
  • What triggers the crisis: When further curative treatments fail, doctors declare "no more care plans," forcing families into rushed, agonizing decisions under immense guilt.
  • Who is affected: Terminally ill patients and their families bear the brunt, facing emotional trauma, a shortage of hospice beds, and the physical and financial ruin of home-based care.

A recent panel discussion hosted by the Baeksae Committee—featuring medical professionals, a pharmacist, and Kyung Hee University medical anthropologist Kang Ji-yeon—has put a spotlight on how South Koreans face death. The consensus is stark: the country's medical culture treats dying as a technical failure to be fought with aggressive, mechanical interventions rather than a natural phase of life requiring human dignity and comfort.

In South Korea, major general hospitals are optimized for acute care. When cancer or other terminal illnesses progress to a point where doctors run out of treatment options, patients are routinely pushed out of the system. Families, gripped by the cultural burden of doing "everything possible" for their parents, frequently opt for ICU care. This usually results in patients spending their final days hooked up to machines in isolated rooms, robbed of a peaceful goodbye. Meanwhile, alternatives like hospices remain severely under-resourced, and home care places an unsustainable emotional and physical toll on family members.

Moving Toward Dignity and Better Policy

The discussion highlights an urgent need for cultural and systemic change in how South Korea approaches the end of life. Experts emphasize that hospice care—which actively manages pain using treatments like morphine rather than focusing solely on futile cures—provides a much-needed baseline of human dignity. However, social stigmas around palliative care and strong pain relievers continue to create barriers for patients seeking comfort.

Ultimately, experts argue that death must be reframed not as a medical defeat, but as a manageable part of life's trajectory. South Korea's current legal frameworks, such as the Life-Sustaining Treatment Decision Act, remain too narrow, and public infrastructure for palliative and home care is lacking. To prevent families from enduring preventable trauma and financial burnout, society must normalize open conversations about dying long before the final days arrive.
