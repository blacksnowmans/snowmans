South Korea Races to Curb AI Cheating Ahead of College Entrance Exam

November 18, 2026

Two days before South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam, the government of President Lee Jae-myung unveiled unprecedented anti-cheating measures—including a 10-year exam ban and a roughly $70,000 (100 million won) whistleblower bounty—to stop students from using artificial intelligence.

  • Harsh penalties and bounties: According to South Korean government announcements, President Lee called "grade theft" a serious crime, ordering a review of a 100 million won ($70,000) reward per tip and reduced penalties for accomplices who turn themselves in, while confirming a 10-year ban, admission cancellations, potential criminal charges, and post-exam investigations for cheaters.
  • Analog inspections and loud alarms: Prime Minister Han Seong-sook announced that proctors will inspect every test-taker’s eyeglasses for hidden smart cameras, place security stickers on clothing buttons, deploy radio detectors, and trigger an emergency disaster alert mid-exam to force hidden smartphones to ring even in silent mode.
  • Who is affected: All students sitting for the Suneung, South Korea’s annual college entrance exam, face heightened physical screening and potential mid-test disruptions, while exam proctors take on heavier enforcement duties.
  • Bipartisan legislative push: Despite fierce political clashes, the ruling Democratic Party and the main opposition People Power Party agreed to fast-track a special law codifying the 10-year ban and voiding university admissions tied to AI cheating.

Political fallout and tech pushback

The rollout quickly became a political flashpoint. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin drew intense criticism from lawmakers and the public after telling parliament he might not be able to catch all cheating because of his age. Seizing on the remark and a retracted suggestion from a ruling-party committee chair for students to monitor each other, the People Power Party threatened to seek the dismissal—and even impeachment—of both the prime minister and the education minister.

Critics also questioned whether analog tactics can outsmart modern hardware. Lawmaker Cheon Ha-ram warned that thermal cameras and radio detectors struggle against sophisticated AI devices, while opposition lawmaker Lee Jun-seok proposed a software alternative: a smartphone app he coded on a flight to the United Kingdom to help proctors spot abnormal radio signals without expensive equipment.

The broader read

According to critics and observers cited in Korean media coverage, the government’s scramble highlights a widening gap between fast-moving consumer AI tools and the state’s ability to police them. Relying on button stickers and mid-exam emergency alerts shifts the burden onto honest students and proctors, trading calm testing conditions for visible deterrence.

In South Korea, where the Suneung is widely viewed as a cornerstone of social fairness, any perceived vulnerability in exam security instantly becomes an existential political test. While the bipartisan special law signals rare agreement on raising legal stakes, lasting confidence will likely require technical solutions rather than emergency stopgaps.
