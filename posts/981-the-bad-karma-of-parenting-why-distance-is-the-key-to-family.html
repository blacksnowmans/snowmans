The "Bad Karma" of Parenting: Why Distance is the Key to Family Harmony

October 11, 2026

  • Boman, a prominent Buddhist monk, argues that parent-child relationships are inherently prone to "bad karma" due to intense emotional entanglement.
  • Excessive control and overprotection are often disguised as love, but they frequently function as emotional violence that strips children of their autonomy.
  • True healthy parenting requires "emotional distance" and mutual respect, replacing the obsession with control with genuine trust.
  • Counseling and legal experts emphasize that emotional abuse—including volatile parental arguments—can cause long-term psychological damage to children.

In a recent appearance on the talk show Intellectuals’ Invitation, the Buddhist monk Boman offered a provocative take on modern family dynamics. He suggested that the bond between parent and child is naturally fraught with difficulty. This is not because the relationship is inherently evil, but because the proximity and emotional baggage involved make it nearly impossible to treat one another with the objectivity required for a healthy connection.

Many parents mistakenly believe that their intervention in a child’s life is purely benevolent. However, Boman argues that phrases like "I did this for your own good" are often a form of emotional violence—a subconscious desire to mold a child into a version of the parent’s own making. Children raised under this pressure often grow up feeling like extensions of their parents, eventually losing their own voice and sense of self.

Beyond Control: The Necessity of Emotional Distance

The conflict often stems from a lack of "emotional space." When parents treat their children like personal property or trophies to be showcased to others, they strip the child of their identity. Boman compares this to two glass cups placed too close together; the slightest tremor causes them to chip or shatter. By intentionally creating a "healthy distance"—through polite language, respecting privacy, and avoiding the urge to micromanage—parents can actually preserve the relationship rather than destroy it.

This philosophy extends to how parents handle conflict. Instead of using threats or guilt-trips—which often lead to children becoming adept liars out of fear—parents should strive for open, honest communication. Boman notes that even in Buddhist tradition, the goal is not to enforce rigid rules for their own sake, but to understand the "karmic intent" or the heart behind an action. When a parent admits that their "best efforts" might have been misguided, it opens the door to genuine reconciliation.

Ultimately, Boman stresses that the most vital lesson for parents is to stop living through their children. Real authority does not come from fear or rigid control, but from being a reliable, independent role model. By loosening the "chains" of kinship and treating children as independent individuals, families can move away from toxic patterns and discover a more sustainable form of affection.
