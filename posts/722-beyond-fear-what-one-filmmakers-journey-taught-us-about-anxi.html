Beyond Fear: What One Filmmaker’s Journey Taught Us About Anxiety

September 11, 2026

  • Director Park Jung-mi's documentary, The Person in the Blanket, has sparked a debate about the risks of solo female travel and the psychological nature of fear.
  • The film argues that our perception of danger is often a "distorted lens" rather than a reflection of reality.
  • Instead of seeking total control, the director suggests that acknowledging both the world's risks and its underlying kindness is the key to living fully.

Director Park Jung-mi’s documentary, The Person in the Blanket, explores her experiences traveling alone. While many viewers expressed alarm in online comments—worrying that her journey might encourage others to take dangerous risks—Park argues that the film is not a travel guide. Rather, it is a deep dive into how human beings process fear. After experiencing a genuine threat in Iran, Park found that the memory of that one traumatic incident began to eclipse the thousands of instances of kindness she had received from strangers during her travels. This phenomenon, which psychologists attribute to the brain’s tendency to prioritize danger signals, highlights how easily we allow one negative experience to define our worldview.

The Paradox of Control

The urge to prepare for the future often curdles into an obsession with control. We believe that by accumulating more information or resources, we can eliminate uncertainty. However, as the film demonstrates, this is an impossible mission. In reality, the more we try to "control" our environment to feel safe, the more we expand our capacity for anxiety. It is only when we release that grip that we become capable of seeing the world as it actually is: a place where danger and kindness coexist. Statistics support this balanced view; while safety levels vary by region, travel experts often point to countries like Finland or Slovenia not because they are free of all crime, but because they foster high social trust and respect for boundaries.

Living in the Present

Park’s most profound realization was that she was suffering more from the "mental replay" of her traumatic experience than from the event itself. She spent her days dwelling on what could have happened or what might happen next, effectively living in a loop of imagined tragedy. By training herself to ask, "Is what I am afraid of happening right in front of me right now?" she was able to pull herself back to the present moment.

Ultimately, the film serves as a reminder that we don't need to be fearless to live a bold life. The goal is not to eradicate fear—which is an inherent human mechanism—but to stop letting it dictate our reality. By choosing to focus on the small, tangible moments of warmth and assistance that occur every day, we can learn to coexist with our fears rather than being imprisoned by them.
