South Korea’s Sweeping Prosecutor Overhaul Faces a Crucial Test

October 02, 2026

South Korea’s long-sought overhaul of its powerful prosecution service is hitting a wall of skepticism as the country prepares to split the agency into two separate bodies.

    What happened: South Korea is moving forward with dismantling the traditional prosecution office, replacing it with two new entities: an investigative agency (the Serious Crimes Investigation Office) and a prosecuting authority (the Prosecution Service). The controversy: President Yoon Suk Yeol’s former officials and loyalists are facing scrutiny over their past decisions, including Kim Ji-yong, a candidate for head of the new investigative agency, who previously handled politically sensitive cases. Who is affected: The entire South Korean legal system, which is attempting to decentralize decades of unchecked prosecutorial power without simply recreating the old system under a new name. The stakes: Public confidence remains low, with roughly 40% of citizens viewing the transition as mere political theater rather than a genuine democratic reform.

Can a New Bureaucracy Break Old Habits?

Decades in the making, the dismantling of South Korea's monolithic prosecution office traces its roots back to the liberal administration of former President Roh Moo-hyun. The goal has always been simple in theory: break up the absolute power of prosecutors who have historically controlled both the investigation and indictment of crimes. But execution is proving messy. Commentators and legal experts warn that simply splitting the office in two doesn't guarantee a healthy tension between the new agencies. If the key leadership roles remain staffed by conventional prosecutors carrying over their old habits and institutional loyalty, the reform is nothing more than a fresh coat of paint.

This tension puts intense pressure on the upcoming parliamentary confirmation hearings. Critics argue that lawmakers on both sides must move past partisan bickering to rigorously vet nominees like Kim Ji-yong, whose past record includes controversial decisions during politically charged investigations. True reform requires establishing a real check and balance, rather than letting the old guard build a new playground. For an outside observer, South Korea's struggle highlights a universal governance lesson: structural reorganizations mean little if the culture of the institution remains untouched.
