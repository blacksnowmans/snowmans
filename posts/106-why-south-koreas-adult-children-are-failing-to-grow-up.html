Why South Korea’s "Adult Children" are Failing to Grow Up

September 03, 2026

A growing trend of arrested development in South Korea—where adult children remain emotionally and financially tethered to their parents—is creating a cycle of mutual dependency that prevents both generations from maturing.

  • Financial codependency: Parents providing excessive financial support, such as buying cars or covering living expenses, strips children of the opportunity to develop self-reliance.
  • Emotional enmeshment: Parents acting as a "dumping ground" for their adult children's problems prevents those children from building their own coping mechanisms.
  • The "Grandparenting" trap: As grandparents take over child-rearing duties, they often undermine the authority of the actual parents, causing confusion and preventing young parents from taking responsibility.
  • Long-term impact: Research suggests that individuals who remain dependent on parents into their late 20s or 30s face higher rates of helplessness and lower self-esteem once that support system eventually disappears.

The High Cost of Over-Parenting

In South Korean culture, the line between "loving support" and "debilitating interference" is increasingly blurred. When parents rush to pay off debts or solve everyday crises for their adult children, they aren't just being generous; they are depriving their children of the "identity-forming" struggle that defines adulthood. This creates a false sense of security, leading young adults to believe that there is always a safety net. In the real world, this leaves them ill-equipped to handle failure, often resulting in severe social maladjustment later in life.

The solution requires a difficult, intentional shift in family dynamics. Parents must move from being "fixers" to "partners." This means setting firm emotional boundaries, refusing to be an infinite emotional crutch, and allowing children to experience the natural consequences of their own choices. True maturity—for both the parent and the child—begins only when the "parenting" stops and a relationship between two independent adults begins.

Redefining Family Independence

The current rise in "twilight parenting"—where grandparents raise their grandchildren—often exacerbates these issues by blurring generational lines of authority. When grandparents step into the primary caregiver role, they often exert influence over their own adult children's lives, preventing the younger generation from establishing their own home culture and personal authority.

Ultimately, holding onto a child as a "child" long after they have reached adulthood is a disservice to their future. The most loving act a parent can perform is to trust their child enough to let them navigate the world on their own. As uncomfortable as it may seem, letting go is not an act of rejection; it is the fundamental requirement for raising a functional, independent human being.
