Supreme Court Dissents Slam Acquittals in Kim Keon-hee Polling and Martial Law Cases

November 20, 2026

In two landmark en banc rulings, South Korean Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyung-mi issued pointed dissents accusing the court's majority of opening dangerous legal loopholes by clearing Kim Keon-hee over free campaign polling and overturning former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo's insurrection conviction.

  • The polling acquittal: A Supreme Court majority led by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae finalized a not-guilty ruling for Kim Keon-hee, the wife of Yoon Suk-yeol, finding that free opinion polls from political broker Myung Tae-kyun were not illegal political funds because there was no prior payment agreement or clear replacement of campaign expenses.
  • Oh's campaign-finance dissent: Joined by Justice Kim Seong-su, Oh argued that 12 customized polls worth tens of thousands of dollars—arranged from July to October 2021 after Kim asked Myung to fix a halted survey during the conservative People Power Party primary—were illegal "other benefits" under the Political Funds Act, even without an explicit cost-sharing deal.
  • Clash with lower-court convictions: The decision sharply diverges from an earlier Seoul Central District Court ruling, stemming from Special Counsel Min Joong-ki's probe into 58 polls worth about $200,000 (274.4 million won), which convicted Yoon Suk-yeol to two years in prison and Myung to 18 months over 14 polls worth roughly $20,000 (27.9 million won).
  • The martial law remand: On the same day, a nine-justice majority overturned and remanded Han Duck-soo's 15-year sentence for aiding an insurrection, ruling that constitutional subversion was not strictly proven regarding a cabinet meeting he convened right before an illegal martial law declaration—a split Oh, Shin Sook-hee, and Kim Seong-su condemned as an artificial excuse for impunity.

A green light for hidden campaign funding?

Legal commentary surrounding the rulings warns that the Supreme Court majority's strict requirement for a pre-existing payment deal leaves a glaring gap in South Korea's campaign finance rules. By treating high-cost polling data like ordinary physical gifts or informal advice, critics argue, the court effectively allows politicians to accept valuable third-party services and avoid liability simply by denying any prior agreement to pay.

In modern elections, tailored polling data is neither cheap nor neutral—it guides campaign strategy and shapes voter perception. As Oh emphasized in her dissent, failing to separate unpaid political advice from expensive, third-party data production overlooks how modern patronage works, potentially making it easier for brokers and candidates to exchange favors off the books.

Questions over judicial accountability

Viewed alongside the Han Duck-soo decision, the two rulings highlight a sharp philosophical divide inside South Korea's top bench. Splitting a cabinet meeting called one to two hours before a martial law declaration from the decree issued immediately afterward struck the dissenting justices as overly formalistic, leaving observers to question whether the court's narrow standards are eroding accountability on major constitutional and political matters.
