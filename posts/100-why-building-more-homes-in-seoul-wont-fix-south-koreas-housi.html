Why Building More Homes in Seoul Won't Fix South Korea's Housing Crisis

September 03, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean policymakers are aggressively pushing to build more apartments in Seoul to tackle housing shortages, while tweaking loan rules and easing restrictions on real estate project financing (PF).
  • What it changes: Rather than lowering prices, endless building in the capital fuels a relentless cycle of migration that supercharges Seoul's overpopulation and revives dangerous financial risks in the construction sector.
  • Who is affected: Young, first-time homebuyers who are sidelined by narrow, mismatched loan policies, and an economy exposed to growing property debt.

The Endless Loop of Capital Overcrowding

South Korea's policy debate has long operated on a single, stubborn reflex: when housing prices rise in Seoul, officials promise more concrete. But treating the capital's housing crunch simply as a lack of physical space misses the core dynamic. Pouring more supply into Seoul acts like adding lanes to a jammed highway—it only encourages more people to pile in, reinforcing the magnetic pull of the capital region.

Compounding this is the treadmill of migration. New apartment complexes trigger localized displacement, as residents shuffle from older neighborhoods into shiny new blocks, leaving vacancies that spark demands for yet more development. Until policymakers address the root cause—the concentration of jobs, industry, and culture outside of Seoul—housing supply targets will remain an exercise in running on a treadmill, justifying endless urban expansion while solving nothing.

Flawed Financial Fixes and Risky U-Turns

Financial interventions from the government have done little to inspire confidence. Recent low-interest loan policies targeting non-apartment properties under about $290,000 (400 million won) miss the mark for young people who desperately want sustainable long-term homes, not forced compromises into niche housing types. Restricting financial lifelines to narrow categories treats the symptom while ignoring the systemic hurdles facing first-time buyers.

Worse still is the sudden policy pivot on real estate project financing. After spending the past few years tightening lending rules to weed out vulnerable construction loans, authorities are now looking to loosen them again in pursuit of short-term supply quotas. Igniting the property market through renewed liquidity—while banks view construction project lending with extreme caution—risks seeding the very next financial crisis. Real housing stability requires structural shifts and regional balance, not a constant cycle of panic-driven building and patchwork loans.
