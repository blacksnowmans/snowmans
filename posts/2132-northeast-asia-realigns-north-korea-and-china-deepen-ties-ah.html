Northeast Asia Realigns: North Korea and China Deepen Ties Ahead of APEC

November 09, 2026

North Korea and China are transitioning from traditional friendship to a stage of strategic coordination as a high-level Pyongyang delegation concludes a pivotal five-day mission to Beijing.

  • Kim Song-nam, the Director of the International Department of the Workers’ Party of Korea, met with Cai Qi, the fifth-ranked official in the Chinese Communist Party, and Foreign Minister Wang Yi to solidify "party-to-party" cooperation.
  • The diplomatic flurry comes just ten days after Kim Jong-un visited China, signaling that Beijing and Pyongyang are synchronizing their positions ahead of the upcoming APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) summit.
  • In Tokyo, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is maintaining a hardline "strength-against-strength" stance toward China while simultaneously signaling a desire for a surprise summit with North Korea to resolve the abduction issue.
  • Meanwhile, China is purging its military ranks—with 50 high-ranking officers removed in three years—as it prepares for the 21st Party Congress to confirm a fourth term for President Xi Jinping.

The Emerging Continental Bloc

The recent high-level meetings in Beijing suggest that the "continental power" axis of North Korea and China is hardening. By moving beyond formal state diplomacy into deep party-to-party integration, the two nations are building an ideological buffer against U.S.-led pressure. For North Korea, China provides a vital exit from diplomatic isolation; for China, North Korea remains a strategic asset to leverage against Washington’s regional influence. This tightening bond is a stark reminder of the shifting security environment that President Lee Jae-myung’s administration must now navigate.

The current government faces a significantly more complex map than that of the previous era. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, a tilt toward ideological diplomacy often left South Korea’s strategic interests vulnerable, inadvertently fueling the trilateral alignment of North Korea, China, and Russia. In contrast, the Lee administration is now attempting a more pragmatic, multi-channel approach to ensure that Seoul is not sidelined as Beijing and Tokyo make their own moves toward Pyongyang.

Internal Volatility and Regional Risks

While China appears unified on the surface, the internal "loyalty audits" for 2,360 upcoming Party Congress delegates and the ongoing purges in the military suggest a regime hyper-focused on internal stability. History shows that when authoritarian regimes face internal friction or power transitions—such as Xi’s move toward a fourth term—they often adopt more aggressive stances abroad to divert attention. This makes the Taiwan Strait and the Korean Peninsula potential flashpoints for "calculated provocations."

Japan’s current trajectory adds another layer of unpredictability. Prime Minister Takaichi’s refusal to follow the more conciliatory "Abe-style" approach toward China has led to a deep diplomatic freeze, highlighted by a recent spat where China threatened "double retaliation" if its consul general was expelled. However, her openness to meeting Kim Jong-un suggests that Japan is willing to break ranks with its allies if it means securing a domestic political win on the abduction issue.

The essence of international relations is the cold pursuit of national interest behind a veil of rhetoric. As the U.S. and China seek to establish "guardrails" through their first high-level military talks in 11 years, South Korea must remain a nimble, realistic actor. President Lee's focus on technological sovereignty and diversified supply chains is the correct response to a region where economic assets are increasingly being used as weapons of war.
