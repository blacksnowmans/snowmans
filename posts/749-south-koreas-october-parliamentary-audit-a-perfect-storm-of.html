South Korea’s October Parliamentary Audit: A Perfect Storm of Political Warfare

October 06, 2026

The annual parliamentary audit—a month-long period where the National Assembly scrutinizes government administration—has begun, and for the Lee Jae-myung administration, it is shaping up to be an exhaustive defensive battle against a coordinated offensive from opposition parties and conservative media.

  • The Audit’s Scope: The National Assembly, led by the conservative People Power Party (PPP), is leveraging the audit to target real estate policy failures, financial market controversies, and presidential personnel decisions.
  • Key Flashpoints: High-profile sessions include the confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong, the nominee for head of the newly established Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA), and the October 30th audit of the Presidential Office.
  • Media Strategy: Conservative outlets are aggressively amplifying narratives surrounding the "Seongnam Line"—a group of aides associated with President Lee’s time as mayor of Seongnam—to suggest cronyism and a lack of moral authority.
  • Public Sentiment: With housing prices hitting record highs and retail investors reeling from leveraged ETF losses, public frustration is being expertly channeled by the opposition to destabilize the government’s core support base.

A Coordinated Offensive

The timing of these attacks is no coincidence. Since early September, there has been a systematic "buildup" in the media. By linking President Lee’s increased activity on social media to a "lack of serious governance" and weaponizing statistics on housing prices, critics are painting a picture of an administration disconnected from the average citizen's struggles. The recurring focus on the "Seongnam Line" is clearly designed to frame the current administration as a private interest group rather than a national government.

Perhaps the most significant development is the growing alienation of the administration's core supporters. Many who backed President Lee feel increasingly stifled by the current leadership’s internal communication breakdowns. When a government begins to view the concerns of its own base as "internal noise" or "disloyalty," it loses its most vital shield. The sentiment expressed by some supporters—that they feel more suppressed today than during the authoritarian eras of the 1980s—is a warning sign that the government is facing a crisis of legitimacy that no amount of parliamentary maneuvering can fix.

Beyond the Audit: The Real Cost of Political Polarization

The parliamentary audit is intended to serve as a check on administrative power and a search for policy solutions. Instead, it has devolved into a mechanism for political "character assassination." The controversy surrounding leveraged ETF losses is being used to summon specific former officials as scapegoats, turning complex financial market failures into shallow political theater.

The administration is currently caught in a pincer movement: they must defend against a highly prepared opposition while simultaneously managing the exhaustion of their own supporters. If the government continues to treat the audit solely as a tactical battle to be won—rather than an opportunity to address the genuine economic pain of the public—they risk complete isolation. In politics, the most dangerous threats often arise not from external attacks, but from the internal silence that follows when a leader stops listening to those who put them in power.
