Seoul Housing Crisis Deepens as Monthly Rents Spike and Supply Plans Face Local Backlash

November 02, 2026

Seoul apartment prices have extended their rally to 90 consecutive weeks, prompting President Lee Jae-myung to frame the ongoing rally as an "endurance phase" toward market normalization while surging monthly rents inflict fresh pain on working-class tenants.

  • 90-week rally sparks political clash: President Lee urged patience as Seoul home prices continued their record climb, drawing swift condemnation from the main conservative opposition People Power Party over the administration's market outlook.
  • Surging monthly rent burdens: A chronic shortage of jeonse (South Korea’s traditional lump-sum lease system) pushed up monthly rents in outer Seoul districts, led by double-digit jumps in Nowon (12.9%) and Seongbuk (11.8%).
  • Resistance to mega-housing plans: Government proposals to lift greenbelt land restrictions for 37,000 homes in Gimpo and 9,800 units in Gwacheon triggered strong protests from local authorities and residents over transit shortfalls and land compensation.
  • Household debt rebounds: Bank lending to households resumed its upward trend after a brief one-month lull, even as home buyers faced mortgage rates near 5%.

Market Realities Clash with Policy Calls for Patience

President Lee Jae-myung’s call for public endurance comes at a precarious moment for the real estate market. While the liberal administration views credit controls and supply announcements as essential steps to curb speculative asset growth, price pressures are simply shifting. High-end districts like Gangnam have seen transaction volumes slow under strict loan caps, but buyer demand has migrated to relatively affordable northern Seoul neighborhoods and southern satellite cities such as Suwon and Yongin—a classic "balloon effect" that keeps overall index figures rising.

For non-homeowners, the crisis has migrated directly into lease contracts. Tightened bank lending and reduced deposit insurance limits have made securing a traditional jeonse lease increasingly difficult. Displaced renters are forced into expensive monthly rent (*wolse*) arrangements, pushing monthly payments in outer districts past 3 million won (about $2,200). Meanwhile, deposit-to-value ratios for small studio apartments (*officetels*) have neared 90%, creating heightened risks of default and threatening to undermine the lower rungs of Korea's housing ladder.

Supply Bottlenecks and Administrative Missteps

The government's push to expand supply by unblocking greenbelt zones in the greater Seoul area is encountering immediate friction. Local leaders, including Gwacheon Mayor Shin Kye-yong, and residents in Gimpo have publicly rejected the state-led projects, pointing to severe traffic bottlenecks and unilateral decision-making without adequate transit infrastructure. Without local buy-in and clear compensation frameworks, these public housing projects risk long delays.

At the same time, individual land value appraisals (gaebyyeol gongsi jiga) have been posted across municipalities, opening an official objection window through November 27 for property owners facing higher holding taxes. Until the government matches its long-term supply pledges with immediate relief for the rental market, administrative calls to endure the storm will do little to reassure struggling households.
