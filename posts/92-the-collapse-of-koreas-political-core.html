The Collapse of Korea’s Political Core

September 27, 2026

South Korea’s political and judicial systems are suffering from a "core collapse"—a fundamental erosion of social trust where the law has shifted from a shield for citizens into a weapon for those in power.

  • The root cause: A systemic loss of empathy among politicians, who increasingly view their rivals and the public as mere obstacles or tools rather than partners.
  • Judicial overreach: Critics, including attorney Jung Pil-seung, argue that the prosecution has acted as a political entity, using its monopoly on investigation and indictment to target opponents.
  • Political volatility: Declining approval ratings for the current administration and internal power struggles within the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal opposition party) reflect a growing disconnect between political leaders and the public.
  • Implications for the future: Without structural reforms, such as the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, the democratic process risks being stifled by an unchecked "prosecutorial state."

The Crisis of the Rule of Law

The core of the issue lies in a misunderstanding of what law is. In a healthy democracy, the law exists to protect the individual from the state. However, in Korea, the prosecution has historically held unchecked power, often operating under the guise of public service while functioning as an independent political actor. The "Cho Kuk incident"—a high-profile legal battle involving a former Justice Minister—serves as a cautionary tale of how state agencies can weaponize information and media to undermine political rivals.

Recent legislative moves to abolish the prosecution's direct supplementary investigation powers are a necessary, if painful, first step toward balancing the scales of justice. Without clear checks and balances, the judicial system remains a "monster" that citizens are forced to fear, rather than a system they can rely on for protection. The creation of a dedicated investigative agency for major crimes, separate from the prosecution office, is now viewed by many observers as an essential reform to prevent further political interference.

Empathy Deficit and Political Decay

Beyond the legal framework, there is a psychological crisis among the political elite. Drawing on neuroscience, critics point to the "atrophy of mirror neurons"—a biological phenomenon where those in power lose the ability to empathize because they no longer feel the need to understand others to survive. In this environment, colleagues are reduced to "obstacles," and political supporters are treated as disposable tools rather than the bedrock of the movement.

This "core collapse" is dangerous because political power is not as stable as it appears. History shows that when politicians lose sight of the public interest in favor of internal power struggles, they risk losing their base entirely. In a digital age, the electorate is faster than ever to identify when their representatives have stopped listening. If political parties fail to realign with the needs of the people, they should not be surprised when voters seek out new alternatives. The power to elect is also the power to eject, and the current state of political arrogance is a path toward self-destruction.
