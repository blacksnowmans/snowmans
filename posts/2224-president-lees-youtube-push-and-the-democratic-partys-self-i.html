President Lee’s YouTube Push and the Democratic Party's Self-Inflicted Cult Scandal

November 11, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing twin political headaches: growing skepticism over President Lee Jae-myung’s unfiltered direct messaging to the public, and bitter internal fallout after party leadership floated—and then retracted—explosive allegations of infiltration by the controversial religious sect Shincheonji.

  • Direct messaging dilemma: President Lee expanded his personal communication push beyond X (formerly Twitter) by launching the official YouTube program President’s Y, but critics argue increasing the sheer volume of unmediated remarks only multiplies confusion and ideological disputes.
  • The Shincheonji walk-back: Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok formally apologized on November 6 after claiming during his summer campaign that a secret alliance existed between factional rivals and Shincheonji, admitting that internal reviews found no evidence of such collusion.
  • Data debunked: An internal party audit revealed that suspected Shincheonji members eligible to vote in the party convention numbered roughly 3,300—just 0.4% of the electorate—demolishing claims that the sect swayed election outcomes.
  • Factional fallout: Restricting access to the audit report to 30-minute, read-only sessions at the party headquarters has fueled suspicions of a cover-up, prompting the conservative opposition People Power Party to demand an independent special counsel probe.

More Channels, Less Clarity: The Limits of Direct Presidential Broadcasts

President Lee’s launch of President’s Y was pitched as an innovative way to bypass traditional press filters and explain state affairs directly to citizens. However, media commentators note that expanding personal broadcast channels misdiagnoses why government messaging struggles. When political leaders run into public pushback, the natural instinct is often to assume they simply have not explained themselves enough. In reality, substituting formal press conferences with one-way video monologues robs listeners of the chance to ask tough questions, turning nuance into polarization.

Conspiracy Politics Backfires on Party Leadership

Meanwhile, the ruling party is grappling with the aftermath of an irresponsible internal whisper campaign. During the July leadership race, Kim Min-seok weaponized rumors of mass recruitment by Shincheonji—a secretive religious group deeply unpopular with the Korean public—accusing rival internal camps of relying on cult support. Once elected, Kim formed a task force to inspect party rolls. The findings proved anticlimactic: suspected sect members made up a negligible 0.4% slice of voters, showing zero coordinated impact.

Instead of transparently closing the chapter, the party leadership allowed lawmakers to view the report only behind closed doors in 30-minute shifts without photocopies. This half-hearted containment strategy has backfired spectacularly. Hardliners like former party chief Song Young-gil have stoked the fire by demanding aggressive purges of the membership, while the rival People Power Party is seizing on the chaos to push for a special prosecutor. By manufacturing a phantom conspiracy to win an internal election, the Democratic Party leadership has created a lingering scandal that now threatens to drown out its own governing agenda.
