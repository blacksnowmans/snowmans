Losing the Majority Means Impeachment: Former Lawmaker Choe Kang-wook Warns the Lee Administration

October 22, 2026

Former progressive lawmaker Choe Kang-wook has warned that President Lee Jae-myung’s administration will face immediate administrative paralysis and opposition-led impeachment drives if the ruling party fails to retain its parliamentary majority in upcoming elections.

  • The warning: Speaking on YouTube, Choe argued that the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is heading toward defeat in pivotal contests, including the Seoul mayoral election and the parliamentary general election, unless it radically overhauls how it governs.
  • The consequence: Losing the legislative majority would strip the administration of its governing mandate, halt ongoing reform agendas, and trigger relentless attacks from conservative opponents eager to launch counter-investigations and impeachment proceedings.
  • The administrative bottleneck: Choe targeted the top-heavy structure of the presidential office, arguing that roughly 350 staff members are reduced to passive messengers waiting on the president's individual instructions instead of running an autonomous, proactive policy system.
  • The historical parallel: Choe warned the ruling camp against complacency, noting that failure to deliver tangible economic relief could lead to a catastrophic collapse reminiscent of former conservative leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was ousted and is now in prison.

Dismantling the PR Machine: From Live Broadcasts to Working Cabinets

Choe’s critique cuts directly at the performative habits of modern executive governance. In his view, administrative momentum is stalling because decision-making remains bottlenecked at the top. The presidential secretariat must function as an integrated engine across policy, political affairs, and public communications rather than a passive clearinghouse. When all eyes simply look to the president for final cues, ministries stall, inter-agency conflict festers, and administrative agility disappears.

Choe also called for an end to turning Cabinet meetings into public spectacles. Live broadcasts, he argued, paralyze government ministers, incentivizing rehearsed posturing for the cameras over rigorous policy debate. Sensitive calculations—ranging from economic shocks and labor disputes to diplomacy with Washington, Tokyo, and Pyongyang—cannot be hashed out under studio lights. Choe recommended holding closed-door, gloves-off brainstorming sessions at least once a month so cabinet members can argue candidly before arriving at coherent policy decisions.

The Taejong Dilemma: Why Governance Demands a Hard Edge

To illustrate the administration’s broader strategic vulnerability, Choe invoked Joseon Dynasty statecraft: King Sejong the Great achieved his celebrated, enlightened reign only because his father, King Taejong, had already carried out the ruthless political purges required to secure authority. Choe argued that Korean progressive leaders from Kim Dae-jung to Lee Jae-myung have consistently aspired to be the benevolent "Sejong," while neglecting the gritty, adversarial work of building defensive political infrastructure and containing hardline opposition tactics.

In Choe's assessment, good intentions and polished communications are no substitute for institutional hard power. If the administration continues to manage state affairs through individual charisma while the ruling party bleeds legislative leverage, its reform agenda could disintegrate within months of an electoral defeat. For the Lee administration, surviving the next electoral cycle will require moving beyond public relations and constructing a disciplined, system-driven government capable of taking political hits.
