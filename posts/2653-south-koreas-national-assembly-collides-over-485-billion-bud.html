South Korea’s National Assembly Collides Over $485 Billion Budget and Corporate Relief Bills

November 24, 2026

South Korea’s ruling and opposition parties entered a fierce standoff on November 23 over next year’s 677 trillion won ($485 billion) national budget and a slate of critical economic bills, raising fears that key decisions will be pushed into secret, unrecorded negotiations.

  • Budget stalemates trigger secrecy concerns: Intense battles over spending—including a 162 trillion won ($116 billion) fund for future technology and growth—threaten to transfer final reviews to informal, off-the-record legislative subcommittees.
  • Corporate workout law faces cliff edge: Lawmakers are deadlocked over extending the Corporate Restructuring Promotion Act, set to expire in one month, which provides the legal framework for out-of-court debt restructuring for distressed firms.
  • Business leaders demand fast-tracked support: Industry associations urged parliament to pass semiconductor subsidies and regional growth bills, but partisan disputes over regulation and state spending have stalled progress.
  • Local governments scramble for cash: Regional governors and mayors flooded parliament to lobby for emergency central funding amid worsening local fiscal deficits.

Gridlock Over Corporate Relief and Cybersecurity Fines

The legislative collision highlights deep strategic divides between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP). Meeting with executives from six major business groups, DPK leadership pledged to fast-track targeted state spending and growth initiatives. In contrast, the PPP argued that reducing tax burdens and scrapping restrictive regulations are the only viable paths to economic recovery. Industry leaders warned that parliamentary delays are costing South Korean exporters their competitive edge, particularly in semiconductors where global rivals receive aggressive state subsidies.

Tensions are equally high over financial legislation. The Assembly is reviewing a contentious amendment to the Electronic Financial Transactions Act that would impose punitive fines of up to 3% of revenue on financial institutions suffering major cyberattacks or data leaks. Meanwhile, parliament remains split on whether to extend the expiring Corporate Restructuring Promotion Act by five years or make it permanent—a delay that financial experts warn could force vulnerable companies straight into court receivership.

Unrecorded Budget Talks and Local Funding Crises

With the December statutory budget deadline looming, the risk of negotiations shifting to informal sub-subcommittees is growing. Because these off-the-record sessions leave no transcripts, critics warn they allow party leadership to swap pork-barrel projects and quietly trade off disputed allocations—such as funding for AI news verification systems, Ukraine reconstruction aid, and the massive future fund—without public oversight.

This gridlock comes at a dire moment for regional authorities. Local leaders, including the mayor of Busan requesting an additional 1.2 trillion won ($860 million) and the governor of South Chungcheong Province seeking 13.5 trillion won ($9.7 billion) in state support, descended on the Assembly to rescue their local budgets. Concurrently, DPK Lawmaker Hwang Un-ha introduced a bill to fully relocate the National Assembly from Seoul to Sejong City. Yet as long as political brinkmanship dominates parliament, structural reforms and local economic lifelines remain trapped in gridlock.
