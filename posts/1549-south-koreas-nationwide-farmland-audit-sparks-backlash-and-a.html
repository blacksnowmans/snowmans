South Korea's Nationwide Farmland Audit Sparks Backlash and a Blame Game

October 27, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung faces mounting political fallout after attempting to shift blame onto his agriculture minister for a costly, nationwide farmland audit that disrupted rural land markets and sparked furious pushback from farmers.

  • The Policy: An 80 billion won (about $58 million) full-scale audit launched to root out speculative agricultural land purchases nationwide.
  • The Fallout: Rural land transactions froze, farmland values plummeted, and aging farmers faced sudden margin calls and loan recall pressures from lenders as collateral values dropped.
  • The Shift: At a meeting with farmers at the Blue House guesthouse, President Lee claimed he had only sought targeted audits around Seoul, asserting that Agriculture Minister Song Mi-ryung pitched the nationwide sweep—despite live-broadcast cabinet records from February 24 showing Lee explicitly ordered the full investigation.
  • The Opposition: Lawmakers from the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, launched a coordinated attack, accusing Lee of ducking accountability when flagship policies sour.

A Televised Order and a Convenient Shift

During the meeting with farmers and fishermen at the Blue House, President Lee attempted to defuse rural anger by telling attendees that he originally intended only to inspect speculative land around the greater Seoul metropolitan area. He credited—or rather, attributed—the nationwide scope to Song Mi-ryung, the Minister of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs, claiming she suggested conducting a complete audit while they were at it. Lee added that the government had no intention of confiscating farmland or unfairly penalizing owners. However, the explanation quickly backfired. Broadcast records from a cabinet meeting on February 24 clearly show Lee himself calling farmland management a mess, ordering large-scale task forces, and directing Song to inspect the entire country and issue disposal orders to absentee owners.

Conservative lawmakers in the People Power Party seized on the contradiction. In committee meetings and official party statements, opposition figures—including Jang Dong-hyeok, Lee Sang-hwi, Jeong Hee-yong, and Yoon Sang-hyun—blasted the president for displaying "selective amnesia" and sacrificing a subordinate. They argued that shifting blame onto a minister for carrying out a presidential directive betrays the fundamental norm of cabinet responsibility, leaving civil servants fearful and paralyzed.

Rural Collateral Damage and Chilled Bureaucracy

The controversy is not merely political theater; it carries real economic damage in the countryside. Aimed at eliminating real estate speculation, the blunt, nationwide mandate treated elderly farmers with fallow land and individuals who inherited ancestral plots as potential criminals. Farmland transactions across rural provinces ground to a halt, triggering a sharp decline in agricultural land prices. Because many Korean family farms finance their operations by pledging land as collateral, the sudden drop in asset values led local cooperatives and commercial banks to demand immediate, partial repayments—pushing cash-poor elderly households toward insolvency.

By attempting to pass off an 80 billion won policy failure as a cabinet minister's overzealous initiative, the administration has compromised its own reform agenda. When heads of state publicly distance themselves from televised orders the moment public opinion turns hostile, the administrative apparatus learns only one lesson: protect yourself through inaction. South Korea's civil service is notorious for risk aversion when political winds shift, and this public disavowal of an agriculture minister will only harden bureaucratic inertia across government ministries.
