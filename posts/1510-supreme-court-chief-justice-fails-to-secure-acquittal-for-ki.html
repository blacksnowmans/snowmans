Supreme Court Chief Justice Fails to Secure Acquittal for Kim Keon-hee

October 26, 2026

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s reported attempt to clear Kim Keon-hee of criminal charges through the Supreme Court’s Full Bench has collapsed after failing to secure a majority of justices.

  • Only four out of 13 justices on the Supreme Court’s En Banc (Full Bench) session voted to acquit Kim Keon-hee, the wife of the imprisoned former president, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • The deliberations focused on allegations of stock manipulation involving Deutsch Motors (a BMW dealer in Korea) and the illegal receipt of free public opinion polls valued at approximately $200,000 (270 million won).
  • The failure marks a significant setback for conservative factions within the judiciary who were allegedly seeking to provide a "judicial pardon" to the family of the disgraced former president.
  • The ruling Democratic Party of Korea is now expected to accelerate aggressive judicial reforms, including the dismantling of the National Court Administration, the court's powerful administrative arm.

The vote reveals a deepening divide within South Korea’s highest court. While Chief Justice Cho and three other conservative-leaning justices—Oh Seok-jun, Eom Sang-pil, and Park Young-jae—pushed for a resolution in Kim’s favor, three justices—Oh Kyung-mi, Kim Sung-soo, and Lee Soo-yeon—resolutely argued for conviction based on legal evidence. The remaining justices either opposed the acquittal or remained uncommitted, preventing the seven-vote majority required for a final ruling.

This internal resistance serves as a crucial check on what critics are calling a "judicial coup." Despite the Chief Justice’s administrative influence, the refusal of the majority to bypass standard legal procedures suggests the judiciary’s independence is not yet fully compromised. However, the political friction is worsening; Chief Justice Cho has already clashed with President Lee Jae-myung’s administration over judicial appointments, leaving one seat on the Supreme Court vacant for over 200 days after the Chief Justice rejected the government's consultation on candidates.

A Looming Push for Systemic Judicial Reform

The controversy has energized calls for structural overhaul. Legislators from the ruling Democratic Party are preparing bills to replace the National Court Administration with a more transparent "Judicial Administrative Office" that includes non-judge members. This move aims to strip the Chief Justice of "imperial" power over personnel, budgets, and administrative decisions, which critics argue has been used to pressure lower courts into conservative-leaning rulings.

The push for reform is driven by a perceived "paradox" in the appellate system. Observers have noted a trend where the Seoul High Court frequently reduces sentences or overturns convictions delivered by lower courts in cases involving high-profile conservative figures. By implementing stricter disciplinary measures for judges and curbing the influence of former high-ranking officials—often referred to as "senior counsel privileges"—the current administration seeks to prevent the judiciary from acting as a political interest group. For international investors and observers, this struggle highlights the ongoing volatility as South Korea continues to adjudicate the legacy of the former Yoon Suk-yeol administration.
