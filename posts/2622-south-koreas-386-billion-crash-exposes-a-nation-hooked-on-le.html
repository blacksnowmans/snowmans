South Korea’s $386 Billion Crash Exposes a Nation Hooked on Leverage

November 22, 2026

A simultaneous rout in U.S. semiconductor stocks and South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI index around November 20, 2026 has inflicted an estimated $386 billion (563 trillion won) in domestic equity losses on South Korean retail investors while obliterating their heavily leveraged bets on Wall Street.

  • A historic wipeout at home: After peaking near 9,000 in June, the KOSPI halved to the 4,800–4,900 range, costing 14 million individual investors an average of roughly $27,400 (40 million won) each—and triggering $226 million (330 billion won) in forced liquidations alongside $1.4 billion (2 trillion won) in margin-debt fallout.
  • An outsized gamble abroad: South Koreans hold $46.6 billion (68 trillion won) in U.S.-listed exchange-traded funds (ETFs), with 40% parked in leveraged products; despite representing just 2–2.5% of global GDP, Koreans own 44% of all global shares in TSLL (2x Tesla), 25% in SOXL (3x semiconductors), and 11.2% in TQQQ (3x Nasdaq-100).
  • Tech plunge triggers panic: On November 20, U.S.-listed shares of South Korean chipmaker SK Hynix tumbled 13.11% alongside Nvidia (-14.01%) and SanDisk (-16.05%), driving CNN’s Fear & Greed Index down to an extreme 10—a level where cash-strapped retail traders had nothing left to buy the dip.
  • Regulators slam the brakes: Following a U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission warning to nine asset managers against launching products above 2x leverage, South Korea imposed strict hurdles requiring a $20,500 (30 million won) cash-only deposit, three hours of training, and five days of mock trading to buy single-stock leveraged funds.

Trapped between a distrusted home market and daily decay

South Korean investors did not flood into triple-leveraged U.S. tech funds out of simple recklessness; many were fleeing a domestic market they view as rigged. Local traders have grown deeply cynical toward South Korean equities, which routinely fall alongside Wall Street without sharing in its rebounds. Corporate governance scandals have only deepened the mistrust, exemplified by SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won, who urged investors not to sell SK Hynix shares only to later offload roughly $1.4 billion (2 trillion won) worth of stock in a block deal. Yet chasing leverage abroad proved a structural trap: because daily-reset ETFs suffer from negative compounding during volatile swings, investors in TQQQ and SOXL captured only 1.5 to 1.9 times their underlying indexes even during rallies—before getting decimated in the downturn.

A broken wage ladder fuels high-stakes bets

Behind the retail mania lies a grim math problem for ordinary workers. According to April 2026 figures from South Korea’s national statistics agency, 49.8% of salaried workers earn less than $2,050 (3 million won) a month before taxes, and 61.6% take home less than that after taxes. Reaching even a modest middle-class income often requires working six-day, 11-hour rotating shifts or taking grueling overnight jobs in -20°C freezer warehouses for e-commerce giant Coupang. With wages barely covering living costs and major Seoul apartment complexes like Gangnam’s Eunma sliding 16% to 18% from their peaks, saving slowly felt futile. Now that those high-risk bets have evaporated years of hard-earned pay in days, South Korea faces a severe freeze in household spending—and no obvious path back for a generation of burned investors.
