Lee Jae-myung’s 90-Minute Presser: A Turning Point or Just More Talk?

September 16, 2026

South Korean opposition leader Lee Jae-myung’s recent 90-minute press conference has sparked intense debate over whether his proposals can rescue a political landscape currently defined by the president’s struggling 30% approval rating.

  • Constitutional Reform: Lee signaled openness to discussing structural changes, including shortening the presidential term, but critics warn this looks more like a tactical power-grab than genuine reform.
  • Foreign Policy Risks: Concerns are mounting over potential South Korean troop deployments to the Strait of Hormuz, with critics urging the government to prioritize national interests over reflexive, alliance-first diplomacy.
  • Economic Strain: The government’s housing policies are under fire for prioritizing ideological "price control" over the immediate need for residential stability for ordinary citizens.
  • Leadership Crisis: The administration is facing backlash for internal communication failures and a perceived isolation from public sentiment.

The Constitutional Reform Trap

The push for constitutional amendment is a recurring theme in Korean politics, often surfacing when a government faces a crisis. While Lee Jae-myung expressed a willingness to consider changes to the power structure, the timing and motive are highly suspect. In a political climate where trust is at a nadir, voters increasingly view such proposals not as necessary updates to the 1987 constitutional framework, but as desperate maneuvers by a cornered administration to redistribute power rather than address systemic issues.

Successful constitutional changes require broad consensus and a long-term national vision. When leaders approach the constitution as a tool to gain a strategic advantage, they inevitably fail. For this government, the People Power Party—the main conservative party—is unlikely to cooperate, and the public remains skeptical of any "reform" that appears to prioritize political survival over the needs of the electorate.

Foreign Policy and Housing: Priorities Out of Sync

On the international front, the government is caught in an awkward position regarding the Strait of Hormuz. Despite domestic opposition and polling data showing a lack of public appetite for military involvement, there is palpable anxiety that the administration may lean too heavily on its alliance with the U.S. at the expense of national interest. This "alliance-at-all-costs" mentality is being interpreted as a lack of diplomatic independence.

Domestically, the situation is equally fraught. The government’s housing policy has become trapped in an ideological struggle to "bring down prices." However, for the average citizen, this rigid focus has ignored the reality of surging monthly rents and housing instability. By focusing on macroeconomic metrics while families struggle to keep a roof over their heads, the administration is losing touch with the daily struggles of its people. Genuine governance requires a shift from ideological signaling to practical, market-based solutions that ensure stable housing for all.

Ultimately, a 90-minute press conference cannot solve these problems. The public is no longer moved by speeches; they are waiting to see if the government is capable of admitting its failures and committing to the difficult, necessary work of policy reform. Without a fundamental change in tone and approach, these issues will continue to erode the remaining foundations of the current administration.
