South Korea's Opposition Pushes Special Counsel Probe Into Alleged Prosecutor Document Leaks

September 23, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's minor opposition Rebuilding Korea Party has introduced a third special counsel bill targeting Han Dong-hoon, a prominent conservative politician and former justice minister, over accusations that he used confidential prosecution documents for political attacks.
  • The core allegation: Han publicly displayed unfiled, non-public documents—including text message transcripts and a draft indictment written by the Seoul Western District Prosecutors' Office—raising suspicions of illegal document leaks and violations of official secrets acts.
  • Political fallout: Criticism has emerged across the political spectrum, including from within the ruling People Power Party, over how confidential investigative files routinely end up circulating as political ammunition.
  • What's next: The Rebuilding Korea Party is urging the main opposition Democratic Party to jointly review the special counsel bills through the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee to bypass internal prosecution oversight.

South Korea's legal and political establishment is rattled following revelations that highly sensitive, unfiled prosecutional documents—including private call transcripts and indictment plans—ended up in the hands of conservative heavy-hitter Han Dong-hoon. The incident has laid bare how state investigative information can be weaponized in political turf wars, triggering fierce backlash from opposition lawmakers who argue that internal oversight is dead.

The Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive minor opposition group, argues that letting prosecutors investigate their own colleagues over leaked records is akin to "leaving fish in the care of a cat." Because the documents in question were allegedly drawn from restricted internal databases without proper public oversight, critics warn that ordinary citizens remain vulnerable to having their private records treated as political collateral. The proposed special counsel legislation aims to bypass the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials—which critics accuse of dragging its feet for over two years on related complaints—and grant independent investigators full powers to probe, indict, and prosecute those involved.

The Battle Over Systemic Reform and Accountability

This controversy reaches far beyond a single politician, striking at the heart of South Korea's enduring debate over prosecutorial reform. For years, critics have accused successive administrations of utilizing state investigative archives as informal political weapons rather than neutral tools of justice. With figures across political lines—including conservative figures expressing discomfort over the sloppy handling of state records—conceding that information controls have failed, pressure is mounting on the main opposition Democratic Party to align with smaller parties and force structural changes through the legislature.

Editor's Take: This scandal is a stark reminder that South Korea's criminal justice system remains vulnerable to systemic abuse by those who wield its levers. If state investigative data can be freely repurposed as political ammunition by former prosecutors-turned-politicians, no citizen is safe from arbitrary exposure. True reform requires dismantling these entrenched information cartels through independent oversight, not political cover-ups.
