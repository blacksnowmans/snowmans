South Korean Politics: Approval Ratings Slip as Partisan Tensions Rise

October 13, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is facing a critical juncture as falling approval ratings for President Lee Jae-myung and intensifying parliamentary audits define a week of volatility.

  • Approval Slide: Recent polling from Realmeter shows President Lee’s approval rating dropping for the second consecutive week to 35.2%.
  • Power Shift: In a significant reversal, the main opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea's main conservative bloc—has overtaken the ruling Democratic Party of Korea in favorability, 41.3% to 35.5%.
  • Legislative Conflict: The ongoing National Assembly audits have been marred by fierce partisan fighting, ranging from disputes over government inspections to threats of impeaching the Chief Justice.
  • Platform Scrutiny: In a rare moment of alignment, both ruling and opposition parties have united to condemn e-commerce giant Coupang for its refusal to comply with on-site inspections by the Korea Fair Trade Commission.

The Warning Signs for the Ruling Bloc

The latest polling data serves as a sharp wake-up call for the Lee Jae-myung administration. The 7 percentage point drop in Democratic Party support over a single week suggests that the ruling party is losing its grip on both progressives and independent voters. While the opposition PPP is eager to capitalize on this momentum, the root cause appears to be growing fatigue among voters regarding the government’s communication style and the pace of its policy implementation.

The political atmosphere has been further poisoned by "emperor golf" allegations leveled against President Lee. Opposition figures have aggressively targeted the President over a golf outing during a period of national mourning, while the administration has hit back, accusing critics of engaging in malicious personal attacks. This cycle of mudslinging—where private lives are used as political ammunition—is increasingly alienating a public that is desperate for tangible economic relief rather than partisan theater.

Judicial and Institutional Friction

The friction extends into the halls of justice, where the independence of the judiciary and the integrity of the prosecution are being tested. Minister of the Interior and Safety Yoon Ho-jung’s recent order to inspect the conduct of public prosecutor Lim Eun-jeong has sparked a firestorm. The administration views the move as a matter of restoring public official discipline, while critics argue it is a transparent attempt to purge non-compliant figures.

Adding to the chaos, the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, progressive group—has signaled its intent to pursue the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. This move has drawn sharp backlash from legal experts and the opposition, who view it as an unprecedented assault on the principle of the separation of powers. By treating judicial leaders as political targets, these parties risk eroding the public trust required for the court system to function as a neutral arbiter in society.

Ultimately, as politicians fixate on short-term gains and legislative brinkmanship, the issues that affect the average citizen—such as rising costs, digital safety, and platform monopolies—are relegated to the background. Unless the ruling and opposition parties can pivot from perpetual confrontation to substantive governance, they risk losing the one thing they cannot recover: the confidence of the electorate.
