How a 26-Year-Old Korean Creator Turned YouTube Shorts into a Global Cash Machine

November 01, 2026

A 26-year-old South Korean college dropout has generated over 620 million views and more than $110,000 (around 147 million won) from a single YouTube Shorts channel by running an AI-driven, multilingual content pipeline.

  • The model: Sourcing pre-validated viral footage—ranging from universal pet clips to niche military topics—from platforms like TikTok and China's Xiaohongshu, then adapting them for overseas audiences.
  • The automation: Custom AI tools trained on thousands of viral scripts generate rigid five-part narratives (Hook, Setup, Twist, Conclusion, and Call-to-Action), cutting production time to 20 minutes per video.
  • The footprint: Operating 10 channels simultaneously across high-advertising-rate markets like the United States, Japan, and Spain, pulling in peak daily revenues close to $3,000 (4 million won).
  • The cultural shift: The operation functions as a family cottage industry—involving the creator's parents and sister—reflecting how young Koreans are weaponizing automation to escape domestic wage stagnation.

Industrializing Short-Form Video

South Korea’s domestic digital market faces an insurmountable bottleneck: a shrinking population and relatively low advertising rates (RPM). For Korean creators, domestic views yield only a fraction of the ad revenue generated by viewers in North America or Western Europe. Lee Si-hyun bypassed this ceiling by treating YouTube Shorts not as a creative art, but as standardized process manufacturing. By sourcing visual content that requires zero cultural context and licensing footage via Creative Commons or cross-platform arbitrage, his operation exports localized content directly to high-margin ad markets.

Crucially, the operation relies on heavy leverage rather than technical complexity. By training specialized AI assistants on the exact pacing and retention curves rewarded by platform algorithms, the team stripped out guesswork. Lee’s retired father and mother were trained to assemble completed video cuts in roughly half an hour, proving that platform distribution has shifted from artisanal video production to modular factory assembly.

The Anatomy of Korea's Automated Side Hustle

This success highlights a broader socio-economic pressure cooker in South Korea. Squeezed by real estate costs, inflation, and rigid corporate hierarchies, younger Koreans have aggressively embraced the "N-job" phenomenon—holding multiple side hustles to generate non-labor cash flow. Online communities that once traded real estate tips are now obsessed with automated digital pipelines that bring in foreign currency.

Yet the model sits on a precarious edge. While Lee safeguards his operation by adding original AI narration, localized translations, and selective filtering to navigate YouTube's reused content policies, platforms are steadily raising the bar against synthetic and recycled media. For now, however, the playbook shows how digital creators in smaller economies can build scalable, borderless export businesses from a home desk.
