How Bureaucratic Loopholes Are Threatening South Korea's Prosecution Reform

September 08, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's Ministry of Justice has proposed a new organizational structure for the newly planned Prosecution Office (Gongso-cheong)—which takes over the non-investigative duties of the gutted prosecution service—that retains hundreds of prosecutors and staff despite losing its core investigative powers.
  • The loophole: Critics, including former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae, point out that keeping the same staff numbers and creating new oversight units like a "Judicial Control Department" amounts to reviving old prosecutorial powers through executive enforcement decrees ( 시행령).
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party's Judiciary Committee members, who face intense backlash from reform advocates for remaining silent while bureaucrats hollow out hard-won legislative reforms.

South Korea’s long-running efforts to strip the prosecution of its sweeping investigative powers were supposed to mark a historic turning point. Instead, a familiar battle is playing out behind the scenes: a major reform passed by the legislature is being quietly dismantled through bureaucratic fine print and executive decrees.

Under the newly proposed structure, the incoming prosecution agency is slated to launch with hundreds of prosecutors and general staff, maintaining a massive organizational footprint even after losing its direct investigation mandate. Critics argue that without investigative duties, keeping this many prosecutors makes no administrative sense and serves only to protect institutional turf. Moves to establish a "Judicial Control Department" to oversee police decisions and guide special judicial police look less like reform and more like an attempt to smuggle old powers back through the backdoor.

The Silence of the Democratic Party and the Limits of Bureaucratic Reform

The political fallout has exposed deep fractures within the progressive camp. While a vocal minority of lawmakers—such as Choo Mi-ae and Jung Cheong-rae—have forcefully criticized the Ministry of Justice's restructuring plan, the majority of the Democratic Party’s Judiciary Committee members have stayed largely silent, caught up in local constituency work or distracted by other political fights.

This inaction has left reformers frustrated, raising uncomfortable questions about whether the party ever truly intended to see the overhaul through to the end. History suggests that passing a grand law is only half the battle; the real test lies in preventing bureaucrats from using administrative rules and executive orders to restore the status quo.

As things stand, the legal framework for reform is intact, but the enforcement decrees threaten to hollow it out completely. With mainstream politicians looking the other way, reform advocates argue that the only way to keep the pressure on is through active civic engagement and party membership mobilization—ensuring that politicians fear organized voters more than they fear entrenched bureaucratic power.
