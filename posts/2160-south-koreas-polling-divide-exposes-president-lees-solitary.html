South Korea’s Polling Divide Exposes President Lee’s Solitary Communication Strategy

November 10, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung faces wildly divergent public approval ratings, highlighting a growing split between enthusiastic core supporters and a cautious broader public while underscoring the limits of relying solely on direct social media outreach.

  • What happened: Polls from early November 2026 showed stark contrasts, with pollster Flower Poll reporting positive approval at 61.3% via automated response (ARS), while Next Meter registered a low 35.9% positive and 60.1% negative rating.
  • What it changes: The 25-percentage-point gap illustrates a polarized landscape where reform-minded progressives rally behind the administration while moderates and conservatives express growing fatigue over economic and daily livelihood issues.
  • Who is affected: President Lee, his presidential aides, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP), and the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), which remains competitive despite the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol.

Divergent Polling and the Limits of One-Man Outreach

The discrepancy between polling agencies reflects more than statistical methodology. In the first week of November 2026, Flower Poll’s telephone interview survey showed President Lee at 55.5% approval, while its ARS survey jumped to 61.3%. Conversely, Next Meter put his approval at just 35.9%. This polarization shows that while highly engaged progressive voters actively support Lee's reform drive, broader public sentiment remains cold due to persistent economic pressures. Meanwhile, the main opposition PPP maintains approval in the upper 30s—capitalizing on moderate dissatisfaction—while the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party holds 6.9%, capturing progressives seeking stronger reform.

To bypass hostile media coverage, President Lee frequently uses X (formerly Twitter), Facebook, and YouTube live broadcasts to communicate directly with citizens. However, this direct strategy acts as a double-edged sword. By acting as his own chief communicator, the president absorbs all frontline political fire while cabinet ministers, presidential aides, and DP lawmakers hide in the background. Running a government requires systemic support rather than solo performances, and leaning heavily on individual social media outreach risks overburdening the chief executive.

Internal Friction Over "Fandom Politics"

Controversy recently erupted after President Lee posted on social media that while political leaders need praise and support, it should never escalate into "religious praise or worship." Intended as a warning against uncritical fandom politics, the vague message sparked fierce debates among supporters. Different factions within the ruling bloc immediately began interpreting the statement as a strike against rival internal opinion leaders, turning a call for self-reflection into internal division.

The episode highlights a growing political weakness: a lack of delicate political coordination from the president’s staff. Without clear institutional backing, even well-meaning presidential messages can trigger unnecessary factional conflict. For the Lee administration to maintain momentum, the ruling party and cabinet must step forward to lead legislative reforms and protect the president, shifting focus from core-base rhetoric to tangible improvements in everyday livelihoods.
