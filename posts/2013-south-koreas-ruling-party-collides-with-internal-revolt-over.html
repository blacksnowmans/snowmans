South Korea’s Ruling Party Collides With Internal Revolt Over Religious Sect Probe

November 06, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party is facing a severe internal crisis after leadership opted to restrict internal findings regarding members' alleged ties to Shincheonji, a controversial religious sect, to a private, time-limited review rather than a full public disclosure.

  • The decision: Starting November 6, only lawmakers and regional chapter heads may individually inspect the Shincheonji investigation results upon request.
  • Leadership's apology: Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok publicly apologized to party members for the uproar, admitting he has felt uneasy about the issue since it first emerged.
  • Internal backlash: Senior figures, including lawmaker Choe Min-hee—who accused the leadership of attempting to silence her—and lawmaker Jung Chung-rae, have openly attacked the closed-door approach as a cover-up.
  • The political risk: Critics warn that the handling of the scandal undermines the ruling party’s stated commitment to transparency, alienating supporters and threatening party unity under President Lee Jae-myung.

A Test of Transparency for Lee Jae-myung’s Ruling Party

The core friction within the Democratic Party stems from a clash between institutional self-preservation and the radical transparency demanded by its base. When allegations surfaced that members of the controversial religious sect Shincheonji had infiltrated the party, supporters expected an aggressive, open house-cleaning. Instead, leadership chose a bottlenecked review process, citing the legal and political hazards of a blanket name-drop. By refusing full transparency and leaning on the defense that past conservative administrations triggered chaos by releasing similar lists, the current leadership has handed ammunition to its critics. For a party whose identity is built on contrasting itself with the secretive governance of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, treating internal corruption allegations like state secrets is a self-inflicted wound.

The public airing of grievances by party heavyweights like Choe Min-hee and Jung Chung-rae signals that this is no longer a localized dispute, but a full-blown crisis of confidence in Kim Min-seok's leadership. When a ruling party begins policing its own members' speech and restricting investigative access to an exclusive club of elites, it invites accusations of a cover-up. As the inspection window opens, the risk is not that the truth remains hidden, but that partial leaks and rumors will tear the party apart from the inside out, hobbling the legislative engine meant to back President Lee's agenda.
