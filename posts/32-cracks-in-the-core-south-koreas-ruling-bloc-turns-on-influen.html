Cracks in the Core: South Korea’s Ruling Bloc Turns on Influential Critic

September 24, 2026

The South Korean political landscape is reeling from reports of a widening rift between President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and Rhyu Si-min, a legendary figure and intellectual godfather of the country's progressive movement.

  • The Conflict: Rhyu Si-min, long a pillar of progressive influence, has publicly attacked the Lee administration, prompting reports that ruling party insiders now consider him a liability to be "discarded" rather than an ally to be courted.
  • The Trigger: Mounting frustration over the administration’s handling of policy failures and a perceived defensive, arrogant attitude toward criticism.
  • The Impact: The dispute signals a dangerous erosion of the administration’s "core" support base, rather than just middle-of-the-road voters, as the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal party—prepares for key upcoming party conventions.

A Government Under Siege from Within

The core of the issue is not merely inflation or real estate policy, but a growing perception of administrative incompetence and hubris. Rhyu has compared the current governance style to "wearing shoes with stones inside"—arguing that by refusing to acknowledge and remove the "stones" (policy blunders), the administration is actively harming itself. This sentiment is echoed by other influential progressive media figures, such as Kim Ou-joon, who warns that the party’s most loyal voters have shifted from active support to a "folded-arms" stance of passive observation.

While the administration remains locked in a bitter power struggle with the conservative opposition, led by People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyeok, it appears dangerously detached from the reality of its own supporters. The ruling bloc’s attempt to paint Rhyu as a mere "critic" to be isolated, while cozying up to more compliant media figures, suggests a strategic pivot toward control over critical reflection. By prioritizing the consolidation of power under the "pro-Lee" faction, the administration risks alienating the very base that brought it to power.

The Risk of Silent Defection

Political history in South Korea shows that regimes do not usually fall because of the opposition's shouting; they collapse when their own supporters stop applauding. The current "heavy silence" from formerly ardent backers is a precursor to a potential exodus. The administration’s reliance on the belief that voters have nowhere else to go—"who else are they going to vote for, the conservatives?"—is a dangerous gamble that ignores the power of voter apathy.

As the Democratic Party heads toward its August convention, the internal struggle between ideological purists and pragmatic "Lee loyalists" will define its future. If the administration continues to view internal critique as an act of betrayal rather than a necessary corrective, it may find that its biggest obstacle isn't the political opposition, but the quiet, irreversible departure of its own people.
