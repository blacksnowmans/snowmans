South Korea's Ruling Camp Faces Internal Friction Over Media Control and Presidential SNS Habits

October 09, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea's ruling Democratic Party official YouTube channel, "Minty," faces collapse as its lead writer resigned amid internal turf wars and accusations that the show alienated its core base.
  • Presidential digital burnout: The Blue House defended President Lee Jae-myung's X (formerly Twitter) account after he amassed over 400,000 mutual follows, dismissing concerns as overblown despite critics calling it an inefficient use of presidential time.
  • Press freedom friction: The presidential office and the Presidential Security Service face backlash from the press corps for unilaterally disciplining a journalist without adhering to standard reporting pool protocols.
  • Who is affected: President Lee's administration, progressive digital media outlets, and journalists covering the Blue House, all of whom are navigating rising tensions over transparency and message control.

The Fracturing of Progressive Media and Presidential Digital Overreach

South Korea's progressive media ecosystem is showing deep structural cracks. The departure of "Minty's" lead writer—who shaped the show's format and direction—brought simmering backstage grievances into the open. Rather than uniting supporters, the show became a flashpoint for internal blame-shifting, with production staff shouldering public backlash while hosts allegedly distanced themselves from editorial decisions. Critics argue this points to a broader rot in pro-government YouTube commentary, where loyalty is demanded downwards but accountability flows nowhere.

This insular approach extends straight to the top. The Blue House's defense of President Lee's relentless social media networking—manually managing over 400,000 mutual follows—misses the mark entirely. While the administration frames it as grassroots engagement born of high-stakes political urgency, spending hours on a smartphone is an inefficient use of a head of state's time. When paired with the administration's heavy-handed discipline of a journalist over a security dispute, the message being sent is one of defensiveness rather than confident leadership.

Selective Discipline and the Loss of Reformist Credibility

Compounding these communication blunders is an apparent double standard in how the Blue House treats internal infractions. Critics point out that while past severe security breaches by pro-government media figures went unpunished, a reporter questioning security protocol was swiftly hit with disciplinary action. This selective enforcement risks alienating the very public that brought the Democratic Party back to power following the impeachment and imprisonment of former President Yoon Suk-yeol.

Ultimately, these controversies share a single root cause: a preference for message control and patronage over transparent systems. When a government relies on personal hustle—whether a president's endless X scrolling or arbitrary press pool penalties—institutional checks break down. If the current administration continues to weaponize rules against critics while shielding its own, it risks burning through the public trust that made its return to power possible in the first place.
