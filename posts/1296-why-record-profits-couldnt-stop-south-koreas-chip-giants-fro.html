Why Record Profits Couldn't Stop South Korea's Chip Giants from Plunging

October 19, 2026

South Korea’s semiconductor flagships, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, have suffered a brutal market rout driven not by deteriorating business fundamentals, but by an aggressive institutional liquidity squeeze and automated risk-off selling.

  • Historic profits met with steep drops: Despite factory production lines running 24/7, order books locked in for two to three years, and operating margins topping 70%, Samsung fell over 9% in a single session, contributing to an intraday KOSPI plunge that triggered a 20-minute circuit breaker.
  • Massive foreign capital flight: Foreign investors dumped roughly 1.45 trillion won (about $1.1 billion) in Samsung and 1.11 trillion won (about $830 million) in SK Hynix in a single day, locking in profits after steep year-to-date rallies.
  • Compounded by mechanical sell rules: Domestic asset managers and sovereign funds—governed by rigid asset-allocation bands—joined algorithmic trading programs in dumping shares automatically once key index thresholds broke.
  • Retail traders absorb the blow: Individual Korean retail investors ("ants"), heavily exposed through margin and leveraged accounts, bore the brunt of cascading liquidations amid unfounded market rumors.

A Liquidity Shock Disguised as Industrial Trouble

The severity of the selloff has sparked fears of structural collapse, yet the balance sheets tell the opposite story. Samsung’s quarterly operating earnings outpace the combined operational income of Japan's top 100 listed firms, and next-generation lines like High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) remain heavily bottlenecked by high technological barriers. Rumors claiming Chinese memory maker CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies) will displace Korean DRAM overlook physical constraints: strict US semiconductor export controls deny Chinese fabs access to advanced ASML extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography systems essential for viable yields.

Instead, the crash was an institutional portfolio reset. Macro headwinds—including US 10-year Treasury yields spiking to 5.32% and September CPI hitting 3.7%—prompted global funds to trim emerging-market risk. Having already hit their annual return targets on Korean chips, foreign desks cashed out of the most liquid large-cap counters on the exchange. SK Hynix recorded foreign net selling across 21 out of 22 consecutive trading sessions, overwhelming domestic market depth.

Rigid Mandates and the Fundamental Reality

The selloff intensified because domestic institutional buyers could not act as a backstop. Major institutional players, bound by statutory medium-term strategic asset allocation rules, are forced to trim equity exposure when volatility spikes or portfolio weightings drift out of bounds. This mechanical selling merged with automated stop-loss algorithms from leveraged private funds, unleashing continuous programmatic dumping across the order book.

While the sudden drop devastated leveraged retail portfolios, the long-term semiconductor cycle remains structurally anchored. Beyond current server GPU demand, upcoming waves of "physical AI" in robotics and national "sovereign AI" infrastructure initiatives will require high-density memory at unprecedented scale. For unhedged retail investors, the episode offers a harsh lesson in leverage and algorithmic trading mechanics: even the most dominant balance sheets can be temporarily overridden when institutional risk models trigger an exit.
