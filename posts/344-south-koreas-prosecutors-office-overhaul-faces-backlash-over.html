South Korea's Prosecutor's Office Overhaul Faces Backlash Over "Cosmetic" Reforms

September 07, 2026

    South Korea is abolishing its 78-year-old Prosecutor's Office on October 2, splitting its functions between a new Prosecution Service and a Serious Crimes Investigation Agency.
  • Critics, including lawmakers from the main opposition Democratic Party, warn that the current reorganization preserves the existing staff and culture without genuine downsizing.
  • Proposed rules would keep thousands of prosecutors and investigators intact, raising fears that old investigative cartels will simply rebrand under new names.
  • The changes also introduce controversial oversight powers and elite leadership appointments that critics argue strengthen, rather than check, prosecutorial overreach.

South Korea's long-running push to overhaul its powerful investigative agencies is running into a wall of skepticism. While the dissolution of the traditional Prosecutor's Office marks a historic institutional shift, the fine print of the Ministry of Justice's reorganization plan reveals a familiar problem: the structures of power are remaining largely untouched.

Despite splitting prosecution and investigation duties, the government's current blueprint retains the full cap of roughly 2,300 prosecutors while only slightly trimming administrative staff. Critics argue that moving thousands of legacy investigators directly into the new agencies merely paints a fresh coat of paint on an old building. Compounding these concerns, proposed leadership pools for the new investigative agency consist almost entirely of former judges and prosecutors, fueling accusations of entrenched legal elitism.

Old Habits Die Hard in South Korea's Legal Overhaul

The core promise of the reform was to shatter the unchecked monopoly on power long held by South Korean prosecutors. Yet, the newly proposed "pre-indictment investigation" rules and judicial control units look suspiciously like tools to preserve institutional dominance. By allowing prosecutors to screen cases before formal investigations begin, the system risks enabling selective enforcement—where politically sensitive cases quietly stall while others are aggressively pursued.

True reform requires a fundamental reduction of personnel and a clean break from the past, not an administrative upgrade that preserves cozy networks. Until South Korea strips away the structural privileges embedded in its legal elite, any new agency risks operating as the old prosecution system by another name.
