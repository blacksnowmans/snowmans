Why South Korea's Ruling Party is Plunging Even as President Lee Jae-myung Rebounds

September 22, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is flashing urgent warning signs as President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating edges up to 35%, while the ruling Democratic Party suffers a sharp 7-percentage-point collapse to 33.6% amid growing anger over botched key appointments and compromised reforms.

  • Diverging polls: President Lee's approval rating broke a nine-week skid to hit 35%, but negative sentiment among 30-somethings spiked to an alarming 79.5%.
  • Ruling party freefall: The Democratic Party's support dropped nearly 7 points in a single week, driven by internal factional feuding and public backlash.
  • The flashpoint: Public outrage centers on the nomination of Kim Ji-yong—a former senior prosecutor who aggressively fought against prosecution reform under past administrations—to head the newly formed Major Crime Investigation Agency.
  • Core grievances: Voters and party members feel betrayed as the administration recycles old conservative-aligned figures into top reform posts, sparking fears that promised prosecution overhauls are being abandoned.

The stark disconnect between President Lee’s modest bump and his party’s steep decline points to a deeper crisis of trust. Voters are no longer judging single policies; they are re-evaluating the entire ruling bloc. Economic pain is compounding political fatigue, and the public has little patience for politicians bickering over power while everyday living costs climb. When the base and the moderate middle both start turning away, it signals that the administration's core narrative of reform is wearing thin.

Worse still, the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as head of the Major Crime Investigation Agency feels like an outright betrayal to progressives. Kim previously led the charge against the very "prosecution reform" laws the ruling camp once championed, famously warning that curbs on prosecutorial powers would hurt the vulnerable. Handing the reins of a dedicated anti-corruption body to a former prosecutor who resisted structural change looks less like pragmatism and more like capitulation. If the administration continues to ignore grassroots anger and defensive bureaucrats, it risks squandering its mandate entirely.
