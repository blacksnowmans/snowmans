The Gyeonggi Dongbu Distraction: Deconstructing President Lee’s Power Base

November 03, 2026

Critics of the current administration are falling into a strategic trap by focusing on fringe activist groups instead of the broader "New Lee Jae-myung" establishment that now governs South Korea.

  • The "Red Scare" Trap: The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, is using the "Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance"—a hard-left activist faction—to frame the government as ideologically radical.
  • A Complex Coalition: The current power structure is not a single entity but a "New Lee Jae-myung" alliance composed of loyalists from the President’s time as Mayor of Seongnam, Gyeonggi provincial officials, and mainstream figures in the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK).
  • Personnel as Policy: Despite calls for inclusive politics, high-level appointments remain concentrated among the President's closest allies, signaling a "league of their own" rather than a broad-tent government.
  • Internal Fractures: Power struggles within the DPK, particularly surrounding figures like Kim Min-seok (a high-ranking DPK official), suggest a fragile balance that could shift if the current leadership fails to deliver results.

The Failure of Ideological Attacks

The obsession with the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance is a tactical error for the opposition. By invoking the "Red Scare"—a long-standing Korean political tactic of labeling opponents as pro-North Korean radicals—the PPP risks exhausting the centrist electorate. While certain presidential aides have roots in these activist circles dating back to President Lee's 2010 Seongnam mayoral run, reducing the entire administration to a single activist faction is an oversimplification. It allows the government to dismiss legitimate criticism of policy failures as mere "ideological warfare."

If critics want to be effective, they must target the "New Lee Jae-myung" machine as a whole. This machine is a pragmatically assembled conglomerate of power-seekers and loyalists who have followed the President from the periphery of local government to the center of national power. Attacking the fringe only hits the "supporting actors" while the "lead actors" continue to consolidate control over the state apparatus. The real story is not a secret activist takeover; it is the unprecedented consolidation of power by a once-outsider political faction that has now become the new establishment.

The Litmus Test for Reform

The only genuine metric for change in this administration is personnel. In Korean politics, Tangpyeong—the historical practice of balancing rival factions—is the traditional sign of a healthy presidency. However, the current era has been marked by a "my people first" approach. By excluding the "non-Lee" (Bi-myeong) factions of the DPK from core decision-making roles, the administration is building a fortress rather than a bridge. This exclusivity is the administration's greatest vulnerability; it creates a "spoils system" that is prone to internal decay and public backlash.

Ultimately, the survival of this political project depends on whether it can transition from a personality-driven coalition to a stable governing institution. If the DPK leadership remains a monolithic shield for the President, it will eventually crack under the pressure of its own weight. The opposition should stop chasing the ghosts of old student activism and start scrutinizing the actual mechanics of how the "New Lee Jae-myung" establishment exercises its current, very real power. Real reform will only be signaled when the administration appoints its critics to positions where they can actually speak truth to power.
