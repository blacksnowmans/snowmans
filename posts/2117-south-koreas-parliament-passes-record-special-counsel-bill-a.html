South Korea’s Parliament Passes Record Special Counsel Bill as Budget Clash Ignites

November 09, 2026

South Korea’s National Assembly has passed a historic special counsel bill to investigate past prosecutorial abuses, triggering a fierce parliamentary deadlock that now threatens the government's $590 billion national budget.

  • Record investigation launched: The bill authorizes a 309-member team and a 38 billion won ($27 million) budget—the largest in South Korean history—to probe alleged targeted investigations and fabricated indictments conducted during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency.
  • Opposition hits the streets: The People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition party, boycotted the vote and launched rallies, labeling the probe a "shield bill" to protect President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Budget targeted: Conservatives are threatening deep cuts to the ruling Democratic Party of Korea's 821 trillion won ($590 billion) budget proposal, zeroing in on a signature 162 trillion won ($116 billion) Future Response Fund for advanced technology.
  • Justice Ministry clash: Opposition lawmakers have also vowed to block the nomination of Lee Yu-cheol, a legal veteran named as Justice Minister to lead prosecutorial reforms.

Prosecutorial Reform Meets Street Protests

The passage of the special counsel bill marks a decisive push by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea to overhaul the nation's powerful prosecution service. Proponents argue that the unprecedented scale of the investigation is necessary to root out systemic political bias built up during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, when prosecutors were accused of weaponizing indictments against opposition figures. However, the conservative People Power Party views the probe as political retaliation and an effort to insulate President Lee Jae-myung from legal scrutiny, prompting opposition lawmakers to walk out of parliament and take their protests to the streets.

This conflict has spilled directly into cabinet appointments. President Lee's choice for Justice Minister, Lee Yu-cheol—a legal veteran with 30 years of experience who previously headed the defense acquisition agency—signals an effort to place a non-prosecutor in charge of the ministry to decouple investigative and prosecutorial powers. While the administration frames him as an outside reformer needed to break internal cartels, opposition members have launched fierce attacks, accusing the administration of using the appointment to influence ongoing judicial proceedings.

Fiscal Gridlock and Legislative Escalation

The partisan standoff now jeopardizes critical economic legislation as the National Assembly begins reviewing next year's budget. The opposition's threat to slash the 162 trillion won ($116 billion) Future Response Fund—designed to support artificial intelligence, biotechnology, and clean energy—could delay state investments at a time of heightened global economic uncertainty. Conservative lawmakers argue the fund is an unnecessary executive slush fund, while local governments have raised concerns over potential reductions in regional grants.

With the opposition preparing filibusters against pending housing and social reform bills, legislative momentum has stalled. Unless both sides retreat from maximalist positions, South Korea faces a turbulent year-end legislative session where broader economic policy risks becoming collateral damage in the feud over prosecutorial reform.
