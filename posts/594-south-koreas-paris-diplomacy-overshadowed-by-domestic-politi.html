South Korea's Paris Diplomacy Overshadowed by Domestic Political Friction

September 10, 2026

  • South Korea and France upgraded their ties to a "global strategic partnership" in Paris, marking 140 years of diplomatic relations and agreeing to closer military and security cooperation.
  • The diplomatic breakthrough comes as President Lee Jae-myung’s administration faces sliding public approval ratings driven by economic friction and party infighting.
  • Critics within the ruling Democratic Party and the conservative opposition remain locked in bitter political feuds over past prosecutorial investigations and legislative policies.

While President Lee secures major diplomatic wins on the global stage, back home the administration is bogged down by domestic squabbling and falling public support. The juxtaposition highlights a familiar Korean political paradox: robust international ambition colliding with a polarized, hostile local political landscape.

The source of the domestic friction runs deep, fueled by lingering fallout from past prosecutorial investigations targeting figures linked to the Democratic Party, and ongoing internal divisions within the ruling bloc. At the same time, policy missteps—particularly concerning real estate tax adjustments and stock market turbulence that hit retail investors—have eroded public trust, dragging down approval ratings. Yet, political posturing and internal turf wars often distract from the government's broader structural goals.

Global Partnerships and Cultural Soft Power

In stark contrast to the noise in Seoul, the Paris summit yielded tangible strategic gains. Beyond the upgraded partnership with French President Emmanuel Macron, the two nations signed an amended military intelligence-sharing agreement and mapped out joint air force drills. The countries also extended cooperation into Middle East security, touching on freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz, alongside a roughly €1 billion ($1.1 billion) partnership to boost cultural and video industries.

On the softer side of diplomacy, South Korea's cultural footprint continues to expand in Europe. Media reports from Paris highlighted how local eateries are successfully adapting Korean ingredients into innovative menus like "Joseon steak" and acorn jelly rice bowls, cementing K-food as a major cultural export. These wins demonstrate a maturing national standing that goes well beyond traditional statecraft, proving that South Korea's growing global influence is leaving a lasting mark overseas even as its politicians struggle to find common ground at home.
