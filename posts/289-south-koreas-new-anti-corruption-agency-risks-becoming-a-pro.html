South Korea's New Anti-Corruption Agency Risks Becoming a "Prosecution Season 2"

September 29, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea is set to launch the Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA)—a new body designed to strip investigative powers from the traditional prosecution—next month, but reports indicate the agency's top leadership posts are likely to be filled entirely by former prosecutors.
  • Who is affected: High-profile figures close to President Yoon Suk Yeol, including prosecutors who clashed with former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae during past turf wars, are reportedly being lined up for key roles.
  • What it changes: Critics warn that staffing the new independent agency with prosecutors steeped in the old institutional culture risks turning the MCIA into a toothless replica of the prosecution service, undermining public trust and democratic oversight.

The core promise behind establishing the MCIA was simple: break up the formidable, centralized powers of South Korea's prosecution service and place investigative authority under democratic check. But as reports emerge that even deputy-level positions are slated to go to career prosecutors, critics argue that a change of nameplate means nothing if the personnel remain the same. Institutional design matters very little when the people operating the machinery are culturally hardwired to protect old institutional loyalties.

This dynamic is painfully familiar. Observers note eerie parallels to past political networks where ideology and institutional background trumped reform. With politically sensitive investigations—including cases tied to first lady Kim Keon-hee—pending across various independent counsels and anti-corruption bodies, placing former prosecutors at the helm of a brand-new watchdog looks less like meritocratic hiring and more like institutional self-preservation. When the people running the investigative line are cut from the same cloth as the old guard, selective investigations and institutional foot-dragging become virtually inevitable.

The Return of the Pro-Yoon Guard

The controversy is sharpened by the specific names reportedly under consideration. Figures identified as key loyalists during the bitter 2020 showdown between then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol and Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae are once again surfacing for top assignments. When prosecutors who fought tooth and nail against civilian oversight are handed the reins of a supposedly reformist agency, it sends a clear message: this is political protection, not neutral law enforcement.

Ultimately, a reform is only as good as the people tasked with carrying it out. If the MCIA opens its doors next month only to be staffed by prosecutors loyal to the old power structures, the public will be left with a hollowed-out institution that serves the powerful rather than the citizenry. True reform requires changing the people at the top, not just renaming the building.
