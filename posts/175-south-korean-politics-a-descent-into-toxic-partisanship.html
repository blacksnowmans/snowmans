South Korean Politics: A Descent into Toxic Partisanship

September 05, 2026

South Korean politics has devolved into a cycle of "frame wars," where political figures and media outlets prioritize character assassination and sensationalist narratives over substantive policy debate, leaving the public increasingly alienated.

  • The Text Message Controversy: Lawmaker Kim Seung-won (of the liberal Democratic Party) is being accused of "improper solicitation" after a text exchange with the former head of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. Despite the official stating it was standard legislative inquiry, the incident is being weaponized to imply corruption.
  • Policy Displacement: Real issues, such as the actual timeline of clinical trial approvals, are being ignored in favor of polarizing headlines, creating a chilling effect on legitimate communication between the legislature and government agencies.
  • Tax Policy Concerns: The ruling People Power Party's proposal to raise the threshold for the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (an annual tax on high-value properties) from 1.2 billion won (about $900,000) to 1.4 billion won (about $1.05 million) is being criticized as a move that favors asset holders over the broader public, potentially fueling further market speculation.
  • Internal Democratic Party Fractures: Tensions within the opposition Democratic Party, highlighted by the resignation of senior advisor Lee Seok-yeon, reveal a growing intolerance for dissent as the party remains hyper-focused on protecting party leader Lee Jae-myung.

The Anatomy of a Frame War

The current political environment has weaponized "toxic political grammar." By reframing routine interactions as scandals—a tactic seen in both the current administration's supporters and opposition circles—politicians have made it nearly impossible to address complex policy issues. When politicians like Han Dong-hoon (a prominent conservative figure) utilize inflammatory rhetoric, they successfully drag the national discourse down to the level of tabloid gossip, ensuring that the public remains focused on "who is fighting dirtier" rather than the merits of government decisions.

This is particularly evident in how the legal system is increasingly dragged into political battles. Prosecutors are accused of using "suspended indictments" to keep political opponents under a cloud of suspicion, effectively creating a permanent, politically motivated stigma rather than pursuing justice. This "judicialization of politics" serves as a tool for destruction rather than accountability.

Policy as a Proxy for Power

The government's approach to real estate reform is a prime example of putting optics and donor-class interests over structural stability. By proposing tax cuts for property owners under the guise of "relief," the government risks repeating the policy failures of its predecessors. This is not just about a 200-million-won ($150,000) shift in tax brackets; it is a signal to the market that the state is prioritizing asset protection over housing affordability. When policies lack a clear, long-term philosophy beyond favoring a specific constituency, they only serve to widen the social divide.

Ultimately, the obsession with "protecting the leader" or "destroying the opponent" has created a political system that fails to solve the problems of the average citizen. Whether one views current political leaders as agents of reform or as liabilities, the current culture of internal purges and external shouting matches is unsustainable. For a healthy democracy, both politicians and the public must stop consuming the sensationalist narratives provided by partisan media and demand a return to substantive governance.
