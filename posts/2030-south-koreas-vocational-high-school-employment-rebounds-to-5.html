South Korea’s Vocational High School Employment Rebounds to 55.8%, But Structural Gaps Persist

November 06, 2026

South Korea’s vocational high school employment rate rebounded for the first time in four years to reach 55.8%, though low job retention and a persistent rush into university highlight deep structural flaws in the youth labor market.

  • Employment tick up: The overall employment rate rose 0.6 percentage points year-on-year to 55.8%, led by specialized Meister high schools and a rising share of placements (38%) at companies with 300 or more employees.
  • High job instability: Around 20% of graduates remain entirely unemployed, while nearly 3 out of 10 who land jobs fail to retain them for even a single year.
  • The college detour: Nearly half of all vocational graduates opt for university rather than entering the workplace directly, reflecting deep-seated societal bias against non-degree holders.
  • Sharp regional divides: Daegu ranked first nationwide with a 68.7% employment rate, while industrial hubs like Ulsan face high youth out-migration despite solid placement statistics.

Beyond the Headline Numbers: Quality vs. Quantity

At first glance, the slight uptick in graduate employment offers a welcome sign of relief for South Korea's vocational education system. The five-year trend of growing placements at large enterprises and public institutions suggests that partnerships between industry and government-designated Meister schools—specialized vocational institutions geared toward key national manufacturing and tech sectors—are bearing some fruit. Public energy companies like Korea Western Power have also introduced safety training programs for students to prevent workplace accidents during field internships.

However, celebrating a 55.8% employment rate ignores the fragile reality facing young workers. With three in ten employed graduates leaving their positions within 12 months, many are simply being funneled into unstable, low-quality jobs. Without guarantees of long-term job security and safe working conditions on factory floors, high employment figures risk creating a revolving door of short-term youth labor.

The Degree Trap and Regional Disparities

The fact that nearly half of vocational graduates choose higher education over employment underscores South Korea’s rigid credential-obsessed culture. High school graduates face significant wage disparities, limited promotion opportunities, and social prejudice in the workplace. Unless the labor market rewards technical skill over academic degrees, vocational high schools will increasingly function as waiting rooms for college entrance exams rather than launches for technical careers.

Regional imbalances further complicate the picture. While local government programs pushed Daegu's employment rate to 68.7% and Daejeon's to 59.9%, industrial cities like Ulsan see more than half of employed graduates leave for jobs in other regions. Local authorities are experimenting with new avenues—such as the Sejong City Office of Education's partnership with Australia's TAFE NSW to offer overseas vocational training—but systemic reform in the domestic labor market remains urgent.
