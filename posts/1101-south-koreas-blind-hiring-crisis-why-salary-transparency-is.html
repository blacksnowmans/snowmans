South Korea’s "Blind Hiring" Crisis: Why Salary Transparency is the Next Big Reform

October 14, 2026

  • The issue: Over 60% of South Korean job postings hide starting salaries behind vague terms like "according to company regulations" or "determined after interview."
  • The impact: This "information asymmetry" wastes applicant time, leads to mismatched expectations, and fuels distrust between workers and employers.
  • The push for reform: The Lee Jae-myung administration is considering legislative changes to mandate salary transparency, following global trends in the EU and the US.

In South Korea’s competitive job market, an applicant’s first encounter with a prospective employer is often a lesson in frustration. While companies demand exhaustive personal details—from educational history to family background—they frequently withhold the most critical piece of information: the salary. This culture of "blind hiring" is not just an annoying habit; it is a structural inefficiency that forces job seekers to gamble their time on roles that may not meet their basic financial needs.

Defenders of this practice often claim that salary is "confidential internal information" or that it should only be negotiated after a candidate is vetted. However, statistics suggest a more cynical reality: it is simply institutional inertia. When companies fail to publish pay ranges, they attract a flood of unqualified or misaligned candidates, ultimately wasting the time of their own HR departments. In an era where transparency is a proxy for organizational health, hiding salary data suggests that a firm either lacks a coherent compensation structure or is afraid to show its competitive weakness.

Beyond "Company Policy": A Global Shift Toward Disclosure

While current South Korean labor laws prohibit false advertising, there is no mandate to disclose pay, leaving a legal loophole that many firms exploit. This is increasingly out of step with global standards. The European Union’s pay transparency directives and various US state laws requiring salary range disclosures have turned this issue into a matter of economic survival. The Lee Jae-myung administration, backed by the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's current ruling party), is now debating legislation that would force companies to be upfront about compensation.

To bridge the gap, experts suggest a "banding" strategy—publishing a salary range rather than a fixed number. This provides applicants with a clear market value while allowing firms the flexibility to adjust offers based on specific skills or experience. It moves the conversation away from secretive, arbitrary "negotiations" and toward procedural fairness.

Ultimately, the refusal to disclose pay is often a sign of management laziness. Creating a structured compensation table requires effort, and "company policy" is a convenient shroud to hide the lack of one. If South Korean companies hope to attract top-tier talent in an increasingly globalized market, they must shed the "blind hiring" mask. True fairness begins with acknowledging that a job’s price is not a secret, but the most fundamental piece of information in the labor market.
