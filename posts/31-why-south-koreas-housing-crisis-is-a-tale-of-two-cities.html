Why South Korea's Housing Crisis is a Tale of Two Cities

September 24, 2026

While official reports celebrate falling prices in Seoul's ultra-wealthy Gangnam district, ordinary renters and middle-class families across the capital face a punishing crunch as purchase prices, key money deposits (jeonse), and monthly rents surge simultaneously.

    Gangnam is cooling, the rest is boiling: While elite districts like Gangnam and Seocho see price adjustments due to heavy property taxes, non-Gangnam working-class areas are hitting record highs. The triple surge: Home purchase prices, *jeonse* (lump-sum lease deposits), and monthly rents are climbing all at once, trapping tenants with nowhere to go. Supply choke points: A decade of canceled urban redevelopment projects—dating back to the administration of the late Seoul Mayor Park Won-soon—has created a severe structural shortage of new homes. Political missteps: Successive administrations have relied on punitive taxes and delayed deregulation, leaving first-time buyers to fend for themselves ahead of a projected rental crisis.

The Illusion of "Flattening" Prices

When South Korea's leadership claims the housing market is undergoing a "flattening process" because a few luxury apartments in Gangnam have dropped their asking prices, it offers cold comfort to a tenant facing a doubled lease deposit. Wealthy owners are cutting prices on multi-million dollar properties to dodge heavy holding taxes, but these homes sit empty rather than offering relief to average buyers. Meanwhile, outer districts like Gangbuk and Dobong are seeing fierce bidding wars as desperate buyers and renters are pushed out of the center.

This is not market stabilization; it is extreme polarization. First-time buyers without a home of their own face a terrifying "triple rise" in costs. In top school districts like Daechi-dong and Mok-dong, lease listings have dropped to less than 1% of total units. Government pledges to build hundreds of thousands of public housing units offer little help to a family whose lease expires this December, as those homes are years away from breaking ground.

A Decade of Policy Failures Comes Home to Roost

The roots of this crisis stretch back roughly ten years, notably to the mass cancellation of about 389 urban redevelopment and New Town zones under former Seoul Mayor Park Won-soon. That political push to scrap older residential upgrades vaporized an estimated 250,000 potential housing units—a development capacity several times the size of a new satellite city. Subsequent conservative and progressive administrations added fuel to the fire with punitive taxation, harsh lending curbs, and rigid zoning rules like the controversial land-transaction permit zones maintained under current Seoul Mayor Oh Sehun.

Housing is a long-lead industry that takes nearly a decade to deliver results from the ground up. By treating real estate as a political football and relying on quick-fix rental promises rather than freeing up private supply, policymakers have broken the market's mechanical equilibrium. As new housing completions dwindle further, South Korea's urban renters are being left behind in an unforgiving era of every person for themselves.
