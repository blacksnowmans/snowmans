South Korea’s Public Schools Struggle to Meet Surging Demand for High School Career Counseling

November 13, 2026

South Korean regional education offices are expanding customized academic counseling for incoming high school students, but severe staffing shortages continue to push most families toward expensive private consultants.

  • What happened: Regional education boards in areas like Jeonbuk and Sejong have launched tailored one-on-one academic design programs to help middle school seniors select prospective high school courses.
  • The core issue: Public guidance services remain severely under-resourced; official data from Daegu shows that only 17.9% of local university entrance exam takers received public counseling.
  • Who is affected: Incoming high school freshmen and college-bound seniors, along with parents forced to pay high fees for private admissions consulting.
  • Alternative fixes: Local education offices and universities are introducing artificial intelligence career programs and regional expos to help fill the counseling deficit.

A Deepening Deficit in Public Counseling

South Korea is expanding its High School Credit System, a reform that allows high school students to select elective courses aligned with their intended university majors and career paths. To help students prepare before entering high school, the Jeonbuk State Office of Education and the Sejong City Office of Education’s Career Education Institute have initiated customized one-on-one consulting for middle school seniors. However, public infrastructure is falling far short of demand. Data presented by Namgung Hyun-sook, a member of the Daegu Metropolitan Council, revealed that out of 25,389 students sitting for the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) in Daegu, only 4,550—just 17.9%—accessed public counseling services. With more than 75% of test-takers unable to secure public guidance slots, families are routinely driven to the private market, paying hundreds to thousands of dollars to private consulting agencies.

Tech Integrations and the Push for Reform

In response to the shortfall, education boards and local institutions are testing technology-based solutions and specialized initiatives. The Daegu Metropolitan Office of Education is holding regional expos to help families map out academic tracks, while the Korea National University of Transportation and the Chilgok County Office of Education have introduced artificial intelligence tools to assist students with career exploration. Other regional programs are offering hands-on vocational visits for student-athletes, multicultural youth, and out-of-school teens. While these initiatives broaden student exposure to new industries, educators stress that short-term events and digital tools cannot replace sustained, individual public guidance. Without deliberate budget increases and expanded counselor staffing within the public school system, access to critical academic planning will remain heavily dependent on a family's financial resources.
