The Business of Hate: Why South Korean Social Media is Addicted to Anger

September 07, 2026

In South Korea, hate speech has evolved from fringe social friction into a highly profitable, algorithm-driven business model that is actively eroding the country's democratic foundations.

  • The Profit Mechanism: Digital platforms monetize controversy; the more users engage with hateful, provocative content, the higher the advertising revenue.
  • Algorithmic Silos: "Confirmation bias" traps users in echo chambers, where algorithms feed them content that validates their prejudices and radicalizes their worldview.
  • Political Weaponization: Politicians increasingly use hate-filled rhetoric to demonize opponents and mobilize core supporters, treating social division as a cheap and effective campaign strategy.
  • Democratic Erosion: The focus on "us versus them" destroys the capacity for public discourse, compromise, and the coexistence of diverse values.

The Anatomy of the Hate Industry

In the past, hateful rhetoric was isolated, but today it is expertly curated. Digital platforms function as infrastructure for this "hate economy," tracking exactly what makes users angry and serving them content that confirms their biases. For many, this has turned into a form of "thought imprisonment," where users mistake algorithmic suggestions for objective reality. As individuals become more isolated and frustrated with their own economic prospects, they find psychological comfort in scapegoating "the other"—and platforms are more than happy to facilitate this addiction for a profit.

Politics as a Zero-Sum Game

The danger is most acute in the political arena. Rather than focusing on consensus-building, political actors now prioritize extreme "clarity"—essentially drawing sharp battle lines between friends and enemies. In the South Korean political landscape, conservative factions have been criticized for openly utilizing aggressive rhetoric to consolidate power. By masking this intolerance under the guise of "freedom of expression," these groups frame those who oppose their vitriol as enemies of the state. This is not merely a social disagreement; it is a systemic assault on the democratic process that risks turning political opponents into targets for harassment.

To break this cycle, we must stop viewing hate as a side effect of internet usage and start seeing it as a commercial product. Regulations that hold platforms accountable for their algorithms are necessary, but the most potent defense is individual awareness. We must stop being "loyal customers" to the hate industry by refusing to engage with, share, or click on inflammatory content. Unless we consciously choose to step out of these curated echo chambers, we risk turning our society into a permanent battlefield where the only winners are the platforms and the political extremists who profit from our division.
