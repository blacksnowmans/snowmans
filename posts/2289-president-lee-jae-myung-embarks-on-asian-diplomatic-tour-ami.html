President Lee Jae-myung Embarks on Asian Diplomatic Tour Amid Global Supply Chain Volatility

November 13, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung begins a critical four-day diplomatic tour across Asia on November 16, aiming to defend key semiconductor and mineral supply chains as geopolitical realignments accelerate following the U.S. presidential election.

  • The itinerary: President Lee will travel to Manila and China from November 16 to 19, participating in the South Korea–ASEAN and South Korea–Mekong summits, the ASEAN+3 Summit, the East Asia Summit (EAS), and the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) leaders' gathering.
  • Economic priority: Discussions at APEC will center on securing supply chains for semiconductors and critical minerals, advancing Seoul's push to diversify trade dependencies away from vulnerable single-source relationships.
  • Diplomatic reset: The administration frames the tour as a practical initiative to repair Seoul's standing following the diplomatic missteps seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, while seeking international backing for the Korean Peninsula peace process.
  • Turbulent backdrop: The tour coincides with escalating external friction, including reported high-decibel disputes between Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu over potential strikes on Iran, which threaten to spike energy costs and disrupt global shipping lanes.

Navigating Great-Power Friction Through Strategic Buffers

For President Lee and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, Southeast Asia serves as more than an export destination; ASEAN countries represent a vital geopolitical buffer. With intensifying trade disputes between Washington and Beijing, Seoul is pursuing what the administration calls "pragmatic diplomacy"—refusing rigid bloc alignment in favor of concrete economic security. By engaging directly with China and other regional leaders at APEC, the administration hopes to safeguard domestic exporters from arbitrary protectionist fallout.

This calculated engagement reflects hard lessons learned from past supply disruptions. Rather than tethering South Korea's export engine entirely to great-power maneuverings, the administration is prioritizing immediate national interest: keeping maritime supply corridors open, locking in essential industrial inputs, and maintaining stable regional working ties.

Hedging Against External Volatility

Seoul’s multilateral push is also a defensive hedge against unpredictability abroad. Reports of shouting matches between Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu over regional escalations in the Middle East illustrate how quickly regional conflicts can spill into global markets, threatening imported inflation and crude oil supplies vital to South Korea’s manufacturing base. Coupled with speculative chatter in Washington over congressional realignments and internal Republican pressures, Seoul cannot afford a passive posture.

By engaging multiple multilateral forums in quick succession, President Lee is attempting to insulate South Korea from sudden geopolitical shocks, ensuring that domestic bread-and-butter economic stability remains protected regardless of shifts in overseas capitals.
