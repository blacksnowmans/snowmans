South Korean Politics: A System in Disarray

October 10, 2026

South Korean politics has descended into a cycle of toxic infighting and power struggles that, according to critics, has left the National Assembly, the country's legislative body, completely dysfunctional. The ongoing drama surrounding high-profile figures and the failure of institutional reforms suggest a deeper, systemic rot that is alienating the public.

  • Institutional Breakdown: The National Assembly is increasingly viewed as a venue for personal vendettas rather than legislation, with a total lack of oversight mechanisms to curb power-hungry maneuvers.
  • Han Dong-hoon’s Role: Han Dong-hoon, a polarizing political figure, continues to draw intense scrutiny for his disruptive behavior, which many argue has exacerbated political instability within the main opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP).
  • Stalled Prosecution Reform: The sudden political exit of key reformers, such as Kim Seung-won, has sparked allegations of "sabotage" by established conservative interests within the prosecution, rendering much-needed judicial reform efforts ineffective.
  • Public Exhaustion: With the public focused on daily livelihood issues, the political elite’s obsession with sensational scandals—such as rumors surrounding the mobile phone of Kim Keon-hee (wife of the imprisoned former president)—has reached a breaking point.

The Crisis of Governance and Reform

The state of affairs in Yeouido—the center of South Korean political power—is increasingly described as a "cesspool." Even with the imprisonment of former president Yoon Suk-yeol, who was removed via impeachment, the political landscape remains crippled by a lack of ethics and procedural integrity. The ongoing infighting within the conservative People Power Party, coupled with the inability of political actors to prioritize the economy over petty power games, has left the public feeling cynical and exhausted.

The recent exit of figures like Kim Seung-won has laid bare the fragility of judicial reform. Critics argue that the prosecution remains a "power cartel" capable of purging those who threaten its influence. The consensus among reform advocates is that moderate, polite attempts at systemic change have failed; they are now calling for radical, unconventional appointments to dismantle the old guard that still occupies various layers of the Ministry of Justice and the prosecution.

The Path Forward for the Democratic Party

For the governing Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the current chaos offers a precarious advantage. While the opposition is in a state of self-destruction, the DPK cannot afford to be complacent. There is a growing sentiment that if the ruling party fails to deliver tangible results on economic and social issues, it will be held accountable by an increasingly restless public.

The rise of alternative media and independent political commentary—led by figures like Kim Ou-joon and journalists like Bong Ji-wook—highlights a major shift in public trust. Citizens are no longer relying on traditional media to interpret power dynamics; they are looking for transparency regarding the "hidden hands" behind political maneuvers. As it stands, the political establishment is failing to read the room. If the current power structure continues to prioritize self-preservation over the public interest, it risks a complete loss of legitimacy, regardless of which party is technically in power.
