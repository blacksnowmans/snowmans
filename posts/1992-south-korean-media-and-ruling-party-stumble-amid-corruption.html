South Korean Media and Ruling Party Stumble Amid Corruption and Labor Rollbacks

November 05, 2026

A sweeping bribery scandal involving 28 journalists and a major leisure conglomerate has exposed the persistent "collusion cartel" plaguing South Korea’s media landscape, even as the ruling party faces its own integrity crisis.

  • Reporters from 22 major media outlets accepted luxury golf entertainment from Ramid Group, a South Korean hotel and leisure conglomerate, leading to dozens of promotional "news" articles.
  • The scandal highlights the systemic failure of the Improper Solicitation and Graft Act (the "Kim Young-ran Act"), which was designed to stop journalists and officials from accepting gifts exceeding small amounts.
  • The ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing intense criticism for the "Mega Special Zone Act," a bill that critics say will effectively dismantle the 52-hour workweek in designated areas.
  • Internal party scandals, including mishandled allegations of religious cult infiltration, have sparked accusations of arrogance within President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

The "Golf Cartel" and the Legacy of Cash Envelopes

The recent exposure of the Ramid Group scandal reveals a disturbing level of coordination between the press and capital. Over a period of just ten weeks, 28 journalists were treated to luxury golf outings at the Yangpyeong TPC Golf Club. Each round was valued at approximately $240 (325,000 KRW). Following these "experiences," the reporters published 24 articles praising the group’s facilities. Notably, 18 of these puff pieces were written by the same reporters who accepted the free rounds. When confronted, most journalists remained silent or blamed the company for "poorly managing" the fallout, rather than reflecting on their own ethical breaches.

This behavior is a modern evolution of chonji—the old practice of reporters accepting envelopes of cash from sources. While the 1980s and 90s were defined by newsroom cultures where editors told rookies to "earn their own salaries" through such bribes, today’s corruption is more sophisticated. It relies on the "beat system," where senior reporters facilitate golf bookings and favors for corporate bosses in exchange for exclusive access or personal gain. This creates a cycle where the reporters meant to monitor power instead act as high-end PR agents, rendering their ethics codes meaningless.

President Lee’s Labor Paradox and the "Mega Special Zone"

While the media struggles with its credibility, the Lee Jae-myung government is facing a backlash from its own labor base. The administration is pushing the Mega Special Zone Act, a deregulation bill that would allow companies in specific zones to bypass the 52-hour workweek. Critics argue this is a "labor regression" reminiscent of the policies favored by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during his time in power before his impeachment and current imprisonment. By extending labor calculation periods to six months, the law would allow companies to work employees intensely during peak seasons without providing adequate rest or overtime protections.

This move creates a glaring contradiction for President Lee, who campaigned on a promise of a 4.5-day workweek. The administration claims these exemptions are necessary for national competitiveness in industries like semiconductors, but labor advocates see it as a betrayal. Furthermore, the Democratic Party’s internal handling of controversies—such as Kim Min-seok, the party chair, offering only private apologies for explosive public allegations regarding the Shincheonji cult—suggests a party more interested in consolidating power than maintaining transparency. For a government that rose to power on the promise of reform, these shifts toward corporate-friendly labor policies and secretive internal politics represent a significant risk to its democratic mandate.
