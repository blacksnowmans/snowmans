Niccolò Machiavelli Was Not Evil: Why We Misread The Prince

October 10, 2026

Niccolò Machiavelli’s The Prince remains one of history’s most misunderstood books, read not as a desperate survival manual for a fractured Italy, but as a wicked playbook of unprincipled cunning.

  • The context: Machiavelli wrote The Prince in 16th-century Florence while watching foreign powers like France and Spain tear the divided Italian peninsula apart.
  • The core insight: His brutal realism was born from a world where morality offered no protection against ruthless conquerors, making state survival the ultimate ethical test.
  • The common myth: The famous phrase "the ends justify the means" is not a license for selfish corruption, but a call to prioritize public survival over abstract idealism.
  • The takeaway: Stripping Machiavelli's work down to bite-sized quotes misses his brilliant diagnosis of political hardware and human nature.

Beyond the "Teacher of Evil"

Generations of readers have labeled Machiavelli the "teacher of evil" because he dared to dissect human nature without sentimentality. In chapters addressing cruelty and broken promises, he famously concluded that human beings are ungrateful, fickle, and hypocritical. To survive in such a landscape, a leader could not afford the luxury of standard saintliness. But reducing these observations to a generic handbook for image-making completely ignores the blood-soaked reality of Renaissance politics.

As a diplomat for the Florentine Republic, Machiavelli witnessed firsthand an international order governed by raw muscle rather than lofty ideals. Florence and other Italian city-states relied heavily on untrustworthy mercenaries rather than citizen armies, leaving them utterly defenseless when larger, centralized nation-states invaded. Machiavelli’s infamous advice—that a ruler must learn how not to be good—was a desperate diagnostic warning, not an endorsement of malice for its own sake.

History, Humiliation, and the Birth of a Masterpiece

The tragedy of Italy was rooted centuries deep, stemming from the collapse of the Roman Empire, the shifting fractures between the Holy Roman Emperors and the Papacy, and endless internal feuding. When powerful monarchies like France and Spain emerged from the Hundred Years' War with unified national identities, the divided Italian city-states were caught completely unprepared. Instead of uniting against external threats, Florentine and Italian elites routinely invited foreign powers in to secure their own narrow, factional interests.

For Machiavelli, the fall was personal. When the Medici family returned to power in 1512 backed by Spanish bayonets, the Florentine Republic collapsed. Machiavelli was stripped of his public offices, falsely accused of conspiracy, imprisoned, and subjected to brutal torture. Exiled to a meager farm outside Florence, he spent his nights in study, channeling his observations of human failure and political survival into an immortal text. What emerged was not a cynical guide for tyrants, but a brilliant, hard-headed manual on how states actually live, die, and endure.
