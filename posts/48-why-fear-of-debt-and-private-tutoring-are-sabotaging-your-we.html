Why Fear of Debt and Private Tutoring Are Sabotaging Your Wealth

September 01, 2026

  • Debt is not inherently bad: Low-interest borrowing (around 4%), like mortgages, acts as affordable leverage to build assets rather than something to rush and pay off.
  • High-interest debt is an emergency: Revolving credit lines and card loans with double-digit rates actively drain your income and must be cleared immediately.
  • The real cost of private academies: Pouring heavy sums into private tutoring (hagwons) out of social anxiety robs children of decades of compound interest.
  • Long-term vision wins: Avoiding short-term market panic and prioritizing steady, long-term asset growth and retirement planning is the key to financial freedom.

Differentiating Between Good Leverage and Financial Traps

Many people view any form of debt as a personal failure or an emergency that needs erasing. However, treating a standard mortgage—where borrowing helps secure a tangible asset at a manageable interest rate of around 4%—as a pure liability is a mathematical mistake. Rushing to pay down low-interest debt while missing out on investment returns means sacrificing the broader picture for short-term peace of mind.

On the flip side, high-interest consumer credit charging over 10% is a wealth-destroying engine. Treating this kind of debt casually while attempting to invest is like pouring water into a leaky bucket. Eliminating high-interest liabilities is always the non-negotiable first step toward building genuine financial security.

The Hidden Opportunity Cost of Traditional Parenting Pressures

In South Korea, societal pressure to spend heavily on private education is immense, driven by the fear that children will fall behind peers. Yet, diverting hundreds of thousands of won (roughly a couple of hundred dollars) every month into private academies sacrifices something far more valuable: time. Investing that same capital into reliable stocks or exchange-traded funds (ETFs) in a child's name over a 15- to 20-year horizon harnesses the explosive power of compound interest, teaching them the language of capitalism far better than a standard report card.

Ultimately, treating personal finance as a marathon rather than a sprint requires immense patience. Resisting the temptation of immediate consumption, rethinking how we manage debt, and ignoring herd mentality are difficult hurdles. But embracing these uncomfortable realities is the only reliable path to building lasting independence.
