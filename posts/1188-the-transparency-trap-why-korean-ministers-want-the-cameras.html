The Transparency Trap: Why Korean Ministers Want the Cameras Off

October 15, 2026

Cabinet ministers in the administration of President Lee Jae-myung are formally requesting an end to the live-streaming of high-level government meetings, claiming the practice has turned critical policy debates into scripted political performances.

  • The Appeal: More than 10 ministers recently appealed to Prime Minister Han Duck-soo to restrict live broadcasts to the President’s opening remarks rather than airing the entire 90-minute deliberative sessions.
  • The Chilling Effect: Cabinet members argue that the presence of cameras prevents them from providing honest, critical feedback to President Lee, as contradicting the head of state on a live feed is perceived as a political "rebellion."
  • Policy Volatility: Several recent policy reversals—including changes to the legal age of criminal responsibility for minors—are being attributed to impulsive presidential directives made "on air" without administrative vetting.
  • The Systemic Shift: This pushback marks a significant internal challenge to President Lee’s signature "direct communication" style, which relies heavily on live media and aggressive social media engagement.

The High Cost of Political Performance

The Lee Jae-myung administration, led by the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, has prioritized transparency as a direct counter-model to the secretive governance seen during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former president currently imprisoned after his impeachment). However, the current situation illustrates the "paradox of transparency." When the Cabinet—South Korea's highest executive deliberative body—becomes a television studio, the incentive shifts from solving complex problems to maintaining a curated public image. Ministers admit that saying "No" to the President on a live feed is a political suicide mission, effectively turning experts into a collection of "yes-men."

This friction highlights President Lee’s difficult transition from a local administrator to a national leader. His success as the former Governor of Gyeonggi Province was built on rapid-fire, direct communication with citizens. But national policy—ranging from labor laws to real estate taxes—requires a level of stability and institutional friction that the current live-streamed format discourages. The high volume of presidential activity on X (formerly Twitter), where he posted 116 times in September alone, further compounds this. Civil servants are often forced to treat late-night social media posts as official directives, bypassing the traditional system of bureaucratic checks and balances.

Digital Governance vs. Policy Stability

While the government points to "success" in the form of 22 million views for short-form clips of these meetings, the quality of that engagement is questionable. Sensationalist snippets are replacing nuanced policy discussion. Ministers highlighted the "Yellow Envelope Law" (a labor reform bill) as a prime example where presidential comments made during a broadcast forced the government into a legal corner, leading to public confusion and eventually a quiet withdrawal of the proposed enforcement decree.

"The irony of communication is that the cameras have effectively sewn the mouths of the ministers shut."

For the Lee administration to maintain its credibility with the markets and the public, it must realize that effective governance often happens in the quiet, unfilmed moments where experts are free to disagree. Without a space for private, rigorous debate, the administration risks being governed by the whims of the social media algorithm rather than the long-term needs of the country. Real transparency is not found in a staged broadcast, but in the delivery of stable, well-vetted policies that can survive the light of day.
