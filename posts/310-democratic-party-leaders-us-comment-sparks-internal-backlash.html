Democratic Party Leader's "Us" Comment Sparks Internal Backlash

September 07, 2026

A recent remark by Lee Jae-myung, leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country's main liberal opposition—has exposed a growing rift between the party’s leadership and its grassroots supporters.

  • The Incident: During a private dinner with party leadership, Lee remarked, "I thought you would understand [my decisions] because you are on our side."
  • The Backlash: Supporters, who view themselves as partners in a movement for systemic reform, interpreted the comment as a dismissive, arrogant assumption that their loyalty is automatic and unconditional.
  • The Stakes: Critics argue the DPK is prioritizing the expansion of its own political influence over the substantive reforms—specifically regarding the prosecution—that its base demands.

The controversy highlights a fundamental misunderstanding within the DPK leadership. Supporters do not rally behind the party out of blind devotion to a specific politician; they do so to secure social and historical progress. When leaders frame this support as a "tool" to be managed rather than a mission to be fulfilled, they risk losing the very momentum that keeps the party viable.

The disappointment is deepened by the perceived lack of progress on prosecution reform, a core promise rooted in the political legacy of the late President Roh Moo-hyun. Many voters fear the DPK is becoming a bloated, directionless entity that mirrors the conservative People Power Party in its aversion to genuine change. If the leadership continues to treat the electorate as followers to be mobilized rather than partners to be respected, they risk alienating the public and undermining their own political future.

Arrogance vs. Accountability

The DPK's obsession with "increasing its size" and building its own power base often blinds it to the public's instinctual demand for oversight. By reducing supporters to a "subordinate partner" who must accept decisions without criticism, the leadership has triggered a crisis of trust. Historically, political parties that ignore their base and suppress internal dissent eventually fall into the trap of populism and fragmentation.

True success for the DPK requires a shift away from instrumental politics. It must stop using its supporters as a tool for personal or factional survival and instead focus on establishing a coherent policy framework. The party is at a crossroads: it can continue to demand unconditional loyalty and face the slow erosion of its base, or it can treat the current criticism as a necessary "warning from democracy" and pivot toward the systemic reforms that citizens are desperate to see.
