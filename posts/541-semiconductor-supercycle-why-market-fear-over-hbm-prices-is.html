Semiconductor Supercycle: Why Market Fear Over HBM Prices Is Misguided

October 01, 2026

Despite record-breaking performance in the semiconductor sector, investors remain jittery over fears of a "peak"—but the data suggests the AI-driven memory boom is structural, not cyclical.

  • Supply Tightness: Global DRAM inventory is at record lows (around 3.3 weeks), and major players like SK Hynix—South Korea’s second-largest chipmaker—are operating at near-emergency stock levels.
  • The HBM Paradox: High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) production consumes massive amounts of base DRAM capacity. As HBM output scales, supply for generic DRAM shrinks, keeping prices elevated across the board.
  • Long-Term Stability: Unlike the volatile "spot market" cycles of the past, chipmakers have pivoted to Long-Term Agreements (LTAs) with Big Tech firms, creating a "seller’s market" with built-in price protections.
  • Shift in Strategy: Samsung Electronics, the world’s largest memory chipmaker, has reclaimed the top global DRAM market share and is increasingly outsourcing non-critical "back-end" packaging processes (OSAT) to focus its resources on high-margin AI chips.

Beyond the Goldman Sachs "Peak" Narrative

In mid-2025, a Goldman Sachs report sparked market panic by predicting a double-digit decline in HBM prices due to increased supply. This logic was flawed: it viewed the memory market as a simple commodity trade rather than a complex ecosystem. HBM is not just "extra" product; it is essentially multiple DRAM chips stacked vertically. By prioritizing HBM, manufacturers are effectively starving the market of generic DRAM, which prevents the supply glut many analysts fear.

Furthermore, the semiconductor industry has evolved. The "chicken games" of the past—where companies aggressively expanded factories and crashed prices—are over. Today’s firms, including the U.S.-based Micron Technology, are prioritizing strategic partnerships over raw volume. With $150 billion in remaining contract value (RPO) and strict penalty clauses, Big Tech customers cannot easily back out of these agreements even if market conditions shift.

Macroeconomic Headwinds and the Export Trap

While the chip sector is booming, the broader macro environment remains tricky. U.S. inflation data (PCE) has shown cooling, yet long-term Treasury yields remain high, reflecting deep market skepticism about the accuracy of current economic indicators. Investors are rightfully wary: the U.S. government’s recent methodology updates to how it calculates service prices have created a "trust gap" in official data.

South Korea’s own economy faces a unique "decoupling." While monthly exports recently smashed the $120 billion mark, this success is heavily concentrated in the semiconductor giants. The domestic retail and small-business sectors remain frozen under the weight of high interest rates and a strong dollar (fixed around 1,360 won to the dollar). Additionally, as South Korean firms face pressure to invest heavily in U.S. energy infrastructure—such as the Texas LNG projects—the government must balance political pressure from the Trump administration with the need to protect its own industrial interests.

Ultimately, the market is no longer a place for passive investors. With regulators finally mandating more transparent research from brokerages and high-tech firms tightening their grip on production, success now requires looking past the headlines. The "supercycle" isn't a tide that lifts all boats; it’s a selective environment that rewards those who track the flow of capital into front-line "frontier" companies rather than hoping for secondary, trickle-down benefits.
