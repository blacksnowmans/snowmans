Dual Scandals Plunge South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party into Factional Crisis

November 21, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has plunged into factional chaos as its leadership faces a mounting ethics crisis and a court injunction seeking to suspend Party Leader Kim Min-seok over alleged ties to a controversial religious group.

  • Ethics hearing locked in: The DPK Ethics Committee will officially deliberate on disciplinary measures against Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee on November 27 following leaked audio recordings.
  • Court injunction filed against party leader: Over 1,100 DPK members, alongside political commentator Lee Jong-won, filed a legal petition to suspend Party Leader Kim Min-seok over unresolved ties to Shincheonji, a controversial religious sect.
  • Internal civil war erupts: A coalition of 55 first-term DPK lawmakers is demanding Choi’s immediate resignation, triggering fierce pushback from party loyalists and high-profile figures.
  • Public outrage mounts: A recent poll revealed that 80.5% of South Koreans demand full disclosure of internal party investigations into the Shincheonji allegations.

Factional Warfare Hits a Breaking Point

The simultaneous crises surrounding Choi Min-hee and Kim Min-seok represent far more than individual ethical lapses; they signal a deep structural collapse in party discipline. Choi’s leaked recordings have ignited open warfare between 55 freshman lawmakers demanding her exit and senior loyalists—backed by influential progressive media voices like Kim Ou-joon—who view the push as an organized factional hit. The strain was on full display at the National Assembly, South Korea's parliament, on November 20, where Kim and Choi sat together in stony silence during a legislative session, highlighting how paralyzed the DPK leadership has become despite Kim's attempts to project normalcy through public outreach visits.

Legal Risks and the November 27 Deadline

The upcoming November 27 ethics hearing is shaping up to be a point of no return. If the committee issues a severe penalty against Choi, loyalist factions will erupt; if it grants leniency, reformist backbenchers may revolt over perceived hypocrisy—a double standard already called out by Rep. Kwak Sang-eon, son-in-law of the late former President Roh Moo-hyun. Coupled with an unprecedented lawsuit brought by over 1,000 rank-and-file party members to suspend their own party leader, the ruling party faces a psychological schism that threatens to derail its legislative agenda.
