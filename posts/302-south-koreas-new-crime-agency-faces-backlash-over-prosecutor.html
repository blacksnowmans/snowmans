South Korea's New Crime Agency Faces Backlash Over Prosecutor-Heavy Shortlist

September 29, 2026

  • What happened: South Korea is preparing to launch the Serious Crime Investigation Agency (SCIA), a new body designed to strip prosecutors of their investigative powers and separate investigation from indictment. However, leaked shortlists for its top jobs are dominated by former prosecutors.
  • Who is affected: The new agency's leadership, lawmakers pushing for prosecutorial reform, and the public, who have long demanded an end to the prosecutors' unchecked power.
  • What it changes: Critics argue that staffing the agency with former prosecutors turns the new body into "prosecution 2.0," threatening to replicate old investigative habits under a fresh coat of paint.

South Korea's ongoing saga over prosecutorial reform has hit a fresh roadblock. As the country prepares to launch the SCIA, media leaks have revealed that the frontrunners for its inaugural leadership roles are predominantly former prosecutors. This has triggered intense debate across the political and legal spheres, forcing a hard look at whether the new agency represents a genuine overhaul of the nation's powerful investigative apparatus or merely a bureaucratic reshuffle.

The core tension lies between institutional theory and practical administration. Proponents of appointing former prosecutors argue that veteran legal minds are necessary to stabilize a brand-new organization and ensure a smooth handover. But critics, including prominent liberal politicians, point out the glaring contradiction: if the goal of the SCIA is to break up the prosecution's monopoly on power and stamp out its institutional culture, handing the reins to the very people shaped by that culture defeats the purpose entirely. As commentators have noted, putting former prosecutors in charge of a reform agency is akin to asking a firefighter with a history of arson to put out a blaze.

Personnel vs. System: The Illusion of Reform

The debate goes far beyond a parlor game of guessing who will get the top job. Reports from major outlets like the Dong-A Ilbo have floated several high-profile names for the agency's chief and deputy chief roles. Yet, focusing strictly on individual personalities misses the forest for the trees. South Korea's reform history shows time and again that swapping out individuals without changing the underlying system achieves nothing.

A true separation of investigation and indictment requires a total break from past practices, ensuring that any new agency is subjected to rigorous democratic oversight rather than being absorbed into existing institutional cartels. If the SCIA simply inherits the DNA of the old prosecution service, the public will end up with a larger, more complex investigative bureaucracy rather than a fairer one. Ultimately, the question is not whether the new boss has a reformist personality, but whether the structural system forces them to answer to the public.
