Arts Council Korea Highlights Healing and Resistance in New Cultural Picks

October 22, 2026

Arts Council Korea has unveiled a curated selection of exhibitions, music, theater, and literature focused on collective healing, civic solidarity, and democratic memory.

  • The curation: Spanning the Gwangju Biennale, vintage funk-soul, contemporary theater, and subversive literature, the selection was assembled by veteran cultural critics and media leaders.
  • Key showcases: Highlights include the 5.18 May Mothers' House art exhibition in Gwangju, the historical musical Island: 1933–2019, and Ye So-yeon's award-winning novella The Dog's Revolution.
  • The institutional shift: The selections reflect a deliberate pivot under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung toward public-facing, socially critical art following years of political pressure and covert blacklisting during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office.

Reclaiming Civic History Through Grassroots Art

The centerpiece of the visual arts recommendation is the Gwangju Biennale, organized around Rainer Maria Rilke's imperative phrase, "You must change your life." Rather than leaning exclusively on academic institutionalism, the exhibition highlights raw, deeply personal works by the May Mothers' House—an association of women who lost children or family members to state violence during the 1980 May 18 Gwangju Democratization Movement. Though unpolished by conventional fine-art standards, their canvases document four decades of unresolved grief and survival, grounding national democratic heritage in intimate personal memory.

This focus on marginal voices carries directly onto the stage with Island: 1933–2019. The musical links the historical isolation of leprosy patients on Sorok Island—and the four-decade service of Austrian nurses Marianne Stoeger and Margaritha Pissarek—with modern families raising children with developmental disabilities. Along with literature like Victor Hugo's Les Misérables and Ye So-yeon's The Dog's Revolution, the picks consistently examine how ordinary citizens resist institutional exclusion.

Culture as Comfort and Critique

The cultural selections also highlight sensory respite in an accelerated digital economy. Cosmic Chips, a retro-soul project led by R&B veteran Jung-yup, offers warm 1970s analog grooves on their album Taste, providing an intentional acoustic antidote to hyper-compressed pop production. Even commercial theater like the Korean stage adaptation of Disney's Frozen illustrates changing demographics, drawing adults willing to pay premium prices for merchandise alongside young families.

Ultimately, these recommendations illustrate how South Korea's cultural sphere is consciously rebuilding its public role. By emphasizing themes of human dignity and social reconciliation, cultural institutions are moving past government-led public relations toward work that challenges complacency and invites civic self-reflection.
