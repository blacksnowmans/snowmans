South Korea's Prosecution Reform Faces Backlash Over Pro-Prosecution Appointments

September 09, 2026

South Korea's push to separate investigative and prosecutorial powers is unravelling from within, as critics point to the appointment of former prosecutors who once opposed the reforms to lead the newly created investigative bodies.

  • The core contradiction: Kim Ji-yong, a former senior prosecutor who vehemently fought against stripping prosecutors of their investigative powers, is now being floated to head the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA), South Korea's new anti-corruption and major crime investigative body.
  • Institutional loopholes: The newly drafted Prosecutors' Office structure maintains 2,292 prosecutors—the exact same number as before—raising fears that the powerful prosecutorial apparatus can easily revert to its old form under a future administration.
  • Overlapping control: A newly proposed "judicial control unit" would allow prosecutors to demand police or SCIA compliance and discipline them for non-compliance, effectively recreating the old command structure under a new name.
  • Political fallout: Critics and progressive lawmakers argue that staffing key reform agencies with ex-prosecutors makes a mockery of the system, echoing the recruitment missteps of past administrations.

The Paradox of Putting Prosecutors in Charge of Prosecution Reform

The entire premise of South Korea's long-sought judicial overhaul was to dismantle the unchecked concentration of power held by the nation's prosecutors, who historically wielded both sweeping investigation and indictment authorities. Yet, the blueprint for the newly reorganized system suggests that little has actually changed. With former prosecutors dominating the drafting committees, the review panels, and now the prospective leadership posts, critics argue the reforms have been hijacked by the very people they were meant to rein in.

The nomination of figures like Kim Ji-yong—known for actively opposing past efforts to strip prosecutors of direct investigative powers—to head the SCIA has been widely panned as an absurdity. Observers note that placing an institutional opponent at the helm of a reform agency virtually guarantees that the new body will lack the mandate and the will to fulfill its legislative purpose. Instead of breaking up institutional monopolies, the system appears to be recycling old structures under fresh paint.

A Familiar Déjà Vu for South Korean Democracy

For many reform advocates, the current appointments evoke bitter memories of past political miscalculations, when figures from the prosecutorial establishment were elevated to high office only to leverage their positions against the administration that appointed them. The recurrence of this pattern highlights a deep-seated vulnerability within South Korea's progressive political factions, who are accused of failing to push back decisively against bureaucratic self-preservation.

As the confirmation hearings approach, the government faces a stark choice. If it proceeds with installing career prosecutors to run institutions explicitly designed to check prosecutorial overreach, the entire legislative project risks turning into a hollow exercise—transforming what was supposed to be a historic curb on executive power into a superficial rebranding of the status quo.
