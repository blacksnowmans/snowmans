South Korea's Chief Justice Faces Backlash Over Attendance and Alleged Abuse of Power

September 30, 2026

  • South Korea's Chief Justice, Cho Hee-dae, is facing legal complaints and political fire over allegations of chronic tardiness and a failure to maintain basic attendance records.
  • The opposition Democratic Party and legal critics have filed complaints accusing Cho of abusing his power by overriding the National Court Administration's input.
  • The controversy escalated when the judiciary told parliament it lacks any system to track the arrival times of judges and staff, drawing widespread public ridicule.
  • The scandal places the nation's highest court under rare public scrutiny, eroding trust in a traditionally insulated judicial establishment.

South Korea's judiciary, long viewed as an untouchable bastion of authority, is facing an uncomfortable reckoning. At the center of the storm is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, whose alleged lax attendance habits have triggered not just political point-scoring, but formal legal complaints for abuse of power. For an institution that demands strict adherence to the law from the public, the spectacle of its leader dodging basic accountability is deeply damaging.

The controversy deepened when the court administration admitted to lawmakers that it keeps no records of when judges and officials arrive for work. In an era where ordinary office workers face biometric tracking and strict oversight, the judiciary's defense that it operates without an attendance system looks less like professional autonomy and more like an exemption from accountability. Critics, including opposition lawmakers analyzing vehicle logs to track the Chief Justice's movements, argue that no public servant should sit above basic workplace rules.

A Crisis of Trust and the Myth of Judicial Immunity

The fallout goes beyond a few missed mornings. It exposes a deep-seated elitism within South Korea's legal establishment. When opposition politicians draw parallels between the attendance scandals of former political figures and the head of the Supreme Court, it signals that the judiciary's institutional shield is wearing thin. Courts cannot credibly lecture society on the rule of law while treating internal transparency as optional.

Ultimately, this standoff is about whether the judiciary is willing to subject itself to the same democratic norms it applies to everyone else. If the courts continue to dismiss public concern as a misunderstanding of "professional independence," they risk accelerating a dangerous collapse in civic trust. Public institutions in a democracy answer to the public—and the message from critics is that even the highest judge in the land is merely a custodian of the law, not its master.
