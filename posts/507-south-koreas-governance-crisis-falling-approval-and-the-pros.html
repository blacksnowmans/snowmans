South Korea’s Governance Crisis: Falling Approval and the "Prosecution Power" Struggle

September 09, 2026

  • Falling Approval: President Lee Jae-myung’s administration faces a historic low, with approval ratings dropping to 40.9%, signaling a collapse in support from both key bases and moderate voters.
  • Communication Breakdown: The government is mired in controversy over alleged "media blackouts" aimed at critical voices like those on the popular news program News Factory, fueling public distrust.
  • "Decree Politics": The administration is bypassing legislative consensus by using presidential decrees to restructure the prosecution, effectively neutralizing reforms passed by the National Assembly.
  • Systemic Decay: Critics argue the government is prioritizing "personnel gardening"—placing loyalists in key positions—over addressing systemic issues, economic inequality, and youth disillusionment.

The Illusion of Reform and the "Prosecution Power" Grab

The core of the current political friction lies in a deeply cynical approach to reform. While the administration publicly advocates for the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, the internal reality—specifically the recent restructuring of the proposed Prosecution Service—tells a different story. By rebranding existing divisions within the Supreme Prosecutors' Office under new, softer titles like "Legal Science" departments, the administration has merely engaged in "signboard swapping" rather than actual structural change.

Most alarming is the creation of a "Judicial Control Department." This move allows the prosecution, an executive branch body, to effectively oversee police investigations and maintain its grip on high-stakes power. By utilizing administrative decrees to push these changes through instead of seeking parliamentary approval, the administration is bypassing democratic checks and balances. This "Machiavellian" strategy ensures that even if the Prosecution's direct investigative powers appear curtailed, they retain control over the most lucrative and influential legal tools, including asset recovery—a move that prioritizes the preservation of institutional power over genuine justice.

A Leadership Caught in a Cycle of "Self-Protection"

President Lee’s administration is currently trapped in a cycle of crisis management that only seems to dig a deeper hole. When faced with falling numbers, the leadership has resorted to blaming external figures rather than addressing their own "human and system errors." This defensive posture—seen in their attempts to balance conflicting political demands rather than taking a clear stand—has alienated the public, who perceive the administration as being disconnected from the economic realities of the average citizen.

The youth in particular, struggling with insurmountable asset gaps and a sense of "stolen opportunity," have turned their backs on the government. The administration’s preference for "personnel gardening"—appointing insiders rather than experts—has exacerbated the feeling that the government is more interested in personal power consolidation than national governance. Unless the government pivots from this top-down, opaque style of leadership to one based on transparency and democratic accountability, the current slide in popularity may prove to be the start of a broader political decline.
