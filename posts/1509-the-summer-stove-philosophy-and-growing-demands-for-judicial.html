The "Summer Stove" Philosophy and Growing Demands for Judicial Equality in Korea

October 26, 2026

Public frustration is mounting over a perceived "two-tier" justice system as recent audits reveal that law enforcement took zero aggressive investigative steps against conservative figures, even as the Lee Jae-myung administration emphasizes the need for total judicial reform.

  • Political Patience: The concept of Haru Dongseon (a stove in summer, a fan in winter)—popularized by the late President Roh Moo-hyun—is being cited to encourage political figures and the public to wait for the "right season" for justice.
  • Investigation Disparity: Parliamentary audits showed that the police and prosecution requested zero search warrants over 20 months regarding academic fraud allegations involving the daughter of independent lawmaker Han Dong-hoon.
  • Judicial Interference: Noh Kyung-pil, head of the National Court Administration (the administrative body of the Supreme Court), faces allegations of pressuring Supreme Court nominees to withdraw to manipulate court compositions.
  • Media Ethics: G1 Broadcasting, a regional affiliate of the national SBS network, sparked outrage by airing a three-minute prime-time news segment celebrating the hiking achievements of the station owner’s grandsons.

A Glaring Double Standard in Investigative Rigor

The contrast between the treatment of different political families has become a flashpoint for the Korean public. During the investigation into independent lawmaker Han Dong-hoon’s daughter—which involved allegations of ghostwritten papers and a suspicious donation of 50 laptops to a welfare center—police did not request a single search warrant in nearly two years. Furthermore, an attempt to seek international judicial cooperation with U.S. authorities was reportedly blocked by the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office. This stands in stark contrast to the dozens of raids and aggressive search warrants deployed against the family of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk for similar academic concerns.

Polls reflect this simmering resentment. Over 60% of respondents in recent surveys believe the court should extend the detention of Kim Keon-hee, the wife of the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. For many, these figures represent a "privileged class" that remained untouched during the previous disgraced administration. The Lee Jae-myung government and the ruling Democratic Party now face the challenge of proving that the rule of law applies equally, regardless of political lineage or past proximity to power.

Power Shifts and the Commercialization of Media

As the political "season" changes, prominent figures are maneuvering for position. Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul, has begun consolidating his influence within the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative opposition), holding private meetings with lawmakers following a recent favorable legal ruling. While Oh positions himself as a moderate alternative, critics argue his "rebranding" effort is an attempt to distance himself from the failures of the Yoon Suk-yeol era while maintaining the same conservative core. Meanwhile, Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party continues to receive disproportionate media attention despite lingering questions regarding his involvement in past political scandals.

Simultaneously, the private ownership of public airwaves is under scrutiny. The decision by G1 Broadcasting to use its news platform as a personal family album for its chairman’s grandsons highlights a broader trend of the "privatization of the press." When media outlets serve the interests of their owners rather than the public, they lose their function as a watchdog. Between judicial inconsistency and media capture, the Korean public’s current interest in the "Summer Stove" philosophy—the idea that something currently useless will eventually find its time—is less about nostalgia and more about a desperate hope that a season of genuine accountability is finally approaching.
