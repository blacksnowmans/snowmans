South Korea’s Opposition Urges Exam Delay Over AI Smart-Glass Cheating Fears

November 17, 2026

South Korea’s main conservative opposition People Power Party and the minor Reform Party warned on November 16 that artificial intelligence smart glasses could compromise the upcoming national college entrance exam, urging the government to consider postponing the test.

  • Suneung security alarm: Citing media reports that AI smart glasses have been used to cheat, People Power Party lawmaker Jang Dong-hyuk and Reform Party lawmaker Chun Ha-ram called for emergency inspections and a possible delay of the Suneung, South Korea’s high-stakes annual university entrance exam.
  • Harsher penalties sought: People Power Party floor leader Chung Jeom-sik demanded that AI cheaters be stripped of eligibility for all national exams, arguing that current metal detectors and visual proctoring cannot catch wearable devices.
  • High stakes for families: Postponing the exam shortly before test day would disrupt university admissions schedules nationwide and rattle tens of thousands of students who have spent years preparing.
  • Broader political clashes: Ahead of a November 19 legislative and budget showdown, the People Power Party rejected National Assembly Speaker Cho Jeong-sik’s proposal for constitutional reform next year as a political distraction, while the Reform Party pressed the presidential office over judicial fairness in cases involving President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Election fraud claims return: Broadcaster JTBC reported that lawmaker Joo Jin-woo accused the National Election Commission of pre-entering voter counts for 117 precincts 90 minutes before polls closed in past elections—allegations that prior official verifications and recounts have found to be baseless.

Fairness, Security, and Political Leverage

The debate over postponing the Suneung highlights both how quickly consumer AI hardware is outpacing South Korea’s traditional exam safeguards and how sensitive the issue of fairness remains in domestic politics. While critics view the delay talk as an opposition effort to put President Lee Jae-myung’s administration on the defensive, leaving test halls vulnerable to discreet wearable devices poses a genuine test for the Ministry of Education, which must quickly demonstrate credible technical countermeasures.

That battle over credibility extends well beyond education policy. As parties brace for budget negotiations and major court rulings around November 19, conservative circles are also grappling with internal divisions—highlighted by disputes among right-wing commentators over figure Cho Gab-je and lingering friction tied to Yoon Suk-yeol.

Hardline Narratives at Home and Abroad

Unverified election-fraud claims amplified by online outlets risk eroding public trust in democratic institutions rather than resolving legitimate oversight questions. Globally, the landscape for far-right messaging is similarly mixed: while France’s National Rally under Marine Le Pen has softened stances on issues such as same-sex marriage to appeal to mainstream voters, parties like Reform UK and Romania’s Alliance for the Union of Romanians (AUR) have recently struggled in polls and street mobilization, suggesting that polarizing rhetoric has limits when voters demand practical solutions.
