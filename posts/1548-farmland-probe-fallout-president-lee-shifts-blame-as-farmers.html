Farmland Probe Fallout: President Lee Shifts Blame as Farmers Reel

October 27, 2026

A nationwide crackdown on agricultural land speculation backed by President Lee Jae-myung has sparked an uproar across South Korea's countryside after rural land values cratered and banks began calling in farmers' loans.

  • What happened: The government poured 80 billion won (about $60 million) into a comprehensive nationwide inspection of farmland to weed out speculative buyers, but the move froze the rural real estate market and crushed land valuations.
  • Who is affected: Ordinary farmers who pledged farmland as collateral are facing urgent demands from lenders to repay portions of their principal after their property values dropped sharply.
  • The political trigger: When public sentiment soured, President Lee sparked outrage by distancing himself from the televised directive he championed, publicly remarking that Agriculture Minister Song Mi-ryung "made him do it."
  • What it changes: The misfire has deepened internal fractures inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), where leadership infighting and unverified internal scandals are eroding party discipline ahead of coming elections.

Administrative Overreach and Deflected Blame

The 80 billion won probe is a textbook case of heavy-handed policymaking backfiring on the very people it was supposed to protect. Instead of targeted enforcement in high-risk zones near the Seoul capital region, the administration opted for a sweeping national sweep. The resulting chill in land transactions directly hurt bona fide farmers: as farmland values sank, financial institutions demanded immediate debt paydowns on loans secured by that land. For farmers already struggling to service interest payments, demands to cough up tens of millions of won in cash have proved devastating.

Worse than the policy design itself has been the executive response. Having forcefully ordered full-scale inspections and forced land-sale mandates on live television during a cabinet meeting, President Lee abruptly ducked accountability once rural blowback intensified. Blaming Minister Song Mi-ryung in public destroys bureaucratic morale. When civil servants see a minister deployed as a shield for top-level policy failures, the entire administrative apparatus retreats into self-protective paralysis.

Ruling Party Fractures Widen

This administrative stumble coincides with toxic infighting within the ruling DPK. Party leader Kim Min-seok faces mounting criticism for poor leadership, particularly over unsubstantiated allegations that 100,000 members of the Shincheonji religious sect infiltrated party ranks during internal contests. Rather than providing clear evidence, leadership figures offered vague closed-door briefings, prompting open rebellion from key lawmakers including Han Min-soo, Choi Min-hee, and Lee Sung-yoon, who demand full disclosure or an immediate retraction.

With the current party leadership stalled and public ratings stagnant, voices inside the DPK are calling for an aggressive pivot. High-profile figures like hardliner Jung Chung-rae are gaining traction among core party loyalists who feel the current leadership lacks ideological clarity and political backbone. Unless the administration demonstrates real accountability on livelihoods and cleans up its own internal skirmishes, it risks alienating its base well before voters next head to the polls.
