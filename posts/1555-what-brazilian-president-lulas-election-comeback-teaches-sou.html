What Brazilian President Lula’s Election Comeback Teaches South Korea’s Ruling Party

October 27, 2026

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s dramatic election turnaround offers a stark warning for South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea: trying to appease political opponents and moderate voters only destroys trust among core supporters.

  • What happened: Political commentators analyzing President Lula da Silva's runoff victory argue that concrete policy incentives—such as free weekend public transit and debt relief for small business owners—unlocked a record 80.8% voter turnout to overturn a 2.2-million-vote first-round deficit.
  • What it means for Korea: The strategy offers a critical lesson for South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), who face stagnating popularity due to perceived hesitation on key reform pledges.
  • Who is affected: Progressive lawmakers and party strategists, who are being urged to abandon vague appeals to swing voters and instead deliver bold, consistent policy measures.

Deliver Concrete Incentives, Not Vague Compromises

Lula’s victory demonstrates that turnout is driven by tangible solutions rather than abstract moderation. By addressing the physical and economic barriers faced by young and working-class voters—promising free weekend transit, raising cash transfer benefits, and forgiving federal tax debt for small businesses—Lula mobilized three million new voters in the runoff. For South Korea's DPK, the takeaway is straightforward: supporters leave when they feel their economic burdens and expectations for social justice are ignored in favor of political caution.

Attempting to please everyone usually ends in pleasing no one. When progressive administrations retreat from promised reforms due to pressure from conservative media or financial markets—such as the market volatility and currency drops Brazil faced immediately after Lula’s win—they lose the trust of the electorate that brought them to power. The Lee administration’s caution in pressing forward with sweeping social and institutional reforms risks alienating its primary support base without winning over the conservative opposition People Power Party.

Abandoning the Myth of the Swing Voter

To regain political momentum, South Korea’s ruling party must move past the idea that moderate voters exist as a fixed, independent bloc. In reality, uncommitted voters are drawn toward strong political momentum and clear direction. President Lee built his political career on a reputation for decisive execution; attempting to play it safe to avoid conflict dilutes that defining strength.

Clinging to an ineffective strategy out of fear of admitting error is a classic sunk-cost fallacy. Just as Lula’s campaign revamped its strategy after a first-round setback, the DPK must abandon lukewarm compromises and focus on firm policy consistency. Re-engaging core supporters through clear reform action is the most effective way to build genuine governing power.
