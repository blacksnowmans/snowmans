How to Pack for the Camino de Santiago: The Golden Rules of Minimalist Trekking

September 07, 2026

For anyone tackling Spain's legendary 800-kilometer (approx. 500-mile) Camino de Santiago pilgrimage, the single most critical survival rule is simple: keep your backpack under 10% of your body weight.

  • The 10% Rule:
    •  A backpack weighing more than a fraction of your body weight turns into a physical anchor over 20 kilometers (approx. 12 miles) of daily walking, with hostel trash cans along the route routinely piled high with abandoned books and heavy jeans.
  • Footwear Over Fashion:
    •  Lightweight, ankle-supporting trekking shoes and double-layered toe socks are essential for preventing crippling blisters, far outperforming heavy-duty hiking boots.
  • Smart Gear Choices:
    •  Skip expensive, high-end sleeping bags that invite bedbug stress; a budget-friendly option around 20,000 won (about $15) and a full-coverage poncho are all you need for Spain's variable weather.
  • Pacing and Mindset:
    •  Discard rigid schedules and tourist gear—success on the trail relies on matching your own physical limits, utilizing local pharmacies for blister care, and accepting that excess items can always be bought locally or discarded.

Packing Light as a Metaphor for the Journey

The obsession with packing for "just in case" scenarios is the ultimate trap for first-time pilgrims. Every extra kilogram on your shoulders feels multiplied tenfold by midday, turning what should be a transformative trek into a test of sheer endurance. Veterans of the trail quickly learn to swap anxiety for optimism, trusting that if an item is truly necessary, local shops along the Camino will have it. Shedding unnecessary gear is not merely about lightening a load; it is a forced exercise in minimalism that teaches travelers how to distinguish between real needs and perceived anxieties.

Ultimately, the Camino de Santiago rewards those who walk light—not just in terms of luggage, but in mental attitude. By letting go of rigid timetables, competitive pacing, and the heavy expectations we carry from daily life, the 800-kilometer trek becomes less about physical conquest and more about personal reflection. You do not need to conquer the trail to finish it; you just need to carry enough to keep moving forward at your own pace.
