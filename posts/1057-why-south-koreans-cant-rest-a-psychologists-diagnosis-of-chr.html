Why South Koreans Can't Rest: A Psychologist's Diagnosis of Chronic Anxiety

October 13, 2026

South Koreans are trapped in a cycle of relentless anxiety and low happiness because they have spent decades answering questions set by others rather than learning to think for themselves.

  • The root cause: Official statistics show South Korea's happiness index remains near the bottom, driven by a hyper-fixation on wealth and a total lack of subjective rest.
  • The leisure paradox: National data shows that 92.1% of South Korean leisure time is spent on passive rest like TV or naps, while cultural and creative participation sits at just 4.2%.
  • The cultural trap: Society forces individuals into a rigid script—climbing the corporate ladder, chasing standardized wealth like owning real estate, and fearing non-conformity.
  • The way out: Experts argue that true relief requires physical displacement, stepping away from familiar environments, and building an active, curiosity-driven personal knowledge network.

South Korea’s relentless work-and-worry culture is often blamed entirely on economics—the default assumption is that more money solves everything. But as cultural psychologist Kim Jung-woon points out, an unreflective mind remains empty and miserable even with a full bank account. Society trains people to chase standard milestones like becoming a building owner, while utterly failing to teach them how to interpret their own lives. People mistake exhaustion for hard work and passive distraction for rest, leaving a population caught entirely between acute anxiety and paralyzing boredom.

Breaking this cycle requires treating knowledge and life not as a set of rules to memorize, but as raw material to edit. Drawing on the principles of the Bauhaus design movement—which Apple famously adapted by marrying technology with humanistic aesthetics—innovation comes from cross-editing existing elements through personal perspective, or "meta-cognition." Whether through physical isolation in an unfamiliar space or organizing ideas into digital knowledge tools, the prescription is clear: stop answering tests designed by society, and start asking your own questions.
