Inside South Korea’s Polling Crunch: President Lee Jae-myung’s Base Dilemma

October 26, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung faces a deepening political bottleneck as his approval ratings slip into the 30% range, caught between retaining a restive progressive base and reclaiming the moderate voters needed to govern effectively.

  • The Numbers: Poll trackers from major pollsters show positive ratings sliding from an early peak of 64% down to around 39%, alongside an unbroken upward trend in negative assessments.
  • The Core Weakens: Even the southwestern Honam region, the traditional bedrock of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, has seen support drop from historical highs near 89% to roughly 65%.
  • The Policy Drivers: Public blowback over real estate policies, volatile financial markets, controversial cabinet appointments, and fatigue over ongoing prosecutorial reforms have hardened voter disapproval.
  • The Media Pushback: In response to hostile coverage, the administration is overhauling public broadcast governance, introducing up to fivefold punitive damages for defamatory disinformation, and taking cabinet debates directly to the public via unfiltered livestreams.

The "Structural Minority" Trap

The administration’s central headache is strategic alignment. Media outlets and political factions disagree sharply over why the numbers are falling: staunch progressives insist the government has lost momentum by not pursuing judicial and prosecutorial overhaul aggressively enough, while pragmatists argue that appeasing hardline partisans alienates the broader middle class. Lee himself has recognized this danger, warning within his own camp that ideological purism is self-defeating: relying exclusively on the inner circle inevitably locks the party into a permanent, structural minority.

Unlike former presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, or Moon Jae-in, Lee does not inherit a monolithic factional dynasty within the Democratic Party. With a less entrenched organizational base, minor shifts among core loyalists inflict outsized political damage on the Blue House. When combined with economic frustrations over property regulations and the fallout from leveraged retail investments, the administration finds it difficult to build policy consensus across party lines.

Bypassing the Gatekeepers

To break this dynamic, the administration has leaned into institutional media reform and digital direct democracy. Landmark revisions to the Broadcasting Act have diversified the appointment process for public broadcast boards, reducing direct executive control. Concurrently, new punitive damage rules targeting false reports apply not only to traditional press outlets but also to prominent political YouTube channels with over 100,000 subscribers.

This legislative overhaul accompanies a radical shift in communications. Lee has turned ministerial briefings and cabinet debates into unedited YouTube livestreams, occasionally taking to social media himself to publicly challenge press reporting. While supporters champion this direct model as a necessary shield against biased framing, critics warn that top-down denunciations of reporters risk chilling independent oversight. Moving forward, the government’s challenge will not simply be fine-tuning polling snapshots, but demonstrating whether unfiltered digital outreach can offset real policy frustrations in the pocketbooks of swing voters.
