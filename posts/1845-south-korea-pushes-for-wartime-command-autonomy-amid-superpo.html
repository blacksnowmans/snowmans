South Korea Pushes for Wartime Command Autonomy Amid Superpower Pressure

November 02, 2026

Faced with Donald Trump’s disruptive unilateralism and Xi Jinping’s entrenched fourth term, progressive foreign policy voices in Seoul are urging President Lee Jae-myung to unilaterally reclaim wartime operational control from the United States rather than remain Washington's strategic subordinate.

  • The Command Standoff: The U.S. Senate Armed Services Committee's version of the 2027 National Defense Authorization Act added new prerequisites for transferring wartime operational control (OPCON), a move critics argue deliberately delays military sovereignty to keep South Korea as a forward base against China.
  • Calls for Unilateral Action: Progressive figures, including lawmaker Kim Byung-joo of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, argue Seoul must stop seeking American permission and simply declare independent command of its own forces.
  • Grassroots Mobilization: Activists have launched a "10 Million Peacemakers" campaign with a nominal lifetime fee of 1,000 won (about $0.75), organizing public support to restore the 2018 inter-Korean September 19 military tension-reduction agreement.
  • Global Turbulence: Spreading geopolitical crises—including Benjamin Netanyahu's failure to secure a 61-seat majority in Israel and Poland funneling 90% of military aid to Ukraine—risk spilling into Northeast Asia just ahead of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit.

Breaking the Dependency on Washington

The prevailing progressive view in Seoul holds that South Korea has spent decades acting merely as a "pacemaker" running on Washington’s schedule. Under the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is now imprisoned following his impeachment—Seoul's national security foundation deteriorated under subservient diplomacy. The Lee Jae-myung administration now faces intense domestic pressure to end this deference. Progressive analysts argue that the Pentagon uses shifting benchmarks to retain command authority over Seoul's well-equipped military for its own regional rivalry with Beijing, leaving South Korea exposed as a potential staging ground.

Shifting from Pacemaker to Peacemaker

To overcome this impasse, progressive strategists argue Seoul must build its own negotiating architecture. That starts with recognizing North Korea as an equal sovereign partner—addressing it by its formal name, Joseon—and taking the first step to resurrect the September 19 military pact that reduced border tensions before it was derailed. With Pyongyang deepening its military alignment with Moscow and Beijing, and showing little interest in resuming direct talks with Washington, waiting on American diplomacy has become an exercise in futility.

The upcoming APEC summit will serve as the first major test for President Lee's pragmatic, multi-vector diplomacy, which includes balancing bilateral engagements with China and navigating great-power rivalries. Rather than passively absorbing Trump’s transactional defense cost demands or Xi’s regional dominance, South Korea is being urged to set the regional agenda. The consensus among the administration's supporters is clear: peace cannot be outsourced to foreign capitals, but must be anchored in military self-reliance.
