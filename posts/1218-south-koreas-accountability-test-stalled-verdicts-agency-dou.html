South Korea's Accountability Test: Stalled Verdicts, Agency Doubts, and Seoul's Ferry Scandal

October 16, 2026

South Korea faces growing scrutiny over the integrity of its legal and municipal institutions following a contentious leadership appointment at the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, an alleged procurement kickback scheme in Seoul, and unexpected delays in Supreme Court proceedings involving Kim Keon-hee.

  • Contested agency appointment: Kim Ji-hyung was appointed head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—the investigative body established under prosecution reform—on the very day lawmakers issued a rare divided confirmation report citing evasive testimony regarding abuse of authority charges.
  • Seoul municipal controversy: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon faces accusations of negligence and concealment after awarding a lucrative Hangang River commuter ferry contract to an inexperienced firm without a dockyard, followed by a gutted audit submission to the state watchdog.
  • High-profile judicial delay: The Supreme Court en banc bench unexpectedly ordered additional hearings regarding three major allegations against Kim Keon-hee, the wife of impeached and imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, pushing back an anticipated ruling just as detention deadlines near.
  • Institutional credibility at stake: The developments test the reform agenda of President Lee Jae-myung's administration and deepen public doubts over administrative transparency and judicial independence.

Faltering Watchdogs and City Hall Contradictions

The rushed installation of Kim Ji-hyung casts a shadow over the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, a cornerstone of legal reform championed by President Lee's Democratic Party. Rather than launching assertive probes into top-tier corruption, early critiques indicate the fledgling agency has bogged itself down in administrative logistics and superficial internal audits. For an agency created to dilute traditional prosecutorial dominance, entering office burdened by ethics questions risks neutering its authority before major investigations even begin.

Concurrently, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon—a key figure in the conservative People Power Party—is struggling to contain fallout over the city's river bus contract. Awarding the contract to Gadeok Heavy Industries, a company with no prior shipbuilding record, at rates higher than earlier bidders prompted allegations of broker-driven kickbacks. Mayor Oh’s defense of memory lapses directly clashes with reported internal city documents detailing the illicit money flows. His referral of the case to the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI) backfired when the state auditor revealed the city’s petition deliberately omitted its own internal bribery findings, reducing the filing to a procedural screen.

Judicial Foot-Dragging at the Supreme Court

Compounding public unease is the Supreme Court’s handling of the en banc proceedings for Kim Keon-hee. Rescheduling hearings just days before a verdict window fuels suspicion that the judiciary is dragging its feet under political sensitivity. Because these cases involve high-stakes accountability surrounding the circle of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, any appearance of hesitation damages the judiciary's standing.

Ultimately, these episodes expose a recurring vulnerability in South Korean governance: institutions designed to check power repeatedly buckle under political calculations. Whether in newly minted investigative bodies, municipal procurement offices, or the highest court, dodging documented facts with convenient forgetfulness or procedural stalling only erodes civic trust when decisive clarity is needed most.
