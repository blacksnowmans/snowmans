Why South Korea's Top Dog Trainer and Child Expert Agree on Discipline

September 03, 2026

  • A viral crossover video featuring celebrity dog trainer Kang Hyung-wook and child psychologist Choi Min-jun highlights striking parallels between raising dogs and raising sons.
  • The experts argue that over-indulgence and micromanagement destroy independence, warning that parents often project their own anxiety onto children.
  • The briefing offers a fresh look at Korean parenting culture, shifting the focus from academic drilling to boundaries, patience, and side-by-side communication.

South Korea is famous for its hyper-competitive education fever, where parents often micromanage every minute of their children's lives. But a recent online video pairing Kang Hyung-wook—widely known as South Korea's "dog president" for his canine behavior TV shows—with Choi Min-jun, a prominent expert on raising boys, offers a radical shift in perspective. The core message is blunt: whether dealing with a puppy or a pre-teen boy, modern parents are making the same fundamental mistakes in the name of love.

The duo argues that true discipline is not about strict control or endless nagging, but about maintaining a proper distance. Parents often mistake unconditional permissiveness for love, which ultimately breeds entitlement. Instead of lecturing children who lack the developmental capacity to process long explanations, adults need to let them experience the natural consequences of their actions. Furthermore, as children grow past toddlerhood, parents must transition from strict rule-enforcers to strategic supporters, swapping face-to-face interrogations for side-by-side activities like gaming or building Lego.

Beyond Control: Letting Go of Parental Anxiety

At the heart of the experts' advice is an uncomfortable truth for many Korean parents: control stems from adult anxiety, not the child's incapacity. When parents fear seeing their own shortcomings, they lash out or box children into rigid expectations, labeling them as "difficult" rather than forming hypotheses about why they act out. Both Kang and Choi emphasize that a child's immaturity is not a flaw to be crushed through sheer force of will, but a natural process requiring patient, long-term endurance.

Ultimately, the crossover hits a cultural nerve by challenging the illusion of perfection. In a society obsessed with flawless academic credentials and rapid milestones, the experts frame childhood "deficits" not as disabilities, but as the very engine of growth. For international observers, it is a fascinating glimpse into how South Korea's intense parenting discourse is slowly evolving to value mental health, emotional boundaries, and independent resilience over rote obedience.
