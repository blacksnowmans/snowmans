South Korea's Ruling Party Falters as President Lee Jae-myung's Big Ambitions Face Systemic Flaws

October 10, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dropped to 33.1%, with negative ratings hitting 64.3%, following controversial cabinet nominations and policy missteps.
  • What it changes: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party, has seen its support fall below 40% for the first time under the current administration, tightening the gap with the conservative opposition, the People Power Party.
  • Who is affected: Young voters in their 20s and 30s are leading the backlash, showing higher disapproval rates than older demographics due to concerns over fairness, privilege, and political appointments.

Leadership Styles and the Trap of Political Idealism

Recent commentary from prominent South Korean political figure Rhyu Si-min has reignited public debate by contrasting the leadership of former President Moon Jae-in with that of current President Lee Jae-myung. While Moon focused on operating safely within established political camps to avoid the bitter conflicts faced by his predecessors, Lee operates with significantly larger political ambitions. Lee is known for taking calculated risks, crossing factional boundaries, and prioritizing practical results over rigid camp loyalty.

However, that high-ambition strategy has run into heavy resistance. Recent public friction—including controversies surrounding cabinet nominees and policy confusion over capital gains taxes—has alienated core supporters and triggered a sharp drop in public approval. Commentators argue that attempting to overhaul society too quickly without solid institutional checks often leads to severe political blowback, proving that grand political idealism frequently shatters against the wall of reality.

Internal Systems, Human Error, and Party Governance

Beyond individual missteps, critics argue that the Democratic Party suffers from deeper systemic flaws. When political allies commit errors, failing to replace them due to entrenched factional interests transforms a simple human mistake into a systemic failure. Furthermore, powerful ruling-party lawmakers often act as local kingmakers, tightly controlling candidate nominations for regional elections and reducing local representatives to compliant subordinates. This closed-loop power structure fosters an environment where transparency is lost and patronage thrives.

As the administration alienates the very voters who brought it to power in favor of newly arrived loyalist factions, the political costs are mounting. Unless the ruling party addresses these structural shortcomings and implements strict internal controls rather than relying merely on changing faces, the administration risks facing a severe reckoning in upcoming elections.
