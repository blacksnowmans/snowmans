South Korea Seeks 20-Year Sentence for Ex-Ruling Party Floor Leader Over Martial Law Attempt

October 01, 2026

  • Cho Eun-seok’s special prosecutor team has demanded a 20-year prison sentence for Daegu Mayor Choo Kyung-ho, the former floor leader of the conservative People Power Party, for his role in blocking the parliamentary vote to lift the December 3 emergency martial law.
  • Prosecutors presented CCTV footage showing Choo and ruling party leadership organizing efforts to disrupt the legislature's functions, including shifting meeting locations and stalling lawmakers while special forces attempted to enter the National Assembly.
  • The trial highlights deep divisions within South Korean conservatism, coming as the People Power Party faced a massive public backlash that saw daily party defections jump sixfold in the wake of the martial law decree.

The legal reckoning for South Korea's short-lived December 3 emergency martial law is reaching a critical juncture, placing the actions of the nation's political elite directly under judicial scrutiny. At the center of the latest proceedings is Daegu Mayor Choo Kyung-ho, who faces a heavy 20-year prison demand from prosecutors arguing that his efforts to paralyze the National Assembly crossed the line from partisan loyalty into active participation in an insurrection.

During his tenure as the ruling party's floor leader, Choo reportedly enforced strict party unity to shield the presidential office, utilizing a top-down chain of command that treated presidential directives as unwritten orders. While defenders frame his actions as routine political coordination, the special prosecutors' use of internal surveillance footage paints a darker picture: a calculated attempt to block lawmakers from reaching the floor to repeal martial law, effectively aiding an unconstitutional power grab.

This trial is more than a retrospective look at a chaotic night; it serves as a crucial test of accountability for public officials who choose institutional obedience over constitutional duty. As the judiciary weighs its verdict, the proceedings underscore a vital lesson for South Korea's democracy: the survival of constitutional order depends entirely on elected representatives refusing to serve as instruments of authoritarian overreach.
