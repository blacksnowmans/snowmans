South Korea’s Aging Crisis Forces 70-Somethings to Become Certified Caregivers for Ailing Spouses

October 19, 2026

South Korea’s worsening eldercare deficit is pushing seniors in their 70s and beyond to obtain official caregiving licenses simply to nurse their sick spouses at home, exposing the limits of a family-dependent welfare model.

  • The "elder-care-elder" reality: Surging numbers of elderly Koreans—even nonagenarians—are qualifying as certified care workers (yoyangbohosa) to tend to partners suffering from dementia or terminal illnesses.
  • State stipends for domestic care: Under existing welfare programs, the government offers modest financial stipends to individuals who hold the certification and nurse an ailing spouse, marking a first step toward compensating unpaid household labor.
  • Emotional and physical toll: Ward-level care remains hobbled by public perception treating caregiving as low-status domestic help rather than specialized emotional and physical labor, fueling caregiver burnout.
  • Demands for public reform: Observers are urging the Lee Jae-myung administration to speed up universal coverage for caregiving under National Health Insurance and expand hospital-managed integrated care services to curb catastrophic household bankruptcies.

The Strain of Self-Reliant Eldercare

In South Korea, caregiving has long been viewed through a Confucian lens as an individual family duty. But as the country ages at record speed, that tradition has turned into a grinding crisis of "elder-caring-for-elder" (no-no ganbyeong). Faced with monthly private caregiver fees that reach thousands of dollars and institutional facilities they cannot afford or trust, seniors are hitting the books. Men and women in their 70s, and occasionally in their 80s and 90s, are studying late into the night for national licenses so they can legally provide home care and draw modest state stipends.

While government pay for spousal care validation reflects meaningful public recognition of domestic labor, it also exposes how much the welfare state leans on exhausted partners. Spouses who should be resting in retirement instead spend their final years lifting, cleaning, and managing chronic ailments, bearing the heavy physical toll of geriatric nursing alone.

From Family Sacrifice to State Obligation

The strain inside medical wards illustrates why institutionalized professionalization is overdue. Patients facing terminal diagnoses often default to severe denial and fear of losing independence, lashing out at hospital staff or refusing external help. Bridging that divide requires sophisticated emotional labor and patience—repeatedly reaching out until a patient lowers their guard. When families view caregivers simply as hired hands paid by the day, the dynamic curdles into friction and fatigue on both sides.

Treating caregiving as cheap, ad-hoc manual labor has driven skilled workers away and left families vulnerable to "caregiving bankruptcy" and extreme emotional distress. Shifting this load from private households to the state—via hospital-run integrated nursing-care services and health insurance coverage—is no longer an idealistic welfare proposal, but an urgent structural necessity for an aging society.
