South Korea Mobilizes Local Resources to Help Students Navigate Major Education Overhaul

November 17, 2026

Local governments and education offices across South Korea held a wave of career fairs on November 16 to help middle school students prepare for the nationwide implementation of the High School Credit System and a revamped university admissions process set for 2028.

  • Regional hubs such as Daejeon and Imsil held specialized briefings to address parental anxiety over the 2028 admissions reform, which will fundamentally change how high school grades and entrance exams are evaluated.
  • The Incheon Metropolitan Office of Education, the regional authority overseeing schools in the port city, hosted over 500 attendees at a joint fair for vocational high schools to promote specialized career paths and industry partnerships.
  • Public sector expansion: Municipalities are increasingly moving into territory once dominated by expensive hagwons (private academies), offering AI-based career counseling and expert "talk concerts" to reduce educational inequality.
  • Legislative backing: New measures, such as a recently passed ordinance in Uijeongbu, a city north of Seoul, are providing the legal and financial framework to make these career support services a permanent fixture of local governance.

Decentralizing Admissions Strategy

The rush to organize these events highlights the high stakes of the 2028 University Entrance Exam Reform. This overhaul will transition the country toward a flexible, credit-based high school system similar to university enrollment. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, ensuring a smooth transition is a priority to maintain public trust in the state-led education system. By decentralizing these fairs to rural areas like Imsil-gun and smaller cities like Danyang, the government is attempting to close the chronic information gap between the affluent districts of Seoul and the rest of the country.

The high attendance—exemplified by the 500 people who gathered in Incheon—suggests a shift in how South Korean families consume educational data. Historically, the most "elite" tips for entering top universities were sold behind the closed doors of private consulting firms. Now, the public sector is attempting to act as the primary consultant. In Daejeon’s Seo-gu district, this included using Artificial Intelligence to help students map out potential career paths, a move intended to modernize the image of public school counseling.

Beyond the Classroom

The briefings also signal that high school selection is now viewed as the "first stage" of university entrance. Under the new credit system, the specific subjects a high school offers can determine a student’s eligibility for certain college majors. This has forced local offices of education to become more aggressive in marketing their schools. In Gyeongsangbuk-do, the provincial education office even issued new guidebooks to ensure that the "gap period" for 9th graders—the weeks after final exams but before high school starts—is used for career mapping rather than just leisure.

This localized approach also allows for more targeted support. Events held on the 16th included a media-focused camp in Gongju and a nursing career program in Gyeonggi Province, as well as a specialized career festival for students with disabilities. By integrating career exploration with the administrative reality of admissions, South Korean officials hope to curb the country’s extreme dependency on private tutoring while managing the largest educational shift in a generation.
