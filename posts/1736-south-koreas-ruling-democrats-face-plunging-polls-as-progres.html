South Korea’s Ruling Democrats Face Plunging Polls as Progressive Base Splinters

October 30, 2026

Approval ratings for South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) have tumbled to post-inauguration lows, exposing severe structural cracks as both moderate swing voters and core progressives abandon the administration.

  • A tightening margin: A late-October Gallup Korea poll puts the ruling DPK at 35% and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), at 30%—closing the gap to within the margin of error, while President Lee’s disapproval rating surged to 49% against 37% approval.
  • Defections to the left: The minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, led by newly elected leader Shin Jang-sik, has rebounded to 9% nationally and double digits among voters in their 40s and 50s by taking an uncompromising stance on judicial and prosecutorial reforms.
  • Legislative jeopardy: With 47% of respondents in Gallup polling favoring an opposition victory in the next general election to check the administration, liberal vote-splitting threatens the DPK’s crucial 180-seat threshold needed to fast-track bills and overcome legislative filibusters.
  • Internal isolation: Influential liberal commentators, including author Rhyu Si-min, have publicly warned that the administration is shutting out diverse voices and retreating into an echo chamber dominated by an insular inner circle.

Echoes of Past Defeats and Coalition Decay

The Democratic Party’s sliding poll numbers reflect more than normal mid-cycle fatigue; they signal an unraveling of the progressive coalition. In Gallup’s tracking, non-affiliated voters now make up 28% of the electorate, signaling that disillusionment with the government is swelling the uncommitted ranks. Observers increasingly draw parallels to the disastrous 2021 Seoul mayoral by-election, where unaddressed voter discontent produced a crushing double-digit loss for the DPK despite seemingly close polling leading up to the vote.

The administration's communication strategy bears much of the blame. Following party leadership changes under Chairman Kim Min-seok and the appointment of close loyalists to official party media channels, the ruling camp has increasingly sidelined internal critics. Commentators warn that by adopting a siege mentality and relying on hardline loyalist commentary, the administration is dangerously repeating the insular governance that characterized the political self-destruction during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term.

The Threat of Progressive Vote-Splitting

The immediate danger to the ruling party comes not only from the conservative opposition, but from its own flank. During previous elections, the Rebuilding Korea Party acted as an auxiliary icebreaker for the mainstream liberals. Under Shin Jang-sik, however, the party has embraced an independent posture, criticizing the DPK as sluggish and overly cautious on abolishing remaining prosecutorial investigative powers.

If the Rebuilding Korea Party runs independent candidates in the densely packed Seoul metropolitan area—where general election races are frequently decided by margins of 1% to 2%—the progressive split will hand victory to the People Power Party. Voters who mobilized to end political chaos expect concrete results, not defensive politics; without genuine internal dialogue and tangible legislative deliveries, President Lee faces a rapidly closing window to keep his ruling majority intact.
