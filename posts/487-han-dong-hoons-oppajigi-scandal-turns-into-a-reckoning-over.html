Han Dong-hoon’s 'Oppajigi' Scandal Turns into a Reckoning Over Prosecutor Tactics

September 09, 2026

  • What happened:
    •  Han Dong-hoon, an independent lawmaker and former prosecutor, has been pushing a political offensive in South Korea's parliament dubbed the "Oppa Gate," targeting the main opposition Democratic Party.
  • What it changes:
    •  Critics argue that leaked investigative files and transcripts he is using actually implicate figures across the political spectrum—including members of the ruling People Power Party and Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon—backfiring on Han.
  • Who is affected:
    •  The clash highlights ongoing battles over prosecutorial overreach, leading to calls for a special counsel investigation into alleged fabricated indictments.

South Korean politics is once again fixated on a high-stakes leaking scandal. Lawmaker Han Dong-hoon, a former high-flying prosecutor and former Justice Minister who has long traded on his crime-fighter image, has taken to parliament to accuse opposition politicians of corruption. But rather than exposing the Democratic Party, the maneuver has boomeranged. Investigators and commentators point out that the source material—confidential investigative records and transcripts—reveals a much wider web of alleged lobbying that touches conservative politicians and high-profile figures like Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, exposing Han to accusations of recycling his old prosecutorial habits of leaking selective files to shape public opinion.

The controversy shines a harsh light on how investigative agencies in Seoul handle digital evidence, specifically the unauthorized extraction of files from sealed digital forensics databases known as "Dinet." Critics argue that prosecutors have routinely bypassed legal safeguards to dig up embarrassing personal details and build politically motivated cases against rivals. With plans underway to launch a new Major Crimes Investigation Agency to strip prosecutors of direct investigative overreach, the debate is moving past political point-scoring to a fundamental question: Can South Korea's justice system be trusted to operate fairly without partisan manipulation?

The Echoes of Prosecutorial Politics

For years, critics have accused South Korea's prosecution service of weaponizing investigative powers to target political opponents, using selective leaks to inflict maximum reputational damage before cases even reach trial. Han's recent parliamentary offensive is viewed by detractors as a continuation of these tactics. Yet, as details of the underlying transcripts emerge, the narrative of a neat opposition-only scandal falls apart, pulling in figures from across the aisle and raising urgent legal questions about how classified case files ended up in a politician's hands.

Ultimately, this latest political dogfight is accelerating calls for sweeping judicial reform, including a special counsel probe into alleged fabricated indictments and prosecutorial misconduct. As the public grows weary of political theater designed to ruin rivals rather than solve everyday problems, the pressure is mounting to replace arbitrary prosecutorial power with strict, transparent systems of accountability.
