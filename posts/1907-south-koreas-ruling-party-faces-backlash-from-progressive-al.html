South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Backlash from Progressive Ally Over Merger Talks

November 03, 2026

Tensions are erupting within South Korea’s ruling liberal bloc as the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive ally, sharply criticized the ruling Democratic Party of Korea over manipulative merger talks and exclusionary parliamentary rules.

  • Negotiations freeze: Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP) Secretary-General Jeong Chun-saeng revealed that the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) cut off communication following two high-level meetings in September and October, accusing the ruling party of engaging in insincere media spin.
  • Dispute over parliamentary status: DPK leader Kim Min-seok proposed lowering the threshold for forming an official parliamentary negotiation group from 20 seats to 15—a move RKP views as a calculated trap to keep its 12 lawmakers sidelined without independent legislative privileges.
  • Backlash against 'absorption': Remarks by DPK leadership suggesting an "absorption merger" and demanding pre-conditions on dual party membership have provoked anger within RKP leadership, including party head Shin Jang-sik.
  • Public sentiment: Recent polls indicate a clear majority of voters—and even a plurality of DPK supporters—prefer both parties to maintain independent paths rather than execute a forced merger.

The 15-Seat Trap and Parliamentary Privileges

At the heart of the standoff is the rule governing parliamentary negotiation groups. Currently set at 20 seats—a threshold elevated during the authoritarian regime of the 1970s to suppress opposition voices—negotiation status grants political parties crucial state subsidies, policy research staff, and a seat at the table for main legislative scheduling. While RKP demands restoring the pre-authoritarian benchmark of 10 seats so its 12 lawmakers can participate as a full bargaining bloc, the DPK’s sudden proposal of a 15-seat floor effectively keeps RKP dependent on the ruling party’s goodwill.

Risks to President Lee’s Progressive Coalition

The rift exposes deep mistrust within the reformist camp supporting President Lee Jae-myung. Instead of building a broad coalition to permanently move past the political chaos seen during the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached and is currently imprisoned—the DPK’s top-down push for absorption risks alienating key allies. Forcing a minor party into submission via media pressure and arbitrary numerical barriers damages the mutual trust required for long-term governance.

As RKP leader Shin Jang-sik warned, failing to build an inclusive alliance places future political milestones—such as the 2028 parliamentary elections and the 2030 presidential race—at risk. If the ruling DPK truly intends to champion democratic reform under President Lee, it must abandon high-handed tactics and treat its coalition partners as equal stakeholders rather than targets for political conquest.
