South Korea’s Ruling Party Ensnared in Cult Infiltration Rumors Amid Factional Mudslinging

October 29, 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party is suffering a steep drop in public support as internal factions trade unproven allegations that a controversial religious sect secretly infiltrated its membership.

  • What happened: Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok publicized unverified claims that tens of thousands of members from Shincheonji, a fringe religious group, secretly joined the party, igniting fierce internal conflict.
  • Why it lacks proof: Cross-checking party rolls against religious membership lists is legally impossible under privacy laws, and past law enforcement probes found no evidence of organized Shincheonji infiltration into the party.
  • Political cost: Mired in factional infighting and mounting friction with President Lee Jae-myung's executive office, the ruling party's approval rating sank to 31.5%, falling nearly nine percentage points behind the main opposition People Power Party.
  • Who is affected: Ruling party lawmakers face severe voter backlash for prioritizing internal power struggles over pressing economic hardships facing working-class citizens.

Factional Warfare Masks a Lack of Evidence

The infiltration narrative is a political weapon born out of internal rivalry following the party's August national convention. By invoking the phantom of Shincheonji—a group deeply unpopular among the South Korean public—competing factions within the Democratic Party are seeking to vilify and purge political opponents. However, because the leadership cannot legally obtain or verify secret membership rolls, its persistent hints of a conspiracy have metastasized into an embarrassing self-inflicted wound.

This infighting has created serious coordination breakdowns between party headquarters and President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. Key personnel decisions and policy rollouts have been marred by miscommunication, signaling growing alienation between the presidential office and party leadership. Rather than steering legislative momentum toward economic relief and judicial reform, party leaders remain distracted by a witch hunt inside their own ranks.

Electoral Danger and Public Disconnect

The real-world consequence of this political sideshow is a sharp shift in public sentiment. Recent polling shows the conservative opposition People Power Party opening a commanding lead over the ruling party. For ordinary citizens struggling with persistent inflation and rising living costs, the ruling party’s preoccupation with unproven conspiracy theories demonstrates a alarming disconnect from everyday economic realities.

If the Democratic Party fails to halt its internal warfare and refocus on governance, it risks further alienating moderate voters and undermining President Lee's policy agenda. Surging support for alternative progressive movements like the Rebuilding Korea Party serves as a clear warning: voters' patience with ruling-party infighting has run out.
