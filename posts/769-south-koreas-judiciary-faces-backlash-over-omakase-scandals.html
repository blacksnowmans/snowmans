South Korea's Judiciary Faces Backlash Over Omakase Scandals and Hidden Perks

October 06, 2026

South Korea’s top judicial leaders are facing intense public fury after parliamentary audits revealed lavish dining at expensive restaurants funded by taxpayers, alongside systemic efforts to shield high-ranking officials from accountability.

  • What happened: Lawmakers revealed that South Korea's Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and other senior judicial figures allegedly used manipulated receipts to write off high-end meals—such as luxury omakase—and distributed untracked monthly cash stipends to senior judges.
  • The systemic cover-up: Past probes into prosecutorial misconduct, including a disputed assault indictment involving prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong and blocked phone forensic investigations, have resurfaced, highlighting deep-seated political favoritism within the legal establishment.
  • Who is affected: Public trust in South Korea’s judiciary and legal institutions has plummeted, ramping up pressure on the main opposition Democratic Party to pursue sweeping judicial reforms, including the total abolition of the National Court Administration.

The High Cost of Judicial Impunity

For ordinary South Koreans grappling with economic hardship, watching top legal officials enjoy luxury dining on the public dime feels like a cruel joke. Recent parliamentary disclosures laid bare how senior officials skirted spending caps—which require detailed reporting on expenses exceeding 50,000 won (about $35)—by splitting bills and forging receipts. Meanwhile, Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae brushed off responsibility during a National Audit hearing, claiming he merely attended the meals to encourage staff and that subordinates handled the finances.

This institutional arrogance extends far beyond expensive dinners. The judiciary has long operated as a protected caste, shielded from the transparency demanded of ordinary citizens. Revelations that Supreme Court justices received untracked monthly cash allowances—amounting to roughly 2 million won (about $1,400) per person—highlight an entrenched culture of privilege that bypasses standard oversight. When the country’s highest legal authorities treat public coffers like personal petty cash while demanding strict moral compliance from lower-ranking officials, the hypocrisy is glaring.

The Urgency of Structural Reform

The latest scandals have made one thing clear: incremental changes will no longer suffice. Critics argue that real accountability requires dismantling the National Court Administration to strip judges of unchecked administrative powers, alongside expanding citizen participation systems like jury trials. Yet, the main opposition Democratic Party has often moved too cautiously, fearing political blowback.

As debates flare over constitutional interpretations—such as the balance of power in appointing Supreme Court justices between the Chief Justice and the president—the public mood is shifting. South Korea’s judiciary can no longer hide behind closed courtroom doors. If lawmakers fail to seize this window for aggressive reform, the erosion of public trust in the country's rule of law may prove irreversible.
