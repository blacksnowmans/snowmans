South Korea Approves Special Counsel Bill on Alleged Prosecutorial Abuses, Sparking Fierce Conservative Backlash

November 18, 2026

South Korea’s Cabinet has approved the promulgation of a contentious special counsel bill targeting alleged "fabricated indictments" by prosecutors, triggering street protests and impeachment threats from the conservative opposition.

  • What happened: The Cabinet passed the promulgation bill by proxy vote on November 17 while President Lee Jae-myung was overseas on official business.
  • The partisan split: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea praised the legislation as an essential step to eradicate prosecutorial overreach, while the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) condemned it as an unconstitutional "coup" and an "escape bill" engineered to shield the president from trial.
  • Immediate retaliation: PPP lawmakers staged street rallies outside the presidential office and marched to the Central Government Complex in Seoul, pledging to seek the dismissal—and potential impeachment—of Prime Minister Han Seong-sook and Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin.
  • The broader fallout: The political warfare threatens to stall critical legislative deadlines, including the review of the national budget and debates on constitutional reform.

A Polarized Battle Over Judicial Accountability

According to domestic news reports, the ruling Democratic Party views the special counsel bill as a necessary corrective against what it describes as "political prosecutors" who abused investigative authority in past cases. Democratic lawmakers maintain that uncovering fabricated charges is the baseline for judicial reform, arguing that conservative resistance to the inquiry remains unjustified.

Opposition parties, however, frame the move as an executive power grab designed to halt judicial scrutiny of the president. PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok and floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik led their party’s lawmakers in public marches, accusing the administration of creating an imperial, untouchable presidency and demanding that pending trials against President Lee resume. Figures from smaller factions, such as Reform Party lawmaker Cheon Ha-ram, similarly assailed the government, arguing that the administration is expending legislative capital solely to protect the head of state from imprisonment.

Parliamentary Deadlock and Mounting Confrontation

The conflict has now moved beyond rhetoric into formal legislative retaliation. The PPP announced plans to submit dismissal motions against Prime Minister Han—whom the party termed an accomplice to the measure—and Education Minister Choi immediately following the annual College Scholastic Ability Test (Suneung). The conservative leadership warned that it will initiate formal impeachment proceedings against both officials if the executive branch rejects the dismissal requests, while concurrently proposing a special bill to combat test irregularities.

With both sides entrenched, the National Assembly faces significant paralysis. Observers note that the special counsel dispute risks becoming a political black hole, crowding out regular governance. Unless both the Democratic Party and the PPP find common ground inside parliament rather than on the streets, critical end-of-year priorities—chief among them the national budget review—will remain frozen.
