South Korea’s Local Birth Subsidies Soar as National Budget Stability Is Questioned

November 12, 2026

South Korean municipalities are raising cash incentives to combat severe demographic decline—with the city of Gunsan pledging up to 15 million won ($11,000) per child—even as lawmakers warn that national child welfare funds could face depletion within three years.

  • Gunsan's massive cash incentives: The southwestern city of Gunsan plans to introduce a grant in July 2025 paying 10 million won (about $7,300) for a first child and up to 15 million won ($11,000) for a third.
  • Seoul's structural overhaul: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has bundled 111 initiatives under the city's "Child-Accompanying Project," focusing on work-life balance, flexible evening daycare, and priority housing.
  • National budget alarm: Parliamentary auditing has raised red flags over placing statutory national child allowances inside a discretionary "Future Response Fund," warning the budget could dry up in three years without extra tax revenues.
  • Strict application deadlines: Parents are urged to apply for the central government's monthly parent allowance of 1 million won ($730) within 60 days of birth to avoid forfeiting retroactive payments.

Municipal Cash Competitions vs. Structural Infrastructure

The aggressive payout scheme in Gunsan, located in North Jeolla Province, reflects growing desperation among regional cities fighting population drain. While direct cash injections provide vital short-term relief to new parents facing high costs for infant care, critics warn of an unsustainable "population poaching" war between neighboring towns. Localities throwing around cash grants risk exhausting municipal treasuries without increasing overall national fertility rates.

In contrast, Seoul is taking a systemic approach. Rather than relying purely on localized cash giveaways, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s mega-project targets root causes by offering priority housing opportunities to expanding families and creating late-night and emergency daycare programs. The municipal goal is to keep working mothers in the labor force while alleviating the skyrocketing real estate costs that deter young couples from having children in the capital.

Fiscal Security and National Responsibility

At the central government level, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces pressure to reform how child benefits are financed. The current policy dispute stems from budget structures designed during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s term, which shifted core child allowances into a special "Future Response Fund" rather than drawing from the permanent general account. Lawmakers argue that relying on volatile tax revenues puts statutory family benefits at risk, creating unnecessary anxiety for young parents.

Although the Ministry of Economy and Finance maintains that fund revenues remain manageable, welfare advocates insist that childcare support must not function like a fiscal gamble. For South Korea to rebuild trust among young families, the central government must secure these allowances under general accounting, guaranteeing that national support stays uninterrupted regardless of broader economic fluctuations.
