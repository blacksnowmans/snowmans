The Rise of "Sonjeol" Culture: Why We're Cutting People Off and What It Costs Us

September 03, 2026

South Korea’s growing habit of instantly cutting off friends, family, and acquaintances—known locally as sonjeol—reflects a society hiding behind self-imposed isolation rather than facing the messiness of human connection.

    The trend: Unfollowing on social media, ghosting group chats, and deleting phone numbers has become the casual go-to response to interpersonal friction. The motive: People are treating relationships like Lego blocks or cost-benefit equations, preferring solitude to avoid the risk of being hurt. The hidden cost: Far from being a sign of maturity or independence, this rapid "cutting off" is often a defense mechanism—an avoidance strategy that leaves our lives shallower and ourselves increasingly isolated.

Trading Connection for Convenience

Modern life in South Korea is exhausting, and many view cutting people off as an efficient way to protect their energy. But this transactional approach treats human connection like a contract. When we dump people at the first sign of friction—fearing rejection or the pain of being vulnerable—we block our own opportunities for real trust. As noted in discussions surrounding psychological advice from figures like Professor Lee Ho-sun, this behavior often stems from a fear of getting hurt rather than genuine independence.

The damage goes beyond friendships. The same lack of healthy boundaries fuels deep-seated conflicts elsewhere, such as traditional family tensions or misplaced anxieties over money and retirement. When we let financial stress or rigid family expectations dictate our lives, we risk losing our bonds with others entirely. Instead of retreating behind high walls or viewing money and people as mere tools to manage, the key lies in setting clear, flexible boundaries while keeping the door open to genuine human empathy.
