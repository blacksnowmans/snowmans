Why a $2 Million Portfolio Shift to US Stocks is the Play for Korean Investors

September 08, 2026

  • The Core Argument: With the South Korean Won often volatile and local markets hampered by structural issues, investors should hold 30–50% of their wealth in US-denominated assets.
  • The Catalyst: A temporary cooling in the US dollar—driven by corporate bond buybacks and falling yields—creates an ideal window to shift capital into US-listed stocks.
  • The Targeted Sectors: Instead of broad index tracking, pivot toward memory semiconductor firms (like Micron) that are positioned to meet the massive chip demand of AI, humanoids, and space-based data centers.
  • The Strategy: Use "defensive" assets like the Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD) as a safety net, switching into aggressive growth stocks only when market volatility indices (VIX) spike above 30.

For many South Korean investors, watching the stock market these days is a lesson in frustration. While US markets hit record highs, local portfolios often feel trapped in a cycle of stagnation or decline. Son Ju-bu, a full-time investor who manages a portfolio of about $2.2 million (3 billion KRW) and achieved significant returns this year, argues that this pain is largely self-inflicted by an over-reliance on the Korean Won. Drawing from his experience as an expatriate in Russia during a currency collapse, he warns that keeping 90% of your assets in one domestic currency is a dangerous gamble that ignores the necessity of global diversification.

The case for moving to the US market goes beyond currency hedging. Son points out that the US offers a level of shareholder-friendly culture and geopolitical stability that the Korean market—often criticized for its treatment of minority shareholders—lacks. By utilizing US-based tax-deferral systems and focusing on the structural "super-cycle" of AI and robotics, long-term investors can treat the US market as a compounding engine rather than a speculative casino.

Beyond the "Supply Glut" Myth

Skeptics often point to production increases from companies like SK Hynix—South Korea’s leading memory chip maker—to argue that a "supply glut" will soon crash the semiconductor market. Son dismisses this as a failure to recognize the shift in demand. He notes that a single humanoid robot requires the memory equivalent of 100 iPhones. When combined with the massive data needs of SpaceX’s satellite data centers, the projected demand for memory chips dwarfs the industry's current expansion plans.

Consequently, Son has shifted his portfolio aggressively. While he once held a massive stake in Nvidia, he has rebalanced his holdings to favor memory semiconductor firms, which he views as the "spine" of AI infrastructure. He treats these investments as permanent holdings, choosing to ignore daily price fluctuations. In his view, the "Volatility Index" (VIX) should be an investor's compass: when the market panics and the VIX crosses 30, it is time to sell defensive assets like dividend ETFs and buy into high-growth potential.

Finally, Son warns against the "trap" of Korean government-sponsored tax-advantaged accounts like the Pension Savings Fund. While they offer tax breaks, they come with rigid withdrawal caps—if you withdraw more than about $11,000 (15 million KRW) annually, you lose your tax benefits. For serious investors, prioritizing flexible, globally diversified US assets over restrictive local tax-sheltered products may be the key to ensuring both liquidity and growth in retirement.
