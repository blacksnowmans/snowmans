The Hollow Personnel Reshuffle: Why Korea’s "Blind" Appointments Fail the Public

August 31, 2026

The South Korean government’s recent appointments for the Presidential Secretariat and ministerial roles have been met with widespread indifference, signaling a deeper crisis in how the current administration manages state affairs. Instead of presenting a clear vision for the country, these appointments rely on generic profiles, leaving the public to guess at the government’s true direction.

  • Lack of Vision: Government appointments are being released without clear policy goals or national objectives, reducing high-level roles to mere "name tag" swaps.
  • The Hearing Facade: Public confirmation hearings have increasingly become theater, acting as "bait" to distract from the real, behind-the-scenes personnel planning.
  • Information Asymmetry: The government demands alternatives from critics while withholding the data and risk-assessment criteria used to make policy decisions.
  • Loyalty over Competence: Without transparent selection criteria, political appointments are often perceived as rewards for loyalty rather than merit-based selections.

The "Blind" Administration

Personnel appointments are meant to be a powerful message from the administration to the public. However, the current process is devoid of narrative or philosophy. When the government justifies these choices as "merit-based" without explaining the standards or the verification process, it treats the public as passive recipients of information rather than citizens entitled to transparency. Even in a simple elementary school class president election, candidates provide a platform; yet, officials managing budgets worth billions of dollars are appointed with little more than a brief list of career highlights.

This strategy of keeping the public in the dark is not merely an oversight—it is a calculated tactic to avoid accountability. By refusing to provide a clear set of criteria for appointments, the government ensures that it cannot be held to any objective standard. This forces the political sphere into a state of "sunflower politics," where officials focus on demonstrating blind loyalty to those in power rather than developing policy expertise. Consequently, the government’s frequent demand for "constructive alternatives" from critics rings hollow when the government itself hides the very data needed to formulate those alternatives.

Beyond the Confirmation Theater

Citizens should stop focusing solely on the high-profile drama of confirmation hearings. The real game is happening in the shadows of the secondary and tertiary appointments that follow. When a controversial nominee is inevitably withdrawn, the administration often has a pre-planned replacement ready to step in. This cycle repeats, turning the verification process into a bureaucratic ritual designed to pacify the public while the underlying power structure remains unchanged.

The core issue is that the "personnel" are not the problem; the system is. As long as the government views appointments as rewards for political service rather than tools for national development, the public will continue to face the same policy failures—whether in housing tax policies or financial regulations like leveraged ETFs—all while being told they lack the "expertise" to critique the process. To change this, the focus must shift from individuals to the systemic lack of transparency. Without rigorous public pressure to demand clear visions and selection criteria, the administration will continue to manage the state in a fog, leaving the burden of its failures on the citizens.
