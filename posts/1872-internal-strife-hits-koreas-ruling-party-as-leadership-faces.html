Internal Strife Hits Korea’s Ruling Party as Leadership Faces Critical Review

November 02, 2026

The leadership of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), headed by Representative Kim Min-seok, faces a high-stakes reckoning on October 24 as stagnant approval ratings and internal scandals threaten to spark a premature leadership shuffle.

  • Leadership Accountability: Kim Min-seok’s leadership is under fire for failing to deliver a promised 10% boost in party support, with critics accusing the leadership of rebranding firm promises as mere "intentions" to avoid responsibility.
  • Religious Cult Scandal: A "Shincheonji" religious cult smear campaign, allegedly used during the last party convention to sideline popular lawmaker Jung Chung-rae, has resurfaced as a major point of internal contention.
  • Data Privacy Breach: Calls are mounting for a full investigation into how private party member data was illegally accessed and leaked to fuel political attacks against internal rivals.
  • Strategic Gridlock: Uncertainty grows over a potential merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party—the minor progressive party led by Cho Kuk—as the DPK leadership lacks the political capital to negotiate from a position of strength.

The "October 24" Deadline and the Credibility Gap

For the DPK leadership, October 24 is not just a date on the calendar; it is being treated as a "report card" day when major polling data will determine the political survival of the current front bench. Having seized power following the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the DPK now finds itself in the uncomfortable position of the incumbent. While the administration of President Lee Jae-myung continues to pursue its reform agenda, the party's own popularity has hit a ceiling. Internal critics argue that the leadership’s failure to hit self-imposed polling targets is being met with the same kind of "word games" that characterized the disgraced former administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The frustration within the party stems from a perceived lack of transparency regarding the "Shincheonji frame." During the party convention, rumors of ties to the controversial Shincheonji religious group were used to attack Representative Jung Chung-rae. Now that these rumors appear to have been manufactured, the base is demanding a formal apology. The leadership’s reluctance to admit fault is being viewed as a sign of weakness, potentially leading to calls for an early party convention or an emergency steering committee—though party rules make the latter difficult to implement this far out from the next general election.

Reform Momentum at Risk

This internal friction comes at a sensitive time for the Lee Jae-myung government. The DPK is currently pushing for a Special Counsel to investigate what they term "manipulated prosecutions" from the previous era, alongside other major social reforms. If the party remains bogged down in internal disputes over data leaks and religious smears, it risks losing the legislative initiative to the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition. The PPP is already looking to capitalize on the DPK’s leadership vacuum to stall the President's agenda.

Ultimately, Kim Min-seok’s leadership must decide between a "clean break" through a sincere apology and a full investigation, or continued isolation. The DPK’s strongest asset has historically been its perceived moral superiority and commitment to democratic norms compared to the "old guard" represented by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. By allowing murky political engineering and data breaches to go unpunished, the current leadership risks eroding the very trust that put them in power. To maintain the mandate of the "Candlelight Citizens" who supported the transition to the Lee administration, the party must prioritize internal cleansing over political survival.
