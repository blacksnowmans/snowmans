The Rise and Fall of SimCity: How Greed and Bungled Tech Killed a Legend

October 07, 2026

For decades, video games meant one thing: shoot the enemies, blow up the base, and watch the credits roll. Then came SimCity, a game that threw out the rulebook by asking players to build, tax, and nurture an urban ecosystem rather than destroy one. For 25 years, the franchise defined the city-building genre—until corporate overreach, rushed technology, and bad management ran it into the ground.

  • From shooter to sandbox: Created by Will Wright, the concept evolved from a helicopter combat game called Raid on Bungeling Bay after Wright discovered he enjoyed designing the maps more than bombing them.
  • The birth of Maxis: After publishers dismissed the win-condition-free game as "not a real game," Wright partnered with businessman Jeff Brown in 1987 to found the independent studio Maxis.
  • The EA buyout: Stressed by financial missteps and spin-offs, Maxis was acquired by Electronic Arts (EA) in 1997 for about $125 million, trading creative independence for cash.
  • The 2013 disaster: Forced online-only DRM and half-baked technology crushed the long-awaited franchise reboot, sealing SimCity's tragic decline.

When "No Winning" Was the Greatest Innovation

When SimCity launched on Macintosh and Amiga in 1989, traditional publishers had no idea what to do with it. There were no villains to defeat, no levels to clear, and no ultimate victory condition. Instead, Wright applied system dynamics theory—the idea that behavior is driven by structure, not isolated events—to build an organic simulation that breathed on its own. It birthed the sandbox genre, turning video games from linear puzzles into sprawling platforms for experimentation.

The hits kept coming. SimCity 2000 brought 2.5D isometric perspectives, water pipes, subways, and staggering sales. But success bred overconfidence. As Wright branched out into other experimental titles like SimEarth and eventually The Sims, Maxis flooded the market with poorly selling spin-offs. By 1997, the studio was staring at millions in losses, prompting a desperate leap toward unproven 3D graphics for a rushed SimCity 3000 reveal that ended in public humiliation.

The EA Takeover and the Road to Ruin

Enter Electronic Arts. Squeezed by financial panic, Maxis accepted a $125 million buyout from EA in 1997. While the deal kept the studio alive, it chained an independent artistic workshop to the cold, profit-driven logic of a mega-publisher. Though SimCity 3000 was successfully rebuilt in 2D, and SimCity 4 in 2003 became an absolute masterpiece of deep urban planning—later rescued and expanded by dedicated fans through the Network Addon Mode (NAM)—the corporate countdown to ruin had already begun.

By 2013, EA and Maxis attempted a grand comeback with a new engine called GlassBox. Instead of a triumph, it was a disaster driven by corporate stubbornness. EA forced an online-only requirement for single-player gameplay to combat piracy, which immediately buckled under server crashes. Worse, the engine's technical limits shrank the playable map size to a tiny fraction of its predecessor, while brain-dead AI algorithms forced thousands of simulated citizens to wander aimlessly down single-lane roads. A legendary franchise was left broken on arrival—a stark reminder of how greed and bureaucratic interference can suffocate a masterpiece.
