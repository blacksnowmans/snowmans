South Korea's Political Brinkmanship: Could President Lee Jae-myung Face Trial?

October 16, 2026

Speculation is intensifying that the South Korean judiciary may resume dormant trials involving President Lee Jae-myung, a move that could fundamentally destabilize his administration and the current political order.

  • What’s happening: Reports indicate growing internal pressure within the judiciary to restart legal proceedings against President Lee, whose ongoing criminal cases have been largely paused since he took office.
  • Political shift: Public support for President Lee has hit a concerning 35.2%—a three-week decline—while the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has overtaken the ruling Democratic Party of Korea with 41.3% support.
  • The stakes: A conviction in key cases, such as the violation of the Public Official Election Act, could lead to the loss of the presidency and force the Democratic Party to return over $30 million (43.4 billion won) in state election subsidies, effectively pushing the party toward bankruptcy.
  • Judicial warning: The move is being interpreted by observers as a potential "judicial death sentence" for the current administration, signaling a major shift in the balance of power.

The Legal Noose Tightens

President Lee currently faces five separate criminal cases, ranging from election law violations to corruption allegations stemming from his time as mayor of Seongnam. The most immediate threat is the Public Official Election Act case; the Supreme Court has already sent it back to the Seoul High Court with a guilty-leaning verdict, leaving the lower court little room to maneuver. If the court decides to bypass delays and move to a swift conclusion, Lee could face a disqualifying fine of over $700 (1 million won), which would automatically strip him of his office.

Furthermore, the "perjury solicitation" case, currently paused, looms as a massive political liability. Observers note that if the judiciary breaks its silence and resumes these trials, the administration's defensive options—such as filing for injunctions or constitutional court reviews—are unlikely to stop the momentum of a court determined to reach a final verdict.

Judicial Independence and Political Consequences

The tension has spilled over into the National Assembly’s annual audit, where Democratic Party lawmakers have been aggressively challenging prosecutors, while also facing intense scrutiny over the perceived selective enforcement of the law. Critics argue that the administration’s attempts to influence the judiciary and law enforcement agencies are backfiring, causing public trust to plummet. The image of a president bogged down by perpetual court appearances, rather than focusing on governance, has fueled voter fatigue.

Some political analysts suggest that the current judicial atmosphere is akin to "chemotherapy" for South Korean politics. A total upheaval—potentially involving the collapse of the two-party system through bankruptcy and the prosecution of former figures like the imprisoned ex-president Yoon Suk-yeol—might be a painful but necessary cleansing process. As the President’s approval rating drifts toward the critical 30% floor, the judiciary appears increasingly emboldened to act as an independent check on executive overreach, marking a potential turning point for the nation's fragile political stability.
