The Democratic Party’s Identity Crisis: Compromise, Debt, and the Sidelining of Reformers

October 14, 2026

Recent confirmation hearings and audits suggest that South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is shifting toward political pragmatism at the expense of its core reformist identity.

  • Kim Ji-yong, a candidate for a top judicial post, faced harsh criticism from within the DPK for his "cowardly" silence during politically motivated prosecutions led by the clique of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former president currently in prison).
  • The DPK leadership is reportedly marginalizing Choo Mi-ae, a symbolic figure of prosecutorial reform, as her uncompromising stance creates friction with the party’s current "stability-first" strategy under the Lee Jae-myung administration.
  • The main opposition party, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), has seized on Gyeonggi Province’s ballooning debt—totaling about $5.4 billion (7.5 trillion KRW)—to mock the ruling party’s administrative competence.
  • Internal critics are comparing the party leadership to "cuckoos" who have taken over the nest, warning that abandoning reformist principles to appease the establishment could alienate the party's base.

The "Cowardice" of Bureaucratic Survival

The confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong highlighted a growing contradiction within the DPK. Lawmakers like Yun Kun-young attacked Kim for his past role in the prosecution, specifically his silence during the "targeted investigation" of Prosecutor Jung Jin-woong and the controversial prosecution of officials who tried to stop Kim Hak-ui (a former vice minister accused of corruption) from fleeing the country. Kim Ji-yong’s defense—that he didn't act because he wasn't asked for data—was branded as "cowardly" by his own party members.

The irony is that the PPP, which usually opposes DPK nominees, appeared surprisingly friendly toward Kim. This stems from Kim’s history as a "Yoon-aligned" prosecutor who once signed statements opposing the suspension of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during the previous administration. For reformists within the DPK, seeing their party promote a candidate who once shielded the prosecutorial "cartel" feels like a betrayal of the Candlelight Revolution’s mandate to overhaul the justice system.

Internal Power Struggles and Financial Mismanagement

The friction isn't limited to appointments. There is a palpable tension between the DPK leadership and Choo Mi-ae. As a former justice minister who went head-to-head with the prosecution, Choo remains a hero to the party's hardcore reformers. However, the current leadership, focusing on a "pragmatic" path to maintaining power, appears to view her as a liability. By sidelining Choo, the party risks losing its sharpest edge in the ongoing battle for judicial reform, potentially ceding that ground to the more aggressive Rebuilding Korea Party led by Cho Kuk.

This ideological drift is compounded by administrative struggles. During a recent audit of Gyeonggi Province, it was revealed that the province's debt grew by nearly $3.9 billion (5.4 trillion KRW) under the current DPK governor, Kim Dong-yeon, a former finance official. While President Lee Jae-myung’s previous 2.1 trillion KRW debt as governor was attributed to necessary pandemic relief, the current spike lacks a clear justification. This gave the PPP’s Kim Ye-ji an opening to mock the DPK’s financial stewardship, asking with mock concern how the opposition could "help" the struggling province. If the DPK cannot reconcile its internal power struggles with effective governance, it may find itself losing the moral and administrative authority it worked so hard to build.
