OpenAI’s Astra and the New Era of Artificial General Intelligence

September 07, 2026

OpenAI's new Astra model marks a turning point in artificial intelligence, bringing us to the threshold of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)—systems that match or exceed human-level reasoning across multiple domains.

  • What happened: OpenAI released Astra, a model that scored a perfect mark on South Korea's notoriously difficult college entrance exam (the CSAT) and handles complex multi-step planning without human intervention.
  • What it changes: AI has moved past basic text prediction into autonomous execution, capable of managing daily schedules, writing code, and solving advanced professional tasks in minutes.
  • Who is affected: Enterprise software developers, white-collar workers, and anyone relying on traditional notions of human intellectual labor.

The technical leap is staggering, but it brings severe risks. Astra earned OpenAI's first "Critical" safety rating, highlighting its dual-use potential for advanced cyberattacks if mishandled. While billions are poured into security programs, the speed of algorithmic evolution continues to outpace regulatory oversight.

Simultaneously, this software revolution is driving a massive hardware boom. Training Astra required over 100,000 of Nvidia's Blackwell GPUs, with another 400,000 on the way. As data centers consume unprecedented amounts of electricity, the AI race is quickly turning into an energy and geopolitical crisis, with Korean semiconductor giants caught squarely in the crossfire of US-China supply chain battles.

Korean Memory Giants and the Valuation Puzzle

Wall Street and domestic brokerages are betting heavily on South Korea's memory chipmakers, with Goldman Sachs issuing a bullish target of 490,000 won (about $350) for Samsung Electronics, and Mirae Asset raising SK Hynix's target to 3.1 million won (about $2,200). Analysts point to tight supply through 2027 and long-term contracts (LTAs) that secure 60% to 70% of production capacity as shields against traditional cyclical downturns.

Yet despite record-shattering operating profits—with SK Hynix projecting quarterly profits nearing 100 trillion won (about $72 billion)—Korean tech stocks often lag behind their global peers. The culprit is not technology, but notoriously passive investor relations (IR). While American and Taiwanese giants aggressively market their visions to the world, Korean firms remain hesitant to loudly demand fair valuation for powering the physical backbone of the global AI revolution.
