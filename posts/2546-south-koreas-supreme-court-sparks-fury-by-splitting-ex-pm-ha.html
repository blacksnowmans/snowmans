South Korea's Supreme Court Sparks Fury by Splitting Ex-PM Han's Rebellion Case

November 20, 2026

South Korea's Supreme Court has ignited widespread controversy after ruling that former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo's procedural help in convening the Cabinet right before the December 3 martial law declaration did not constitute rebellion.

  • The Ruling: The Supreme Court sent part of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo's conviction back to the Seoul High Court with instructions to acquit him on charges related to convening the pre-declaration Cabinet meeting.
  • The Split Logic: The majority ruled that Han lacked the explicit intent to disrupt the Constitution prior to the broadcast, upholding guilt only for his actions after martial law was formally declared.
  • The Dissent: Three justices issued a scathing minority opinion, framing the martial law attempt as a "self-coup" and warning that the decision provides a dangerous legal sanctuary for high-level accomplices.
  • Who Is Affected: High-ranking officials facing trial for complicity in the martial law attempt, alongside a public seeking legal accountability for the night 1,605 armed soldiers and 3,790 police officers were deployed to silence state institutions.

Artificial Logic in a Two-Hour Window

The Supreme Court majority’s decision hinges on an extraordinarily artificial premise: slicing a single, high-stress two-hour window into isolated legal fragments. Lower courts had previously handed Han a severe 23-year prison sentence, recognizing that gathering a Cabinet quorum just before the announcement was the essential cog that gave Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s unconstitutional decree a veneer of procedural legitimacy. By treating Han’s pre-declaration assistance as a well-meaning effort to enforce procedural rules, the top court severed the preparation of the crime from its execution.

This technical hair-splitting ignores the reality on the ground that night. Han, a veteran bureaucrat with nearly 50 years in public service, was fully aware of the unconstitutional nature of the impending decree, which suspended parliamentary activity and media freedom. While 1,605 armed special forces troops and 3,790 police officers were dispatched to block Parliament and the National Election Commission, Han provided the administrative cover Mr. Yoon needed to launch his assault on democratic institutions.

A Dangerous Blueprint for Future Autocrats

As Justices Oh Kyung-mi, Shin Sook-hee, and Kim Seong-soo argued in their dissent, the night of December 3 was a textbook self-coup aimed at neutralizing constitutional checks. The dissenting justices emphasized that an official in Han's position could not have been ignorant of the consequences. Assisting in assembling a rubber-stamp Cabinet to greenlight an insurrection demonstrates, at minimum, a clear tacit approval of the destruction of the constitutional order.

By granting an acquittal for Han’s early-stage abetment, the Supreme Court has carved out a troubling precedent. Future state accomplices now have a ready-made escape hatch: offer token hesitation, assemble the procedural paperwork for an autocrat, and claim legal immunity because the formal announcement had not yet been made. Instead of defending the democratic constitution, the court's majority has handed top enablers a shield against accountability.
