The AI Memory Supercycle: South Korea’s Chip Giants Lead a Global Surge

November 06, 2026

  • Record Revenue: Global DRAM (Dynamic Random-Access Memory) revenue skyrocketed 369% in the third quarter, fueled by an AI-driven "supercycle" where price, rather than just volume, is driving market growth.
  • Market Dominance: Samsung Electronics, South Korea's flagship conglomerate, retained its lead in the global DRAM market with a 38% share, while SK Hynix, Korea's second-largest chipmaker, followed with 29%. Together, they control 67% of the global market.
  • Consumer Impact: The surge in memory prices is squeezing profit margins for tech giants like Apple, likely resulting in higher price tags for next-generation hardware, including Samsung’s upcoming Galaxy S27 series.
  • Tightening Supply Chain: Beyond chips, the entire manufacturing ecosystem—from advanced lasers to packaging materials—is facing shortages, creating a precarious environment where any single supply chain disruption could stall production.

The Shift from Volume to Pricing Power

The current semiconductor boom is less about how many chips are being produced and more about who commands the market value. With the industry reaching a scale approaching $200 billion, we are seeing a shift where "price leadership" is the ultimate goal. For Samsung and SK Hynix, the challenge is no longer just manufacturing volume; it is securing their position in the high-value AI memory sector. The pressure is mounting on consumer electronics. As memory components become significantly more expensive, the days of affordable flagship tech are waning. If these costs continue to climb through 2030, the "AI revolution" may become increasingly exclusionary, turning high-end consumer hardware into luxury items rather than accessible tools.

Technological Supremacy and Geopolitical Risks

The rivalry between SK Hynix and Samsung has narrowed to the minute details of HBM4 (High Bandwidth Memory) production. SK Hynix currently holds an edge in HBM4 yield rates, while Samsung is betting on total system optimization to regain its competitive advantage. Their progress is being closely watched, particularly as they navigate the volatile US-China chip trade landscape. While it is encouraging to see Korean firms leading the charge, the industry is increasingly vulnerable. The shortage of manufacturing equipment and the consolidation of the foundry market—dominated by Taiwan’s TSMC, which controls 76% of the market—means the entire industry is walking a tightrope. For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, ensuring the stability of these supply chains has become a matter of national economic security. As the industry moves into an era where AI chips are deemed "essential infrastructure," the ability of these companies to protect their technological lead while managing complex diplomatic pressures will define the country’s economic trajectory for the next decade.
