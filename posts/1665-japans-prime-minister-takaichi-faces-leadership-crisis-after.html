Japan’s Prime Minister Takaichi Faces Leadership Crisis After Missing G7 Summit

October 29, 2026

Japanese Prime Minister Takaichi is facing intense public backlash and growing doubts over her leadership qualifications after skipping a G7 online summit—officially citing technical issues—amid reports that she was attending a dinner with ruling-party heavyweight Taro Aso instead.

  • What happened: The Japanese prime minister sparked outrage over allegations that she skipped an online G7 summit to drink with political powerbroker Taro Aso, a blunder compounded by contradictory excuses from her office and foreign ministry.
  • Foreign policy friction: Chinese President Xi Jinping effectively bypassed Takaichi’s cabinet by sending a condolence message directly to the family of late Japanese Prime Minister Fukuda, explicitly warning Tokyo to abide by historical political agreements.
  • Domestic pushback: Takaichi’s attempt to cut electric vehicle (EV) subsidies faced immediate resistance from Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI), exposing internal friction over economic strategy.
  • Implications for Seoul: Instability in Tokyo complicates efforts by South Korean President Lee Jae-myung to mediate Northeast Asian diplomatic tensions and establish predictable, constructive ties with Japan.

Diplomatic Missteps and Growing Isolation

Diplomatic credibility depends heavily on predictable commitments. Missing a multilateral summit as critical as the G7 to handle internal political factions sends a damaging signal to international allies. The scandal has deepened due to shifting explanations: early claims blamed equipment failures, but subsequent reports of a dinner meeting with Taro Aso prompted widespread media criticism in Japan, with more than ten major outlets questioning the prime minister's basic qualifications within a single day.

This leadership void creates real friction for neighboring countries. South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has been taking a measured diplomatic approach to improve South Korea-Japan relations and help mediate regional ties with China. However, diplomatic progress requires a stable, clear-eyed partner in Tokyo. As former Japanese Prime Minister Yukio Hatoyama noted, healthy bilateral relations depend heavily on Japanese leadership acknowledging historical realities—something Takaichi’s revisionist stance and erratic diplomatic choices continue to undermine.

Policy Disarray and the View from Seoul

The controversy in Tokyo is not limited to foreign policy; it extends to domestic economic management. Takaichi’s attempt to roll back EV subsidies ran straight into a wall of opposition from METI officials, who argued that cutting support was premature while domestic consumer demand was still forming. Bypassing ministry consensus to push top-down policy changes has triggered friction across economic departments at a time when Japanese citizens are struggling with rising inflation.

While Takaichi attempted to project normal diplomatic operations by hosting a bilateral meeting with the prime minister of Bangladesh, small-scale meetings cannot make up for skipping major international forums like the G7. For observers in Seoul, watching Tokyo struggle with basic governance reinforces the need for steady leadership. To ensure South Korea avoids repeating the humiliating diplomatic missteps seen during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the Lee Jae-myung administration must maintain a pragmatic, principled stance while closely monitoring Tokyo's political instability.
