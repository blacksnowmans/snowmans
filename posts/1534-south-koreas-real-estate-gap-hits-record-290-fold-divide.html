South Korea’s Real Estate Gap Hits Record 290-Fold Divide

October 27, 2026

The real estate asset gap between South Korea’s top 20% and bottom 20% wealth brackets has widened to an all-time high of 290-fold, driven by surging property values in Seoul and persistent stagnation in rural regions.

  • The extreme disparity: Average real estate holdings for the top 20% reached 693.41 million won (about $515,000), while the bottom 20% held just 2.39 million won (about $1,800) on average.
  • Pervasive housing insecurity: Homeownership among the bottom 20% asset group stands at a dismal 6.6%, leaving over 93% relying on increasingly expensive lease or rental arrangements.
  • Regional imbalance: High demand and rising apartment prices in Seoul’s prime districts continue to expand wealth at the top, while provincial markets struggle with oversupply and long-term slump.
  • Policy pressure on Seoul: The stark divide amplifies demands for President Lee Jae-myung’s administration to rapidly expand public housing supply and reinforce safety nets for non-homeowners.

A Deepening Divide Driven by Capital-Region Real Estate

While South Korea’s earned income gap has shown modest narrowing in recent years, asset inequality fueled by housing is accelerating out of control. For decades, owning real estate in prime capital locations has served as the ultimate shortcut to wealth generation, outstripping wage growth by a wide margin. For ordinary wage earners and younger generations, the compounding returns of property ownership have transformed housing from a basic living need into a rigid social barrier.

The core driver of this widening chasm remains the intense concentration of value in Seoul and the surrounding capital region. Even under high interest rates and tight credit conditions, Seoul apartments have demonstrated unusual price resilience, expanding the net worth of affluent homeowners while regional property markets languish in slump.

The Political Stakes for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

The historic inequality metric presents a formidable policy challenge for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung. Conservative market observers argue that easing regulations on multi-homeowners is necessary to revitalize real estate transactions. However, critics counter that previous rounds of deregulation—notably during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term—primarily enabled speculative buying and further elevated entry barriers for first-time buyers.

To prevent real estate from permanently cementing economic stratification, public policy must pivot away from speculative market incentives. Restoring social mobility will require the current administration to prioritize public-led affordable housing projects and establish a stable, long-term safety net for non-homeowners.
