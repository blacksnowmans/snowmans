South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party Stumbles Over Judicial Impeachment and Crypto Tax Delays

October 23, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing widening internal fractures and plunging approval ratings as party leadership hesitates on impeaching the Supreme Court Chief Justice and wavers over the country's repeatedly delayed cryptocurrency tax.

  • Leadership under fire: Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok’s job disapproval surged to 67.6% in a recent Realmeter survey, with negative ratings topping 53% even in Honam, the party's traditional southwestern stronghold.
  • Judicial clash: Hardline lawmakers led by representative Jung Chung-rae are rebelling against Kim’s cautious timeline for impeaching Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, arguing hesitation squanders the opportunity to overhaul the judiciary following abuses seen during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure before his impeachment.
  • Policy gridlock: The party is considering a fourth postponement of South Korea's crypto gains tax, paralyzing regulatory clarity while igniting criticism from retail investors and the conservative opposition People Power Party.
  • Pressure from the flank: The unapologetically progressive Rebuilding Korea Party is steadily climbing toward double-digit polling by staking out uncompromising stances on both the judicial overhaul and digital asset regulation.

Revolt on the Left Over Stalled Reforms

The rift over Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae cuts to the core of the ruling party’s identity under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. Party leader Kim Min-seok argues that aggressively pushing an impeachment without airtight justification risks public backlash and institutional instability. However, rank-and-file progressives and Jung Chung-rae’s rising faction view this pragmatism as political cowardice. After witnessing intense legal warfare during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, Democratic supporters expected decisive structural overhauls once their party took full power; instead, they see leadership flinching with the reins in hand.

This indecision creates a dangerous opening on the Democratic Party's left flank. The smaller Rebuilding Korea Party has captured voter attention by demanding an uncompromising confrontation with the judicial establishment. As the progressive electorate loses patience with legislative caution, the ruling party risks losing control of its own reform narrative to an agile, uncompromising competitor.

Policy Drift and Backlash in Traditional Strongholds

Compounding the ideological dispute is an erratic legislative agenda. By dangling another delay on crypto taxation—with Kim offering only a vague commitment to decide before the end of the year—the party has alienated young voters while handing the conservative People Power Party easy ammunition over regulatory unpreparedness. Additional controversies, such as allowing foreign corporate-run hospitals in regional development zones and tangled revisions over whether the central government should fund municipal firefighter wages, reinforce impressions of administrative incompetence.

The polling collapse in Honam and Chungcheong is a clear warning sign. When a ruling party holds the presidency and a commanding legislative majority yet fails to project competence on bread-and-butter policies or resolve its own internal battles, voter fatigue sets in rapidly. Unless Kim Min-seok’s leadership establishes a clear line between political caution and policy paralysis, the ruling coalition will find itself hamstrung by the very factional warfare it sought to avoid.
