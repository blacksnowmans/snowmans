How a Leaked Video Exposed South Korea's Broken Personnel Verification System

October 04, 2026

  • What happened: A newly surfaced video featuring former presidential aides, including former official Lee Jin-kwan, exposes how South Korea's presidential office handles high-level appointments.
  • The reality of the system: Staff admitted to mechanically processing paperwork for critical judicial posts—like Supreme Court and Constitutional Court justices—while facing constant, high-pressure demands from superiors to speed up vetting.
  • Public fallout: Fresh polling shows roughly 67.9% of the public believes the government's vetting system is malfunctioning, highlighting deep-seated voter distrust.
  • Who is affected: The administration's core credibility is on the line, with young voters and capital-region residents showing particularly sharp skepticism toward the executive's handling of state appointments.

Inside the Presidential Office: Speed Over Substance

South Korean politics often features a familiar, exhausting spectacle: high-ranking officials sweating under parliamentary questioning while lawmakers trade sharp barbs and nominees offer evasive answers. But a recently publicised video featuring testimony from the era of former aide Joo Jin-woo pulls back the curtain on the presidential office's civil affairs and secretariat offices, revealing an internal culture driven entirely by panic and haste. Former officials described a relentless, year-round pressure campaign from top brass—including former Chief of Staff Kim Dae-ki—demanding rapid-fire vetting to fill empty government seats. Rather than acting as a rigorous moral filter, the vetting process functioned as a mechanical rubber stamp designed merely to stop complaints and keep government machinery moving.

This relentless focus on speed has predictably backfired, triggering a string of appointment disasters and scandals. When questioned about top judicial nominees, former staff claimed ignorance of how candidates were chosen, essentially passing the buck and treating the protection of judicial independence as an afterthought. Most alarmingly, the testimony reflected a stunningly cavalier attitude toward qualifications—with comments suggesting that as long as legal technicalities are met, public standards and moral fitness do not matter. That kind of institutional arrogance treats the executive's appointment powers like absolute rule, leaving citizens to wonder if anyone in power actually cares about competence.

A Crisis of Public Trust

Voters are noticing. Recent data from the polling firm Research Well indicates that nearly 68% of the public views the current presidential vetting system as broken, with only a fraction believing it works properly. Discontent runs especially high among voters in their thirties and around Seoul, where over 70% point to systemic failure. When a government starts its term by prioritizing quick political appointments over transparent moral checks, it repeats the worst mistakes of past administrations. Unless those in power realize that filling government posts is about public trust rather than administrative quota-filling, these recurring appointment scandals will continue to erode the foundations of public confidence.
