Debunked: The "Cult Infiltration" Scandal Shaking Korea’s Ruling Party

November 06, 2026

An internal investigation by the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has found no evidence that the controversial Shincheonji religious group successfully infiltrated or influenced the party’s leadership elections, despite months of rumors that nearly tore the party apart.

  • Data vs. Rumors: While media reports suggested 100,000 cult members had joined the party, the internal probe found only about 8,000 linked members—just 0.5% of the DPK’s 1.5 million dues-paying voters.
  • Zero Evidence: The investigation report contained no concrete names, dates, or meeting logs, consisting instead of vague testimonies and "subjective guesses" about potential interference.
  • External Verification: A separate eight-month state investigation by a joint prosecution-police task force confirmed that while the group targeted the opposition People Power Party (PPP), there was no evidence of a similar operation against the DPK.
  • Factional Damage: The "Shincheonji frame" was used as a political weapon during the August 17 national convention to attack specific candidates, leading to harassment of regular party members.

A Statistical Non-Event

The scandal centered on the Shincheonji Church of Jesus, a religious movement often described by critics as a cult and widely scrutinized for its secretive political lobbying. Rumors peaked during the ruling party's recent leadership race, with claims that the group had mobilized a "hidden army" of 100,000 followers to skew the vote. However, Choe Min-hee, a member of the DPK Supreme Council who reviewed the confidential findings, clarified that the 100,000 figure represented the group’s total recruitment pool, not actual party members. The 8,000 members actually identified are statistically negligible in a party of 1.5 million, representing a smaller footprint than established Buddhist or Catholic voting blocs.

The disparity between the rumors and reality suggests the "infiltration" narrative was less about religious influence and more about internal power struggles. During the convention, some factions used the specter of the cult to paint rivals as "splitters" or "secret allies" of the group. This led to "mic terrorism" at party events, where aggressive YouTubers harassed supporters of certain candidates, demanding they "confess" to being cult members. Such tactics caused deep emotional rifts within the party base that the current leadership is now struggling to mend.

Politics Over Policy

The DPK leadership now faces a "clean-up" problem. While President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is focused on rebuilding the national system following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the ruling party has wasted significant political capital on this ghost-chase. The investigation's failure to produce even a single record of a meeting between party officials and religious leaders highlights a breakdown in the party’s internal auditing system, which allowed "brain-reflections" and "echo chamber" theories to be treated as high-level intelligence.

To move forward, the party must move beyond the amateurish reliance on unverified tips from partisan influencers. The contrast between the DPK’s internal "Task Force" and the state’s professional investigation—which spent eight months searching the group's headquarters only to find zero DPK involvement—is embarrassing for a governing party. For the DPK to maintain its mandate, it needs to stop fighting imaginary religious shadows and return to the bread-and-butter issues of the post-Yoon era. True reconciliation will require a formal apology to those members who were unfairly branded as cultists for purely political gain.
