Korea Market Briefing: KOSPI Plunges into Value Territory Amid Foreign Outflows and Governance Clashes

October 24, 2026

South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI index is experiencing severe volatility and relentless foreign selling, pushing market valuations below book value even as corporate earnings hold steady.

  • Heavy foreign offloading: Foreign investors have offloaded roughly 230 trillion won (about $165 billion) in Korean equities this year, primarily cashing in gains on chipmakers to normalize outsized artificial intelligence allocations.
  • Historic valuation trough: Forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios have compressed below 5x, while price-to-book (P/B) multiples have slipped under 1.2x, driven by trapped supply overhangs and psychological panic rather than collapsing earnings.
  • Central bank in a bind: The Bank of Korea, South Korea’s central bank, is projecting stronger growth and signaling hawkish rhetoric, but remains hesitant to raise interest rates due to highly leveraged mortgage borrowers who bought during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office.
  • Regulatory showdown on carve-outs: Financial regulators are aggressively pushing back against SK Hynix’s bid to publicly list its U.S. NAND flash subsidiary, Solidigm, declaring separate subsidiary listings a direct violation of shareholder rights.

Supply Snarls and Semiconductor Profit-Taking

The violent swings in Korean equities reflect a market bottlenecked by order flow rather than fundamental decay. Unlike Wall Street, where investor entry points are distributed over multi-year cycles, Korean market participants are heavily concentrated in narrow valuation bands. This concentration creates stiff overhead resistance: as soon as the index attempts a recovery, trapped investors sell to break even. Meanwhile, domestic institutions and retail traders lack the buying power to absorb remaining supply, leaving price discovery stalled.

Global hedge funds have not targeted Korea specifically; they have rebalanced broad AI semiconductor portfolios across Taiwan, Japan, and Seoul. Having accumulated Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix at much lower valuations, foreign funds mechanically locked in profits. With foreign semiconductor allocations having declined from overextended highs toward standard portfolio weights of 27–28%, the daily pace of net selling has dropped from trillions of won to manageable volumes, indicating the worst of foreign capital flight is nearing its end.

Governance Reform Faces the Solidigm Test

Valuation multiples will remain depressed until structural corporate governance risks are resolved. President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and financial watchdogs—including the Financial Supervisory Service and the Financial Services Commission—have targeted the chronic practice of duplicate listings, where conglomerates spin off profitable subsidiaries, diluting existing parent-company shareholders and entrenching the "Korea Discount."

SK Hynix’s effort to list Solidigm on a U.S. exchange has become the latest flashpoint. After absorbing years of Solidigm’s heavy losses during the semiconductor downturn, SK Hynix investors now face the prospect of seeing the subsidiary’s return to profitability carved out for external shareholders through an intricate five-tier corporate pyramid. With regulators threatening strict enforcement and warnings of market sanctions, the dispute signals that future market upside hinges less on tech hype and far more on corporate accountability, aggressive share cancellations, and regulatory limits on corporate carve-outs.
