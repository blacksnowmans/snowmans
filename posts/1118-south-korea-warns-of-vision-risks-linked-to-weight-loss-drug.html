South Korea Warns of Vision Risks Linked to Weight-Loss Drug Wegovy

October 14, 2026

  • South Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) has officially updated Wegovy’s warning label to include potential risks of vision loss.
  • The decision follows nearly two years of investigation into reports of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), a condition where blood flow to the optic nerve is blocked.
  • Despite the drug's surging popularity—with domestic distribution increasing nearly 20-fold—critics argue the government’s oversight and safety measures remain dangerously sluggish.

A Long-Awaited Warning

The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS), South Korea’s primary pharmaceutical regulatory agency, has officially acknowledged that Wegovy—a popular weight-loss drug developed by Novo Nordisk—may cause severe vision complications. Following nearly 22 months of deliberation, the agency updated the drug’s mandatory usage precautions to include the risk of ischemic optic neuropathy.

The move comes after intense pressure from lawmakers, including Representative Baek Sun-hee of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. During recent legislative audits, Baek criticized the MFDS for its "passive and reactive" approach, noting that while international regulators like the European Medicines Agency took early action, South Korean authorities lagged despite receiving domestic reports of adverse effects as early as December 2024.

Regulatory Failure and the "Wait-and-See" Trap

The government's response has sparked a broader debate about the safety of GLP-1 weight-loss medications in a market that has seen distribution jump from 160,000 units to over 3.1 million units in less than a year. Critics argue that the MFDS has hidden behind a lack of definitive "causal links" to justify its inaction, effectively leaving patients to fend for themselves.

In other nations, the fallout is already moving through the courts. In Denmark, the Patient Compensation Agency has already paid out over $1.5 million (approximately 2 billion KRW) to dozens of patients who suffered vision damage after using similar medications like Ozempic or Wegovy. In the U.S., massive class-action lawsuits are currently targeting manufacturers, alleging that companies failed to adequately warn patients of the specific risks to their eyesight.

For the 104 patients in South Korea who have already sought medical treatment for optic nerve disorders while using these medications, the government’s updated label is a belated acknowledgment of a reality they are already living. As the demand for these "miracle" injections continues to explode, the current administration faces mounting pressure to move beyond simple warnings and implement mandatory pre-screening protocols for patients. The question remains: how much of our sensory health are we willing to sacrifice in the pursuit of a standardized beauty ideal?
