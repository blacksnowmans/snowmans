The AI "Slowdown" is a Financial Survival Strategy, Not a Moral Choice

September 22, 2026

  • What happened: Leading AI firms like OpenAI and Anthropic are suddenly calling for an "AI speed limit," citing safety and ethical concerns.
  • The reality: The move is likely a defensive reaction to astronomical infrastructure costs, unsustainable "adjusted" earnings reports, and fears that AI models are becoming too complex to control.
  • The risk: Big Tech companies are trapped in a circular investment loop—spending billions on their own clouds and chips—which masks a lack of real-world consumer profitability.
  • Market impact: South Korea's semiconductor sector faces a potential supply glut by 2029 as massive data center investments clash with cooling demand.

The Accounting Mirage Behind the AI Boom

The tech industry’s newfound interest in "AI speed control" is being framed as a principled stance on safety, but behind the scenes, it reeks of financial desperation. Giants like OpenAI and Anthropic—currently gunning for massive IPOs—are burning through cash at rates that make traditional venture capital models look conservative. To appease investors, they are highlighting "adjusted operating profits," a metric that conveniently ignores massive stock-based compensation and depreciation costs. In reality, these companies are bleeding cash to fund the very infrastructure they hope will one day turn a profit.

This is further complicated by the "circular investment" structure. Amazon and Google, for instance, are pouring billions into AI startups, but much of that money simply flows back into their own pockets as cloud service fees and hardware purchases. It is a massive internal loop that creates the illusion of a self-sustaining ecosystem. However, when you strip away the hype, the actual consumer demand for high-end AI services is not keeping pace with the trillions of dollars (thousands of billions) being spent on data centers and power grids.

Infrastructure Bottlenecks and the Coming Glut

The AI revolution is hitting a physical wall. Power grids and semiconductor supply chains cannot keep up with the projected demand, leading to a strange irony: the AI bubble isn't popping yet simply because the hardware to build it isn't being finished fast enough. Companies are filing for massive data center projects, but many remain stalled due to power shortages, turning land that was once worth little into multi-million dollar assets that sit idle.

For South Korea, which sits at the heart of the global semiconductor supply chain, this presents a volatile outlook. While firms like Samsung Electronics remain fundamentally solid with strong book values, the market is entering a "period of extreme patience." Between the massive supply increases coming from Chinese competitors and the potential for a global AI supply glut by 2029, the industry is bracing for a transition from high-growth euphoria to a brutal price war. For investors, the era of easy AI gains is fading, replaced by a need for sober analysis and a focus on long-term index stability over the volatility of individual tech stocks.
