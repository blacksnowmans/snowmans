South Korea Led the World Against App Store Monopolies. Big Tech Found a Way Around It Anyway.

October 29, 2026

South Korean consumers are paying steep surcharges on everyday digital subscriptions because Google and Apple have successfully bypassed the world’s first law banning mandatory in-app payment systems.

  • A persistent mobile surcharge: Subscriptions for YouTube Premium, KakaoTalk emotes, and local streaming platforms cost up to 30% more when purchased through iOS or Android apps compared to desktop web browsers.
  • Loophole economics: After South Korea outlawed mandatory proprietary payment systems in 2021, Google and Apple introduced alternative third-party billing with a 26% fee, which actually raises developer costs above 30% once payment gateway fees are added.
  • Regulator pushback: The Korea Communications Commission (KCC) moved to fine the two tech giants a combined 63.7 billion won (roughly $47 million), but both companies have challenged the enforcement.
  • Content industry strain: Local streaming services TVING and Wavve, as well as digital comics and e-book publishers, were forced into broad price hikes that disproportionately hit younger creators and digital consumers.

The Illusion of Compliance

When South Korea’s National Assembly amended the Telecommunications Business Act in 2021, international onlookers celebrated it as the "anti-Google law." Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney praised South Korea for pioneering open platform rights, and lawmakers in Washington and Brussels pointed to Seoul as proof that Silicon Valley’s payment monopoly could be broken by statute.

The reality on the ground has proved far messier. Rather than opening their platforms to genuine competition, Google and Apple offered third-party payment options with a nominal four-percentage-point discount: a 26% commission. Once developers pay standard credit card processing and gateway fees of 3% to 6%, bypassing Apple or Google’s native checkout actually costs them more than sticking with it. Coupled with strict app store rules prohibiting developers from linking directly to cheaper web checkouts, the policy effectively locked the 30% ecosystem tax in place.

High Stakes and Mounting Retaliation Risks

The KCC’s 63.7 billion won penalty—42.9 billion won against Google and 20.8 billion won against Apple—is small change for companies generating trillions of won in South Korea alone. But Big Tech's aggressive legal resistance is driven by civil liability rather than the regulatory fine itself. If South Korean courts uphold the KCC's findings, domestic developers could pursue damages estimated at up to 8 trillion won (about $5.9 billion), including roughly 149 billion won in excess fees Apple allegedly collected by calculating its 30% take inclusive of value-added tax for local developers.

Meanwhile, the dispute risks entangling the Lee Jae-myung administration in trade friction, as U.S. industry groups frequently lobby Washington to treat South Korean platform regulations as non-tariff barriers. With domestic app markets dominated by Google (66%) and Apple (21%), lawmakers in the National Assembly are weighing stronger legislative fixes to penalize platform retaliation—such as app review delays and shadow bans—before South Korea’s pioneering regulatory victory is rendered completely toothless.
