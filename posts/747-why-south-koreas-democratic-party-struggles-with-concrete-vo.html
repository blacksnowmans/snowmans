Why South Korea’s Democratic Party Struggles with "Concrete" Voter Loyalty

September 14, 2026

In South Korean political discourse, the term "concrete" is often used to describe the unshakeable, monolithic support base of conservative parties. Recently, however, political commentators have been debating why the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country’s main liberal opposition—fails to replicate this rigid, "concrete" consistency among its own supporters.

  • Structural diversity: The DPK’s base spans from radical progressives to pragmatic moderates, making it difficult to maintain a singular, unified identity.
  • High moral standards: Unlike conservative blocs that often rally around leaders despite scandals, DPK supporters are prone to "self-censorship" and withdrawing support if the party fails their high ethical expectations.
  • Fandom vs. Party: While traditional base loyalty is fragmented, new, highly active "fandom" groups are emerging, changing how supporters interact with the party.
  • The "30-40% ceiling": The party’s struggle to balance the demands of its passionate core and its moderate wing often traps its approval ratings in a narrow band.

The Paradox of Critical Support

The DPK’s difficulty in forming a "concrete" base is not necessarily a sign of weakness, but a byproduct of the high expectations held by its supporters. In Korean politics, the liberal camp has traditionally subjected itself to stricter moral scrutiny. While this promotes democratic health, it creates a tactical disadvantage; whenever the party stumbles, its own voters are often the first to demand accountability. This "critical support" acts as a structural inhibitor to the kind of blind, tribal loyalty that characterizes the conservative People Power Party.

However, dismissing these voters as fickle misses a deeper evolution. We are seeing the rise of "fandom politics," where supporters are not merely passive voters but active participants who pay party dues and directly engage in policy debates. This is a shift from the old, passive "concrete" model to an active, ideological network. These supporters are more loyal than traditional voters, but their loyalty is contingent upon the party remaining true to its promised values.

The Dilemma of the "Steel Fortress"

The persistent challenge for the DPK is that this vibrant, critical base often clashes with the older, more moderate segments of the population. When the party leans too far into the demands of its vocal, progressive base, it risks alienating the swing voters necessary to win national elections. Conversely, catering to the middle leaves the core feeling betrayed. This tension keeps the party’s approval ratings locked in a cycle that feels less like a solid foundation and more like an isolated "steel fortress."

Ultimately, the DPK’s lack of a "concrete" base may be its greatest accidental strength. A party with a blind, unquestioning base risks arrogance and stagnation. By contrast, a base that constantly demands reflection and reform forces the party to remain dynamic. The challenge for the DPK moving forward is not to force its supporters into a rigid mold, but to bridge the gap between its generations and classes, turning its diverse, critical energy into a coherent vision for the future.
