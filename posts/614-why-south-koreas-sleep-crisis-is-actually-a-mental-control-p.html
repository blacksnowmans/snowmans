Why South Korea's Sleep Crisis Is Actually a Mental Control Problem

September 10, 2026

  • The real culprit: Experts argue that sleep deprivation isn't caused by stress and loneliness; rather, a lack of sleep actively amplifies stress and emotional burnout.
  • Cultural drive: South Korea's intense societal drive for self-achievement has historically treated sleep as a sign of laziness, pushing citizens past their physical limits.
  • Social toll: Chronic sleep deprivation erodes personal self-control, driving down voter turnout and fraying public patience in a notoriously fast-paced society.
  • Simple fixes: Experts recommend treating sleep as a gradual winding-down process—starting with keeping smartphones out of the bedroom—rather than expecting the brain to "pass out" instantly.

South Korea is a society built on relentless drive. From neighborhood associations to corporate boardrooms, citizens are culturally conditioned to assert influence and demand results—a high-energy trait that psychologists call "agency." But decades of sacrificing sleep to fuel the country's rapid economic rise have created a dangerous backlog. Sleep is no longer viewed as a biological necessity, but as an obstacle to productivity.

According to cognitive psychologists, this sleep deficit is quietly undermining the nation's self-control. When people are chronically exhausted, empathy plummets, and public friction rises. The fix isn't just about popping supplements or buying gadgets; it requires fundamentally resetting daily routines. That means keeping smartphones in another room at night, soaking up morning sunlight to reset the body's internal clock, and—perhaps most importantly—relearning how to wind down without forcing the brain into sudden, exhausted blackouts.
