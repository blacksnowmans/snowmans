South Korea Faces Diplomatic Crisis Over U.S. Pressure to Send Troops to the Strait of Hormuz

September 08, 2026

The South Korean government is facing intense scrutiny as it weighs a controversial U.S. request to deploy military forces to the Strait of Hormuz—a move that risks violating the constitution and triggering a retaliatory strike from Iran.

  • The Pressure: Since the beginning of the year, the administration of U.S. President Donald Trump has been aggressively pushing South Korea to join a multinational coalition to protect shipping routes in the Middle East.
  • The Constitutional Hurdle: Article 5 of the South Korean Constitution explicitly prohibits participation in wars of aggression, creating a significant legal barrier to the deployment.
  • The Economic Stakes: With roughly 70% of South Korea’s crude oil passing through the Strait, any conflict with Iran puts billions of dollars in local infrastructure projects—managed by firms like Samsung C&T and Hyundai E&C—at severe risk.
  • Public Backlash: Recent polling shows a majority of the public opposes the deployment, with many viewing the government’s behind-the-scenes "testing of the waters" as a failure of democratic process.

A Dangerous Gamble on Alliances

The current administration is trapped in a classic "lose-lose" scenario. While the government hopes that dispatching troops will strengthen the South Korea-U.S. alliance and perhaps offer leverage in negotiations over the transfer of wartime operational control, the potential costs far outweigh these abstract gains. By caving to U.S. pressure, Seoul risks transforming itself into a combatant in a Middle Eastern conflict, abandoning the careful, principle-based diplomacy that has historically defined its international standing.

Furthermore, Iran has already issued stark warnings, with officials signaling that they would view any South Korean naval presence as an act of direct aggression. Even if the government classifies the deployment as "non-combat support," the reality on the ground is stark: once you enter a war zone to support one side, you become a legitimate target for the other. For a nation that relies heavily on Middle Eastern energy and construction contracts, this is an existential economic gamble.

Straying from the Constitutional Path

There is a growing perception that the government is trying to bypass the National Assembly’s authority through unofficial channels and "test" public opinion via anonymous leaks. This approach is not only undermining democratic legitimacy but is also failing to address the fundamental constitutional mandate that forbids South Korea from participating in aggressive wars.

While other allies—including Japan—have maintained a clear, independent stance by prioritizing national interest over knee-jerk compliance with U.S. demands, Seoul appears to be scrambling to appease Washington at the cost of its own security. To navigate this crisis, the government must move past the ambiguity of "careful consideration" and return to the foundational principles of its foreign policy: honoring the constitution and prioritizing the safety of its own citizens over the fleeting satisfaction of a diplomatic partner.
