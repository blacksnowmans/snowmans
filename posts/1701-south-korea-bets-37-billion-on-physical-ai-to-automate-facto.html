South Korea Bets $3.7 Billion on 'Physical AI' to Automate Factories and Logistics

October 30, 2026

South Korea is accelerating its transition from onscreen software to hardware-embedded "Physical AI," launching a 5 trillion won ($3.7 billion) national initiative to integrate artificial intelligence directly into industrial robotics, autonomous vehicles, and factory machinery.

  • Massive state funding: The Ministry of SMEs and Startups announced a plan to deploy over 5 trillion won ($3.7 billion) through 2030 for autonomous manufacturing, alongside a new Physical AI Training Center established by the Ministry of Science and ICT.
  • Regional industrial hubs: Cities like Jeonju and the Saemangeum industrial zone are being transformed into testing and certification grounds, leveraging major corporate projects including Hyundai Motor's investments.
  • Corporate race to scale: Local robotics maker Neuromeka targets mass production line deployment by 2027, on-device AI chipmaker DeepX is pushing low-power edge processors, and global tech giants Intel and SAP demonstrated real-time automated warehouse integration.
  • Broadening real-world applications: Physical AI is expanding beyond assembly lines into safety patrol robots, agricultural drones, and medical devices to counter labor shortages and workplace hazards.

From Virtual Models to Factory Floors

For years, the global artificial intelligence boom focused on onscreen text and image generation. South Korea is now making a concentrated push into "Physical AI"—embedding software intelligence into physical hardware capable of perceiving, deciding, and acting in real-world environments. Facing severe demographic decline and an aging industrial workforce, South Korea views hardware-integrated automation not merely as a tech trend, but as an economic necessity.

The government's multi-billion dollar commitment marks a departure from piecemeal research grants toward full-scale infrastructure buildouts. By creating dedicated training centers and leveraging public entities like Korea Post for real-world field trials, the administration aims to de-risk adoption for small and medium-sized enterprises that previously lacked the capital for advanced automation.

Commercial Execution and Regulatory Bottlenecks

The private sector is moving quickly to supply the necessary hardware and silicon. Local players like Neuromeka and edge-AI chip designer DeepX are building out low-power neural processing units (NPUs) and precision control systems. Meanwhile, international collaborations—such as Intel and SAP synchronizing enterprise resource planning software directly with autonomous mobile robots—highlight how enterprise data is moving off office screens and onto logistics floors.

Deploying physical machines around human workers creates operational and legal risks that software never encounters. While autonomous patrol robots developed by firms like Maum AI offer immediate value in hazard detection, national standards for liability, safety verification, and legal accountability remain incomplete. For South Korea to secure a lead in physical AI, policymakers will need to establish regulatory guardrails as fast as manufacturers roll out the hardware.
