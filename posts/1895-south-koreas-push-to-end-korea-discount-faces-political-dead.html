South Korea's Push to End "Korea Discount" Faces Political Deadlock

November 03, 2026

South Korea's capital markets are facing a severe crisis of confidence, with the benchmark Kospi index stalling at the 5,000–6,000 range rather than reaching anticipated highs, prompting urgent calls for systemic governance reform.

  • What happened: Retail investors are fleeing a market plagued by structural unfairness, while proposed reforms—including a mandatory 100% tender offer rule and strict limits on treasury shares—are being watered down or blocked in the National Assembly.
  • What it changes: If passed, the commercial law amendments would expand corporate directors' fiduciary duty to include ordinary shareholders, fundamentally shifting corporate governance away from controlling families.
  • Who is affected: South Korea's roughly 14 million retail investors, major conglomerates, and listed companies facing pressure to clear up low valuations.

The Root of the Korea Discount: Protecting Controlling Families Over Shareholders

The core issue plaguing South Korea's stock market isn't a lack of corporate earnings, but rather a deep-seated governance structure where corporate profits and management control premiums benefit controlling families at the expense of minority shareholders. During mergers and acquisitions, controlling shareholders routinely pocket high premiums while retail investors are left absorbing sudden stock drops. While the progressive ruling Democratic Party and President Lee Jae-myung's administration push for sweeping commercial law reforms to mandate fairness, the conservative opposition People Power Party has repeatedly pushed back, weakening key provisions such as the mandatory public tender offer rule down to a watered-down 50%+1 threshold.

Compounding the problem is corporate abuse of treasury shares. Rather than burning bought-back shares to boost investor value—a standard global practice—many South Korean firms rewrite their corporate bylaws to stockpile treasury shares as tools for defending management control. A recent analysis by the civic group Economic Reform Research Institute revealed that 160 out of 268 listed firms adjusted their bylaws during recent shareholder meetings precisely to bypass treasury share retirement, a tactic critics condemn as legalized looting of shareholder capital.

Commercial Law Reform as a Matter of National Survival

Frustrated retail investors are increasingly comparing local markets unfavorably with overseas alternatives like the US, where shareholder rights are more reliably protected. For South Korea to finally shake off the stubborn "Korea Discount" and transition toward a "Korea Premium," policymakers must stop treating stock market index targets as mere PR talking points. Enacting robust commercial law reforms that explicitly hold corporate boards legally accountable to all shareholders—not just the company as an abstract entity—is no longer a policy choice; it is an economic survival test for the nation's financial democracy.
