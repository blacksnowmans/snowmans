German Chancellor to Visit Seoul as Middle East Tensions and Oil Shocks Threaten Korean Economy

October 25, 2026

Surging crude prices driven by escalating Middle East tensions and volatile US trade maneuvers are intensifying pressure on South Korea’s export-driven economy just as President Lee Jae-myung prepares for a critical summit with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

  • Energy markets rattle: Brent crude spiked past $116 per barrel following Donald Trump’s threats of an "Epic Fury" military operation against Iran, threatening South Korea’s manufacturing costs and trade balance.
  • Seoul-Berlin summit scheduled: German Chancellor Friedrich Merz will visit Seoul on October 29 to meet President Lee, focusing on artificial intelligence, semiconductors, supply chains, and defense industrial cooperation.
  • Uneasy shifts in Eastern Europe: Russia and Ukraine agreed to a localized humanitarian truce in Oleshky ahead of proposed trilateral talks in the UAE this November, signaling potential realignments in defense procurement and post-war reconstruction.
  • Transactional trade crosscurrents: Ahead of US midterms, Trump partially lifted a 50% tariff on Canadian imports while demanding that the European Union dilute its big-tech regulations in exchange for steel tariff relief.

Pragmatic Diplomacy in an Era of Energy Volatility

For South Korea, which depends entirely on imported crude, oil breaching triple digits is an immediate blow to consumer prices and trade balances. Energy price spikes alongside market jitters underscore the urgency of President Lee Jae-myung’s push for pragmatic, diversified economic diplomacy. The upcoming bilateral meeting with Chancellor Merz offers Seoul an essential opportunity to deepen cooperation in advanced manufacturing, chips, and artificial intelligence with Europe’s largest economy, helping cushion Korean exporters against wider global headwinds.

This engagement also reflects a conscious effort to avoid the missteps of foreign policy during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, which faced widespread criticism for dogmatic alignment that generated needless friction and neglected commercial self-interest. By anchoring the German summit in supply-chain security and joint defense opportunities, the current administration is seeking practical economic dividends rather than symbolic posturing.

Surviving Great-Power Transactionalism

Washington’s willingness to leverage steel duties against Europe’s digital regulations, alongside sudden tariff shifts driven by domestic election cycles, reveals the starkly transactional nature of the emerging global order. At the same time, regional powers like Japan are moving to expand the scope of their defense budgets to align with US strategic demands. For South Korea, surviving this environment requires tightrope diplomacy: keeping domestic living costs manageable amid oil price volatility while leveraging Korea’s industrial strengths to stay indispensable to both Western partners and broader global markets.
