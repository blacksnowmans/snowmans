South Korean President’s Approval Rating Sinks for Eighth Consecutive Week

September 10, 2026

  • The Slide: President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is facing an eight-week decline in approval, signaling profound disillusionment among core supporters and younger voters.
  • The "Silent Counterattack": Critics allege the prosecution is quietly expanding its administrative power through new organizational decrees to bypass legislative oversight.
  • Youth Discontent: Voters in their 20s and 30s are expressing deep economic frustration, feeling that the government has failed to deliver on property and financial stability.
  • Legislative Warning: The opposition-controlled National Assembly is being urged to aggressively block the prosecution’s personnel and budget expansion to prevent a shift toward unchecked judicial power.

The Prosecution’s "Administrative" Power Grab

The current political crisis is rooted in what observers describe as a "silent counterattack" by the prosecution. The government’s recent plan to reorganize the internal structure of the Prosecutors' Office—specifically regarding how they handle cases—was reportedly pushed through quietly, catching even high-level political figures by surprise. By utilizing administrative decrees rather than open legislation, the prosecution is accused of circumventing legal oversight to secure more personnel and budget, effectively cementing its entrenched interests under the guise of organizational management. Critics argue that the proposed structure ignores the needs of victims and instead prioritizes the prosecution’s prestige. Rather than moving toward a system where the prosecutor who indicts a case also manages the trial—a standard for accountability—the current plan allegedly reinforces a fragmented system that allows prosecutors to rotate through courts without deep case knowledge. For the Democratic Party of Korea, the main liberal opposition, the failure to stop this maneuver is being framed as a strategic defeat, prompting calls for a more "hard-nosed" approach to legislative blocking.

Why the Support Base is Evaporating

The decline in approval ratings is not merely a fluctuating poll number; it is a symptom of a widening gap between expectation and reality. Hardcore supporters who once acted as a protective shield for the administration are becoming fatigued, feeling that their policy goals have reached a "zero-delivery" status. The sense of betrayal is most acute among political "super-users"—those who actively campaigned and pushed for this administration, only to feel alienated by internal infighting and a perceived lack of transparency. For the younger demographic, the disappointment is inherently economic. Many voters in their 20s and 30s feel trapped by South Korea’s "property-won’t-fail" culture and are struggling with stock market losses and limited upward mobility. When the government intervenes in issues—such as recent labor disputes at Samsung Electronics or sports equipment controversies—it is increasingly met with skepticism rather than relief. For a generation that feels the ladder of opportunity has been kicked away, the administration’s performance is being graded with cold, unforgiving scrutiny. The message is clear: if the government cannot prove it stands on the side of the marginalized, it will continue to lose the battle for public sentiment.
