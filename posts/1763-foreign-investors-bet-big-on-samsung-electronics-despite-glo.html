Foreign Investors Bet Big on Samsung Electronics Despite Global AI Skepticism

October 31, 2026

Foreign investors are aggressively accumulating shares of Samsung Electronics at valuation lows, signaling a strong "long" position even as global markets tremble over a potential AI bubble.

  • Contrarian Buying: Despite a broader market sell-off, foreign investors have been seen buying blocks of 1.5 million shares of Samsung Electronics during morning price gaps, treating current levels as a strategic floor.
  • HBM Dominance: SK Hynix, South Korea’s leading manufacturer of high-bandwidth memory (HBM), reports that its next-generation DDR5 and HBM supplies are essentially sold out through 2027, backed by long-term contracts.
  • Cloud Growth: While traditional search advertising faces pressure, cloud revenue from "Big Tech" firms like Microsoft and Alphabet continues to exceed expectations, proving that AI infrastructure is already generating significant B2B revenue.
  • Market Stability: Capital is not fleeing to safe havens like gold or treasuries; instead, it is sitting in liquid money market funds, suggesting a temporary correction rather than a structural bear market.

Foreign Investors Signal a "Long" Position on Korean Chips

The recent volatility in the Seoul stock market, triggered by earnings reports from U.S. tech giants, has masked a significant shift in institutional behavior. While retail investors are panicking over headlines suggesting an "AI peak," foreign capital is quietly flowing into Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. This isn't a speculative "hot money" play; analysts view it as a long-term positioning by global passive funds and institutional investors who recognize that the Korean semiconductor sector is currently undervalued.

The skepticism surrounding AI capital expenditure—estimated to reach nearly $950 billion (about 1,300 trillion KRW) next year—is often compared to the dot-com bubble of the early 2000s. However, the financial fundamentals tell a different story. Unlike the debt-ridden startups of the 1990s, today’s tech leaders are financing their AI infrastructure through massive internal cash flows and high-grade corporate bonds. For Korean suppliers, this translates into a more stable, demand-driven market where 50% of orders are secured by multi-year supply agreements.

Why the AI Bubble Narrative Fails to Match Financial Reality

The Lee Jae-myung administration, South Korea's current government led by the Democratic Party, is presiding over a market that is increasingly decoupled from the chaotic "Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era." Under the current leadership, the focus has shifted toward stabilizing the semiconductor supply chain as a matter of national security. Critics who cite the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol as a source of market instability are missing the bigger picture: the global tech industry's physical reliance on Korean silicon is stronger than any local political noise.

From an interpretive standpoint, the market is currently experiencing a "hallucination" regarding earnings. While Alphabet and Amazon saw shares dip due to misses in traditional ad sectors, their AI-driven cloud divisions are booming. This shift from search to "Action AI"—where models perform tasks rather than just retrieving information—requires even more advanced hardware. As long as the U.S. Federal Reserve maintains its "strong" outlook on the economy, the current dip in chip stocks looks less like a crash and more like a necessary valuation reset before the next leg of the AI expansion.

"The demand for HBM and high-performance DRAM remains rock-solid; the current price weakness is a function of sentiment, not a collapse in the industrial cycle."

In short, the "smart money" is looking past the quarterly noise. By accumulating Samsung at these levels, foreign investors are betting that the transition from AI training to AI inference will keep Korean factories running at full capacity for the remainder of the decade.
