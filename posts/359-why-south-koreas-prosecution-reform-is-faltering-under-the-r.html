Why South Korea's Prosecution Reform is Faltering Under the Ruling Party

September 07, 2026

  • What happened: The South Korean government's proposed prosecution reforms and organizational restructuring have sparked severe backlash from reformists and core supporters, who accuse the administration of diluting key changes and preserving prosecutorial powers through bureaucratic workarounds.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung's administration, the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal party—and its traditional progressive base, which is experiencing a deep ideological fracture.
  • What it changes: Stalled reforms, perceived hypocrisy among political elites, and internal factional fighting have triggered a notable slide in the administration's approval ratings ahead of upcoming local elections.

When political commentators on South Korean political YouTube channels debate the state of the nation, the underlying contradictions are often more telling than the official rhetoric. Outspoken former and current political figures have recently pulled back the curtain on why a ruling party with a parliamentary majority hesitates to push through long-promised changes, revealing an uncomfortable internal struggle over who truly wields institutional power.

The core of the current crisis lies in policy inconsistency and political self-contradiction. Figures who previously attacked President Lee Jae-myung during his campaign have refashioned themselves as his defenders under the new administration, alienating the progressive base in a clumsy bid to court centrist voters. By attempting to split the difference between traditional supporters and political opponents, the ruling bloc risks losing both sides.

The Battle Over Prosecution Reform and Administrative Loopholes

The most explosive flashpoint centers on the stalled dismantling of the prosecution's sweeping investigative authority. Critics argue that despite campaign promises of irreversible reform, recent administrative proposals have left backdoor channels open for prosecutors to retain investigative oversight. Rather than stripping away unchecked power, the restructuring of investigative units and the creation of new oversight bodies have left core supporters feeling betrayed, drawing sharp rebukes from commentators who see the moves as a retreat from core democratic mandates.

In our view, this hesitation exposes a dangerous blind spot among political elites who treat democratic mandates as negotiable. When a reform-minded administration starts using bureaucratic workarounds—reminiscent of past conservative tactics—to preserve state power, it invites public cynicism. The ensuing drop in approval ratings is not merely a tactical communication failure; it is a direct result of voters sensing a retreat from core promises of institutional transparency and fairness.
