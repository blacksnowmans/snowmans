South Korea's Ruling Party Fractures as Staff Reshuffle Backfires

October 06, 2026

South Korea's progressive ruling bloc is facing a severe internal crisis, marked by plunging presidential approval ratings, open infighting, and key allies refusing to take up top administrative posts.

  • The Chief of Staff Vacancy: President Lee Jae-myung's ruling Democratic Party faces a paralysis in the presidential office after key pro-Lee lawmakers refused to take the Chief of Staff job, fearing it is a political dead end ("suicide squad" posting) that could trigger a disastrous by-election in the capital region.
  • Public Feuds: Bitter factional disputes have broken out between party leadership and the presidential office, highlighted by a snub of senior lawmaker Jung Chung-rae at a recent airport arrival.
  • Internal Dissent: Even loyal reformist figures, such as senior prosecutor Im Eun-jung, have publicly revolted against recent high-level government appointments, exposing deep cracks in the administration's reform agenda.

A Government Running Out of Options

The core of the current political turmoil lies in a toxic mix of plunging approval ratings for President Lee Jae-myung and a ruling party desperate to avoid collateral damage. When former Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-shik stepped down amid vetting failures and falling poll numbers, the administration expected a line of eager party heavyweights to step up. Instead, core pro-Lee figures ducked for cover. Taking the job means abandoning a safe metropolitan parliamentary seat to join a sinking ship, opening the door for the conservative opposition in potential by-elections. With no political heavyweights willing to take the fall, the presidential office was reduced to floating the name of Kim Min-ki, the current National Assembly Secretary-General, as a little-known stopgap.

This reluctance reflects a broader loss of trust. Rank-and-file party members and online supporters are openly expressing cynicism, pointing out that the administration's troubles stem from a refusal to practice basic restraint and dialogue. Rather than addressing policy missteps—such as rigid real estate regulations and rushed prosecutorial reforms—the leadership continues to rely on insular factional calculations. When even reliable allies and reform icons like Prosecutor Im Eun-jung break ranks to publicly hammer the administration over controversial personnel picks like the appointment of Kim Ji-yong, it signals that the ruling bloc's problems go far beyond mere optics.

Upcoming Local Elections Look Bleak

The compounding crises have left the Democratic Party deeply anxious about the upcoming Seoul mayoral by-election. With public sentiment turning cold and even traditional strongholds showing signs of fatigue, party strategists privately fear that running a candidate could result in a crushing defeat that serves as a national referendum on the administration. Talk of unconventional outside recruits or forced opposition unity highlights a leadership grasping for straws.

Ultimately, the administration is paying the price for sidelining the fairness and transparency it once promised. By treating internal governance as a zero-sum game of factional control, the ruling camp has alienated its own base. Unless the top leadership abandons its heavy-handed approach and genuinely listens to bottom-up feedback, these personnel and policy crises will remain a recurring fuse for the government's undoing.
