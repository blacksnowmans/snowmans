How Geography and Germs Shaped Global Inequality: Lessons from Jared Diamond's 'Guns, Germs, and Steel'

October 17, 2026

In 1532, a force of just 168 Spanish soldiers routed an Inca army of 80,000 at Cajamarca, proving that global inequality was forged not by racial superiority, but by environmental and geographic luck.

  • The Clash: Francisco Pizarro’s tiny troop captured Inca Emperor Atahualpa by exploiting written military history, psychological shock, and absolute complacency.
  • The Invisible Killer: European germs—specifically smallpox and measles, cultivated through centuries of close livestock farming—wiped out roughly 95% of the indigenous population in the Americas.
  • Geographic Luck: Eurasia's horizontal axis allowed crops and livestock to spread easily across matching latitudes, accelerating agriculture, population growth, and technological accumulation.
  • The Modern Lesson: Just as 16th-century geography and information systems predetermined the Incas' fate, today's socio-economic environments dictate life trajectories—making institutional safety nets essential.

The Mechanics of Conquest: Literacy, Horses, and Germs

Pizarro’s victory was not a fluke, but the product of accumulated data. Unlike the Incas, who relied solely on oral tradition and had no warning of the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire 13 years prior, Europeans recorded and shared military strategies in writing. When the Spanish arrived, Emperor Atahualpa viewed them as curiosities rather than threats, walking into an ambush with an unarmed procession adorned in gold while Spanish forces waited with tactical precision.

Beyond information, technological and biological gaps sealed the Inca Empire's doom. The unfamiliar thunder of crude firearms and the terrifying mobility of horses—animals previously unknown in the Americas—paralyzed the Inca army with panic. Yet the deadliest weapon was entirely unintentional: pathogens. Because Native Americans lacked immunity to Eurasian crowd diseases, epidemics decimated their society and governance even before European armies clashed with them in battle.

Geographic Determinism and Modern Parallels

Jared Diamond's environmental determinism explains why Europe, rather than the Americas or Africa, conquered the globe. Eurasia stretched horizontally along an east-west axis, meaning climates and day lengths remained consistent across thousands of miles. This allowed agricultural innovations and domesticable animals—such as cows, pigs, and sheep—to spread rapidly. In contrast, the north-south axes of the Americas and Africa created climatic barriers that slowed down the diffusion of crops and technology.

This structural inequality carries heavy philosophical implications for modern society. Just as the Incas could not overcome the biological and technological disadvantages built into their geography, modern individuals often find their life trajectories constrained by the socio-economic environments they are born into. While critics warn against treating environmental determinism as an excuse for fatalism, progressive governance seeks to counter these inherent disparities. Expanding welfare systems and social safety nets—much like the policy directions emphasized by the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung—aims to dismantle these systemic barriers, ensuring that accidents of birth do not dictate human potential.
