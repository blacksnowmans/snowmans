The High Cost of Trump’s "Allies-Pay" Diplomacy

October 01, 2026

South Korea is facing immense pressure to commit up to $200 billion (approx. 270 trillion KRW) in investments to the United States, including a controversial $37 billion (50 trillion KRW) Alaska LNG pipeline project that was previously shelved for its lack of economic viability.

  • What happened: The Trump administration is pushing Korea to fund massive energy and infrastructure projects in the U.S.
  • The "Alaska" Factor: The Alaska LNG project is widely viewed as a failing, uneconomical venture that major energy companies abandoned years ago.
  • Political Motive: Experts suggest the timing is tied to the upcoming U.S. midterm elections, with the White House using allied funds to boost the political standing of local Republican incumbents.
  • The Impact: Korea faces the risk of sinking massive capital into failing U.S. projects, effectively subsidizing American domestic politics under the guise of an "alliance."

A Debt-Fueled Diplomatic Tax

The Alaska LNG project is, by all traditional metrics, a financial "zombie." After the U.S. shale gas revolution made Alaskan gas uncompetitive, major players like ExxonMobil and BP withdrew from the project in 2016. The fact that the South Korean government is now being pressured to revive this "white elephant" suggests that the U.S. is not treating this as an investment, but as a diplomatic bill for its allies to pay.

This is a hallmark of the Trump administration’s "transactional" diplomacy: bundling security, infrastructure, and nuclear energy deals to extract concessions. By placing these projects in regions represented by key political allies, such as Alaska Senator Dan Sullivan, the White House is essentially using South Korean taxpayers' money to secure votes in the U.S. midterm elections.

Geopolitical Maneuvering in the Middle East

While Korea balances these demands, the U.S. is engaged in a high-stakes, behind-the-scenes dance with Iran. Reports suggest a "seven-day confidence-building measure" is on the table, involving the potential lifting of maritime sanctions and the release of frozen Iranian assets in exchange for the safe opening of the Strait of Hormuz.

Though the Trump administration publicly maintains a hardline stance against Tehran, this dual-track strategy—public defiance matched with private negotiation—is standard procedure. For Korea, which relies heavily on Middle Eastern oil, the stability of the Strait of Hormuz is a matter of national security. However, Seoul must be wary: as Washington maneuvers through its own political cycle, Korea must ensure it is not forced to bear the financial burden of these geopolitical shifts.

The Case for Diplomatic Autonomy

The era of "blind alliance" is over. Whether it is $37 billion for a redundant gas pipeline or the broader pressure to subsidize U.S. domestic economic goals, the South Korean government must pivot toward a strategy of cold, calculated pragmatism. Standing up to unreasonable demands does not weaken an alliance; it strengthens the nation's position at the negotiating table.

Moving forward, Seoul’s priority must remain its own national interest. If the government continues to act as a financial cushion for U.S. political theatre, it risks sacrificing the economic future of its own people to satisfy the short-term political needs of a foreign power. It is time to prioritize "Realpolitik" over the optics of friendship.
