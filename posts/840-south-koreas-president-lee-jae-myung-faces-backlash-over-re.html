South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Faces Backlash Over Re-election Rumors and Troubled Reforms

September 21, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung's administration is battling a sharp drop in public support as debates over a potential presidential term extension, botched appointments, and a controversial troop deployment overshadow economic livelihoods.

  • Re-election speculation: Rumors of a "3+4" constitutional reform plan—cutting the current single five-year term to three years while allowing a four-year consecutive term—have triggered intense political infighting. While the Blue House claims President Lee has no plans to run again, Lee has stopped short of issuing an explicit denial.
  • Plunging approval: Recent polling from the National Barometer Survey shows the ruling Democratic Party’s support slipping to 39%, driven by public frustration over opaque bureaucratic appointments and heavy-handed executive decisions.
  • Controversial troop deployment: Despite broad public and voter opposition, the government is pushing forward with plans to deploy troops near the Strait of Hormuz, a high-risk move critics call a reckless gamble for international prestige.
  • Stalled reforms: Promised prosecutorial and institutional overhauls have stalled, with critics arguing the administration has traded genuine systemic reform for internal factional politics and self-preservation.

The fixation on presidential longevity highlights a deep disconnect in Seoul's political arena. While everyday citizens grapple with economic pressures, the political class is consumed by a high-stakes guessing game over whether President Lee intends to cling to power beyond his constitutional limits. By leaving room for ambiguity rather than definitively shutting down the re-election chatter, the leadership has invited endless speculation, turning constitutional reform from a debate on civil rights and governance into a transparent exercise in political survival. Over 60% of the public opposes applying consecutive terms to the sitting president, signaling that voters see right through the political maneuvering.

Compounding these political wounds are compounding missteps in governance and foreign policy. The administration's personnel choices—marked by unexplained appointments and a lack of basic transparency—have alienated even core supporters. Meanwhile, pushing a risky military deployment to the Middle East without building domestic consensus looks less like a responsible foreign policy contribution and more like a desperate bid to manufacture external urgency amid sliding domestic ratings. When a reforming government starts treating the public's demand for accountability as a nuisance rather than a mandate, it risks transforming itself from the agent of change into the very obstacle that needs removing.
