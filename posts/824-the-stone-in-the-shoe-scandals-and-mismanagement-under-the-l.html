The "Stone in the Shoe": Scandals and Mismanagement Under the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 08, 2026

Leaked audio recordings from the inner circle of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung have ignited a firestorm of controversy, exposing a culture of cronyism, administrative incompetence, and an alarming disconnect from the public. The leaks suggest that a "B-grade" leadership style—marked by the systematic exclusion of high-level talent to protect personal influence—is paralyzing the administration’s core functions.

  • The "Stone in the Shoe": Critics argue that President Lee is being held back by his own inner circle, specifically aides like Kim Hyun-ji, who are allegedly filling public roles with personal acquaintances.
  • Financial Irresponsibility: Recorded conversations reveal aides dismissing a 60 million won (about $45,000) discrepancy in campaign funds as pocket change, despite the overall 50 billion won (about $37 million) scale of operations.
  • Diplomatic Retreat: The government reportedly refused to nominate a South Korean candidate for the WHO Director-General position, citing a defeatist "why bother" attitude toward international leadership.
  • Rising Public Distrust: Growing allegations of "parachute appointments" (patronage-based hiring) at state-run companies are fueling comparisons to the corrupt practices of the past, threatening the administration’s moral standing.

The Anatomy of Administrative Failure

The core of the issue lies in the administration's staffing philosophy. Political observers note that when a leader is "A-grade," they surround themselves with other high-performing experts. Conversely, "B-grade" leaders intentionally block top-tier talent to hide their own inadequacies. This trend, now being dubbed the "Lee Jae-myung 인사(personnel) law," explains why qualified outsiders are being pushed aside in favor of loyalists with personal ties to figures like Kim Hyun-ji.

The leaked recordings provide a damning window into this decision-making process. Officials were heard stressing over the lack of proper oversight for massive financial transactions, while simultaneously treating tens of thousands of dollars with casual arrogance. When power becomes privatized—where who you know matters more than your merit—the institution begins to rot from within. This environment is not just an internal problem; it is a signal of a deepening "lame duck" phase that major conservative outlets like The Chosun Ilbo are already beginning to scrutinize.

Diplomacy and Digital Distractions

Beyond domestic personnel scandals, the administration’s foreign policy is facing backlash for its lack of ambition. By refusing to field candidates for international organizations like the WHO, the Foreign Ministry has demonstrated a passive, defeatist culture. This "why play if we might lose?" mindset stands in stark contrast to South Korea’s status as a global leader, leaving many citizens frustrated by the government’s inability to project soft power.

Furthermore, President Lee’s own behavior on social media has drawn bewildered criticism. Observers point out that the President often ignores thoughtful political discourse, instead seeking out and retweeting aggressive, vitriolic trolls who attack his critics. This obsession with digital validation from fringe accounts—rather than addressing the "stones in his shoe" like the current staffing scandals—suggests a leader increasingly detached from the reality of governing.

For the average citizen, the pattern is clear: a government that prioritizes private relationships over public interest is destined to falter. Unless the administration acts to clear out the personnel causing these administrative roadblocks, the "stones" currently stuck in their shoes will eventually cause the entire machine to collapse.
