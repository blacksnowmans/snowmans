How YouTube Toppled South Korea's Legacy Broadcasters

September 24, 2026

South Korea’s media landscape has fundamentally inverted, with an independent YouTube news channel overtaking legacy broadcasters to claim the number-one spot in national trust.

  • The Shift: A recent joint poll by SisaIN and Korea Gallup ranked the YouTube show Kim Ou-joon's Humble Opinion News Factory as South Korea's most trusted media outlet at 6.3%, beating traditional heavyweights like MBC's Newsdesk (5.9%) and KBS's News9 (4.9%).
  • The Driver: Rather than just moving platforms, the channel positioned itself as a "legacy broadcaster without a transmission tower," combining alternative journalism with deep listener loyalty built over decades.
  • The Divide: While the rise of independent digital media breaks the monopoly of traditional networks, it occurs in a heavily polarized society where 74% of South Koreans do not trust those with opposing political views.

This milestone represents more than a platform shift; it exposes a deep crisis in public trust. Traditional public broadcasters like MBC and KBS have seen their authority eroded not by superior state-of-the-art technology, but by alternative channels that successfully weaponize audience grievance and political alignment. When news consumption is driven by algorithms rewarding partisanship, media outlets frequently shift from objective reporting to amplifying anger for political factions.

Simultaneously, institutional rot extends well beyond the press corps. The South Korean judiciary—long held as an unassailable pillar of the state—is facing its own credibility crisis following revelations that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae's office racked up questionable administrative expenses, including massive wine purchases and manipulated headcounts for dining budgets. Paired with ongoing trials of former military commanders tied to martial law attempts, the public is forced to reckon with systemic abuses by those tasked with upholding the law.

Ultimately, President Lee Jae-myung’s recent 100-minute press conference offered a brief window for a political rebound, pushing his approval rating up to 34.8%. Yet, winning back alienated voters requires more than public apologies; it demands structural reform and economic delivery. As South Korea navigates this polarized era, the real danger is not just that people disagree, but that they no longer recognize each other's right to speak the truth.
