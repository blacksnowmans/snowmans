Record Earnings, Uneasy Markets: The Structural Dilemma Facing Korea’s Chip Giants

October 28, 2026

South Korean memory chip maker SK Hynix shattered market forecasts with all-time high earnings—posting over 104 trillion won (around $75 billion) in revenue and 83 trillion won ($60 billion) in operating profit—yet lukewarm stock performance highlights growing market unease over macroeconomic risks, regulatory friction, and intensifying Chinese competition.

  • Record-breaking figures: SK Hynix beat market consensus across the board, demonstrating strong pricing power in the ongoing artificial intelligence boom.
  • Divergent shareholder payouts: SK Hynix sparked a double-digit rally by pledging to retire 40 trillion won ($29 billion) worth of treasury shares, while Samsung Electronics struggled due to structural constraints under South Korea’s financial separation regulations.
  • Extreme market concentration: Together, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix represent roughly 57% of the benchmark Kospi index's total market capitalization, leaving the entire South Korean stock market exposed to semiconductor cycles.
  • Surging competition from China: State-backed Chinese memory maker CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies) is steadily eating into South Korean market share in DRAM, backed by 10-year supply deals with domestic tech giants like Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, and ByteDance.

Why Record Profits Aren't Driving a Broader Rally

The mismatch between blowout earnings and market hesitation reflects the classic investor rule of selling on the news. Robust quarterly figures merely confirm past performance during the July-September period. What concerns global investors today is the external climate: volatile crude prices, shifting interest rate expectations, and a cooling pace of infrastructure spending as Big Tech firms work to optimize power consumption rather than buy hardware blindly.

Moreover, corporate governance barriers continue to dictate how these cash reserves reach shareholders. SK Hynix’s controlling holding firm, SK Square, leveraged a massive 3.3% share cancellation to defend its mandatory 20% ownership threshold under fair trade rules while boosting per-share value. Samsung Electronics, by contrast, faces restrictions under South Korea's Act on the Structural Improvement of the Financial Industry (commonly known as Geumsanbeop). Because its affiliates Samsung Life Insurance and Samsung Fire & Marine Insurance already hit the 10% legal cap on Samsung Electronics holdings, any major retirement of common stock would trigger forced sell-offs by those affiliates, compelling the company to prioritize cash dividends over share buybacks.

The Looming Chinese Challenge and Next-Gen Architecture

Beneath the headline numbers lies a fundamental erosion of Korea's global DRAM moat, which has gradually shrunk from historical highs of 75% down toward the low 60% range. Washington’s stringent chip export controls inadvertently accelerated China’s internal supply chain self-sufficiency. Chinese cloud platforms are locking in long-term procurement pacts with domestic suppliers, gradually sapping South Korean chipmakers of future pricing leverage in standard enterprise hardware.

To preserve their technological edge, both Korean giants are pivoting toward custom post-High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) architectures. Samsung is doubling down on specialized 3D memory designs (GHBM) aimed at mitigating thermal bottlenecks, while SK Hynix is promoting High Bandwidth Flash (HBF)—an open architecture using stacked NAND flash to address capacity limits in AI inference workloads. Whether these cutting-edge roadmaps can counter macroeconomic volatility and structural market concentration remains the critical test for Korean equity markets.
