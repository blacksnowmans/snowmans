The Perilous Consolidation of Power Under President Lee Jae-myung

October 16, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is facing intense criticism as critics argue his governance style has shifted from public service to the privatization of state power, threatening the country's democratic checks and balances.

  • Concentration of Power: The administration is attempting to consolidate the prosecution, police, and defense ministry under the direct control of the Ministry of National Defense, a move opponents warn bypasses traditional oversight.
  • Controversial Appointments: The forceful appointment of Kim Ji-yong as head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (a new body tasked with high-level investigations) was pushed through with a confirmation report in just five hours, sidelining parliamentary vetting.
  • Economic Instability: Despite government claims of national economic growth, major tech stocks have plummeted, with Samsung Electronics falling below 130,000 won (about $95) and SK Hynix shares halving in value from their recent highs.
  • Political Polarization: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the country’s primary liberal party—faces internal fractures as some members break ranks to criticize the President’s increasingly unilateral decision-making.

The Privatization of State Institutions

President Lee’s administration is increasingly defined by a high-stakes strategy of "complicity." By placing loyalists in key positions and demanding unwavering support, the President appears to be transforming the state apparatus into a personal shield. This shift is most visible in the recent appointment of Kim Ji-yong. The administration’s disregard for standard parliamentary review processes signals a departure from the democratic norms the President once championed, specifically regarding the separation of investigation and prosecution powers.

The proposed integration of the prosecution and police into the Ministry of National Defense is particularly alarming to observers. By centralizing law enforcement and military investigation capabilities under the executive's direct influence, the administration is effectively neutralizing the "check and balance" system. This move suggests an attempt to insulate the presidency from external scrutiny, effectively turning state institutions into tools for political survival rather than public interest.

Economic Mirage vs. Reality

While the President touts the prospect of a $40,000 per capita income, the reality for the average citizen is one of soaring food prices and stagnant growth. The government’s narrative of success is being undermined by a catastrophic decline in the semiconductor sector—the backbone of the Korean economy. With major players like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix seeing their stock prices crater, the administration’s refusal to accept responsibility—instead blaming market volatility—has alienated both professional investors and the broader public.

The current political climate has left the ruling Democratic Party struggling to reconcile its identity. It must now choose between remaining a "bulletproof" party dedicated to protecting the President at all costs or reclaiming its role as a representative of democratic values. With the President’s approval ratings slipping and internal dissent growing, the administration is approaching a critical juncture where the survival of the current political order may depend on its ability to accept the very oversight it currently seeks to dismantle.
