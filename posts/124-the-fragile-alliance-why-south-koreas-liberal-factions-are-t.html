The Fragile Alliance: Why South Korea’s Liberal Factions Are Turning on Each Other

September 29, 2026

  • Failed Reconciliation: A high-profile attempt to bridge the gap between South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and influential liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min has collapsed due to poor mediation.
  • Internal Fractures: The incident highlights a widening rift within the liberal bloc, pitting the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction against traditional supporters of former President Moon Jae-in.
  • Political Risk: With approval ratings hovering around 30%, the administration’s focus on internal purges and legal threats against critics is seen as a major distraction from core policy failures.
  • The "Inside Gunfire" Trap: Critics argue the administration is alienating its base by labeling constructive criticism as internal betrayal, a strategy that echoes the self-destructive patterns of previous failed presidencies.

A Failure of Diplomacy

The breakdown of the meeting between President Lee and Rhyu was largely a self-inflicted wound. In a miscalculation of optics and history, the administration selected an intermediary whom Rhyu deeply distrusts. For the President, this was a failure of basic political intelligence; in the insular world of Korean politics, who you choose to deliver a message is often as important as the message itself. By failing to utilize neutral or respected figures to bridge the divide, the administration effectively turned a potential peace offering into a source of fresh conflict. Rather than treating critical voices as a "supplementary" tool for better governance, the administration has resorted to aggressive tactics, including rumored legal action against its own ideological allies. This has fueled a climate where loyal dissent is rebranded as "internal gunfire," causing political paralysis. Supporters are left wondering why the government is prioritizing the silencing of voices like Rhyu’s over the substantive, necessary work of economic reform.

Echoes of the Past

For many observers, the current infighting is a haunting reminder of the decline of the Roh Moo-hyun administration, whose internal divisions paved the way for a conservative takeover and subsequent political purges. The liberal camp is currently split: the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition) is attempting to maintain distance from Rhyu’s biting critiques, while the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, more radical liberal group—argues that these critiques are the "bitter medicine" the government desperately needs to survive. The danger is not just in the argument itself, but in the obsession with factional purity. By alienating core supporters and prioritizing legalistic responses over political reconciliation, the administration is losing its grip on the narrative. As conservative figures like Han Dong-hoon (leader of the People Power Party, the main conservative party) gain momentum, the liberals are increasingly trapped in a feedback loop of YouTube-driven conspiracy theories and echo-chamber vitriol. If the administration cannot pivot from defensive posturing to inclusive leadership, it risks not only its own political future but the viability of its entire platform.
