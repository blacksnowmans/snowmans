The High-Stakes Confirmation Hearing of Defense Minister Nominee Kang Shin-chul

September 17, 2026

South Korea’s defense policy and the military's role in democratic governance were the focal points of a rigorous confirmation hearing for Defense Minister nominee Kang Shin-chul. The session moved beyond the typical formalities to address the most sensitive issues facing the ROK Armed Forces.

  • Hormuz Strait Deployment: The nominee emphasized a focus on national interest and public safety, though critics remain wary of the potential for the military to be pulled into U.S.-led strategic conflicts.
  • Military Education Reform: Kang endorsed the long-debated consolidation of the Army, Navy, and Air Force academies, aiming to foster joint operational capabilities from the start of an officer’s career.
  • 12·3 Martial Law Accountability: In a critical moment, the nominee labeled the December 3 illegal martial law declaration an "obvious insurrection," accepting responsibility as a high-ranking official at the time.

A Test of Neutrality and Reform

The hearing highlighted the precarious position of the South Korean military, which has historically struggled to balance its status as an ally with the need for independent, sovereign decision-making. Kang’s stance on the Hormuz Strait—vaguely prioritizing "national interest"—underscores the classic dilemma: how to satisfy alliance obligations to the U.S. without compromising the safety of South Korean troops in volatile regions.

The commitment to reforming military academies is similarly ambitious. While the necessity of integrated training is widely acknowledged by experts, the proposal faces decades of ingrained resistance from institutional factions within the different service branches. For this reform to move past empty rhetoric, the Ministry of Defense will need a concrete roadmap that can withstand significant internal pushback.

Confronting the 12·3 Legacy

The most jarring element of the hearing was the candid discussion regarding the December 3 martial law incident. By publicly condemning the event as an "insurrection," Kang has set a new tone for accountability. However, acknowledging the wrong is only the first step. The systemic vulnerability that allowed the military to be utilized as a tool for political suppression remains a structural threat.

Ultimately, the hearing serves as a reminder that the health of South Korean democracy is inseparable from the neutrality of its military. If the Ministry of Defense is to regain public trust, it must move beyond verbal apologies and toward ironclad institutional safeguards. Legislative and procedural barriers must be established to ensure that no government, regardless of its political agenda, can weaponize the armed forces against the constitution. The nominee's "heavy sense of responsibility" will only be validated if it translates into a military that is firmly under democratic control, shielded from political whims.
