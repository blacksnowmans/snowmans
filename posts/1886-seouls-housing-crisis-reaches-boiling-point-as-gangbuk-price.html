Seoul's Housing Crisis Reaches Boiling Point as Gangbuk Prices Surge

November 03, 2026

South Korea's capital is facing an unprecedented housing crunch as Seoul's apartment prices jump 8.73% in the first ten months of the year, already outpacing the entirety of last year's gains while borrowing costs sit at a 3.00% benchmark interest rate.

  • The upside-down market: Unlike previous booms led by affluent southern districts like Gangnam, current record hikes are driven by northern working-class areas such as Seongbuk (up 16.56%) and Nowon.
  • The triple surge: Despite an 18.3% plunge in transaction volume due to strict lending caps and high interest, sales prices, jeonse (lump-sum deposits), and monthly rent are skyrocketing simultaneously across Seoul.
  • Political impact: President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating rebounded to 36.9%, fueled by public expectations for his administration's newly unveiled metropolitan housing supply plans.
  • The rental trap: Monthly rents in key districts like Gwangjin, Yeongdeungpo, and Songpa have surged over 20%, crushing tenants and young couples unable to purchase homes.

For ordinary Seoulites, the housing debate has moved far beyond elite real estate speculation. While the public watches high-profile symbols like Gangnam's Eunma apartments see slight price cooling, the lived reality for middle- and lower-income families is a brutal squeeze in outer districts where monthly rents and basic housing costs are running out of reach. President Lee's administration has won temporary political breathing room by promising aggressive supply expansions, but families living month-to-month feel little relief from blueprints that take years to build.

The core danger of the current market lies in its disconnection between falling transaction volumes and climbing prices. Homeowners are simply sitting on their properties rather than cutting prices, protected by a deep-seated belief that Seoul real estate will always bounce upward. Unless the government can translate its supply pledges into immediate, tangible relief for the rental market, housing in South Korea's capital will remain less a place to live and more an insurmountable barrier to financial survival.
