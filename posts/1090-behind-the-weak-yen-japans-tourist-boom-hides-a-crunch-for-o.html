Behind the Weak Yen: Japan’s Tourist Boom Hides a Crunch for Ordinary Workers

October 13, 2026

The Japanese yen has hit an 18-year low against the South Korean won, trading around the 840-won range per 100 yen—a dramatic drop from past years—fueling a massive wave of budget travel to Japan while masking deep structural pain in the Japanese economy.

  • The Cause: A wide interest rate gap between the U.S. (maintaining high rates above 4% to fight inflation) and Japan (clinging to ultra-low rates) has driven capital out of the yen and into the dollar.
  • The Tourist Surge: South Korean travel to Japan is booming, with over 10 million visitors expected this year—79% of whom are repeat travelers taking advantage of rock-bottom costs.
  • The Hidden Cost: While a weak yen pumps up corporate profits and stock prices by inflating overseas earnings when converted home, it has crushed workers' real wages.
  • The Social Toll: Surging everyday import and utility costs outpace meager wage bumps, leading to a rise in low-income poverty, heavy debt, and severe overtourism that strains local communities.

Corporate Profits vs. Working-Class Pain

To international travelers and investors, Japan’s weak-yen policy looks like a roaring success. Corporate balance sheets look healthy, and Tokyo's stock market has surged as multinational companies convert high-value foreign earnings back into cheap yen. Government defenders and corporate media frame this as proof of sound economic management, relying on overseas investments—the massive interest and dividend streams of a global "landlord nation"—to keep the broader ledger in the black despite a struggling trade deficit in basic goods and energy.

Yet this shiny macroeconomic picture hides a grim reality for ordinary households. As imported food, fuel, and daily necessities soar in price, wage increases are eaten away by higher taxes and social insurance contributions. Millions of low-income workers are slipping further behind, with food bank lines growing in major cities. Japan is essentially using the eroded purchasing power of its own citizens to subsidize corporate stock prices and service a staggering national debt that has surpassed 10 trillion won (over $7 trillion).

Overtourism and the Illusion of Prosperity

The human cost extends beyond rising poverty into the country's neighborhoods. Tokyo’s "tourism-first" survival strategy has triggered severe overtourism, pricing long-term residents out of historic districts like Asakusa and burying local communities under mountains of trash and noise. Much like the Spanish city of Barcelona, where locals have begun pushing back against runaway visitor numbers, Japanese residents are finding their daily lives disrupted by external capital.

For observers outside Japan—particularly in South Korea, where similar economic anxieties over inflation and widening inequality persist—the situation offers a sobering lesson. When national economic "success" is measured solely by stock market highs and corporate earnings while the purchasing power of ordinary workers collapses, growth becomes an illusion built on sand. A cheap bowl of ramen or an affordable weekend getaway may delight foreign tourists, but it rests on the quiet, grinding struggle of a society living through an engineered decline in living standards.
