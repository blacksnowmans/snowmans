South Korea's Push Into Physical AI: Robots, Regional Funding, and Real-World Stakes

October 28, 2026

South Korea is aggressively shifting artificial intelligence out of the software screen and into the physical world, backed by massive regional funding, corporate rollouts, and a growing push for safety guardrails.

    Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province launch a joint project: Backed by about $43 million (60 billion won) in national funding—part of a total $52 million (720 billion won) budget—the region aims to train 3,000 specialists in physical AI and robotics, linking education directly with corporate hiring through institutions like Yeungnam University College.
  • Major conglomerates scale up hardware: Samsung, led by Executive Chairman Lee Jae-yong, is prioritizing robotics and physical AI deployment, while HD Hyundai Robotics unveiled autonomous dual-arm robots and Doosan Robotics gained momentum via its Nvidia partnership.
  • Real-world integration expands: Physical AI applications are hitting everyday infrastructure, from smart hospital parking systems managed by Hiparking to specialized data platforms and international gripper technology developments.
  • Safety and economic filters tighten: Industry experts warn that hype is no longer enough; companies must prove actual productivity gains (P&L) while confronting critical hardware safety and control vulnerabilities.

Regional Strategies Meet Industrial Ambitions

South Korea's drive into physical AI—where software algorithms directly control hardware like robots, vehicles, and industrial machinery—is moving fast beyond theoretical research. Regional governments are utilizing state funds to combat brain drain by pairing localized education with immediate industrial pipelines. Beyond Daegu and North Gyeongsang, the Jeonbuk Provincial Office of Education and Gunsan City are building educational frameworks tied to the Saemangeum industrial complex, ensuring that the next generation trains on physical AI principles from an early age.

At the corporate level, heavyweights are racing to commercialize physical AI applications. Samsung's recent focus on robotics under Executive Chairman Lee Jae-yong signals a clear intent to dominate hardware-based execution. Meanwhile, partnerships with global tech giants like Nvidia and the introduction of advanced industrial machinery show that South Korea is leveraging its formidable manufacturing prowess to anchor itself in the global physical AI supply chain.

The Reality Check: P&L and the Need for Guardrails

Despite the excitement, the market is entering a strict period of discernment. Investors and enterprises are no longer swayed by flashy demonstrations alone; companies must deliver hard numbers, proving that their robotics and AI solutions cut costs and improve efficiency on actual factory floors and service sites. Those unable to demonstrate tangible bottom-line results face swift elimination.

More pressingly, moving AI into the physical world introduces severe safety risks. Security analysts have recently highlighted vulnerabilities where hardware could be manipulated into dangerous physical actions through bypassed prompt commands. Unlike a software hallucination, a compromised physical AI system can cause real-world injuries and structural damage. As South Korea races to build its physical AI ecosystem, establishing multi-layered safety guardrails and robust legal accountability will be just as critical as securing funding and building faster robots.
