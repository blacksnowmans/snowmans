South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Looming Reckoning as 100-Day Leadership Deadline Nears

November 03, 2026

An internal deadline set for November 24 has put South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) leader Kim Min-seok on notice, as party hardliners and mobilized grassroots members prepare to challenge his leadership over stagnating poll numbers.

  • The self-imposed test: After winning the DPK leadership election on August 17 with 54.08% of the vote, Kim promised to lift the ruling party's approval ratings by 10 percentage points within his first 100 days.
  • The polling deficit: Rather than rebounding, party ratings slipped from 38% before the convention to 35% in late October according to Gallup Korea, leaving Kim needing an implausible 21.7-point surge on the Realmeter tracking poll to hit his target.
  • The rival in waiting: Lawmaker Chung Chung-rae, who secured 45.92% in the leadership race, has explicitly framed November 24 as Kim’s final grace period, signaling that he and allied Supreme Council members are already drafting an alternative agenda.
  • The grassroots shift: Empowered dues-paying party members, energized following the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol and the inauguration of President Lee Jae-myung, are demanding direct influence rather than deferring to traditional party management.

A Math Problem Turned Factional Battlefield

Kim’s polling pledge was an aggressive political gamble from the start. In a post-election political environment without campaign tailwinds, clawing back double-digit ground within weeks is statistically almost impossible. By tying his legitimacy to a concrete metric, Kim handed his internal rivals an easy opening. For Chung Chung-rae and allied leadership figures like Supreme Council members Choi Min-hee, Han Min-su, and Lee Sung-yoon, the approaching 100-day mark serves as legitimate cover to mount an open critique of Kim's cautious stewardship.

Chung has made little secret of his preparations, openly stating he has already prepared his political slogans for the post-deadline phase. His faction argues that the current leadership has failed to deliver clear messaging or decisive action at a time when the governing bloc should be consolidating its momentum.

Grassroots Sovereignty vs. Party Establishment

Underneath the leadership rivalry lies a broader struggle over who steers the ruling camp. Commentators like liberal author Rhyu Si-min argue that the Democratic Party remains the only heavyweight vessel capable of steering state affairs, describing smaller progressive outfits like the Rebuilding Korea Party as useful icebreakers that cannot replace the flagship. Yet holding that coalition together requires managing an increasingly assertive party base that refuses to sit quietly.

With Yoon Suk-yeol incarcerated and President Lee Jae-myung leading the government, rank-and-file DPK members view themselves as active stakeholders rather than passive voters. If Kim cannot offer a convincing path forward when his 100 days expire on November 24, the party base is poised to back Chung’s faction, setting the stage for a sharp internal shakeup and a much more combative ruling party.
