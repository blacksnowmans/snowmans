Liberal Pundit Rhyu Si-min Sparks Feud Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party Over Falling Approval Ratings

October 29, 2026

Prominent progressive commentator and author Rhyu Si-min has triggered fierce infighting inside the ruling Democratic Party after warning that President Lee Jae-myung’s dropping poll numbers stem from a breakdown of trust with his core base and departures from his promised political direction.

  • What happened: Rhyu argued that presidential approval rests first on personal trust, second on ideological consistency, and only third on policy details—warning that the administration is alienating its political bedrock.
  • The backlash: Senior figures in the ruling Democratic Party, including Park Hong-keun, Park Jie-won, and Jeong Jin-wook, hit back aggressively, accusing Rhyu of undermining the administration and acting like a "shaman" rather than a supportive ally.
  • The root friction: President Lee recently warned against extreme partisan tribalism and engaged directly with criticisms raised on FM Korea, a prominent conservative online community, which angered hardline loyalists who expect unyielding partisan loyalty.
  • What it changes: The clash highlights growing anxiety on the Korean center-left that defending the administration by silencing internal critics risks repeating the fatal insularity that doomed the right-wing administration of Yoon Suk-yeol.

The Peril of Silencing Friendly Critics

The Democratic Party’s allergic reaction to Rhyu’s critique reveals how fragile the ruling camp's confidence has become amid softening poll numbers. Instead of engaging with his diagnosis, party heavyweights treated candid commentary as treachery. Turning a political party into an echo chamber that rewards only cheerleading deprives the leadership of early warning systems. When a government attempts to muzzle spectators rather than adjust its tactical play, it inevitably pushes away unaligned, moderate voters who decide general elections.

This dynamic also misdiagnoses why voters drift away. As Rhyu pointed out, the electorate rarely abandons an administration purely over the technicalities of a corporate regulation or a tax policy. They pull back when they sense the president's declared course has wavered or when the fundamental bond of trust is fractured. The rise of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party during recent cycles was already a shot across the bow—a clear sign that reform-minded voters were dissatisfied with Democratic complacency.

The Cautionary Tale of Tribal Warfare

President Lee’s instinct to transcend tribalism is defensible in principle; governance cannot be run purely for the base, especially when everyday households face acute economic strain despite aggressive regulatory enforcement against conglomerates. Yet Lee faces a sharp dilemma: reaching across the aisle to address concerns on conservative forums while Democratic hardliners demand total partisan discipline leaves the administration caught between an alienated base and an unconvinced center.

The collapse of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached and imprisoned after dismantling his own governing coalition and purging internal rivals like Lee Jun-seok—remains the ultimate warning in modern Korean politics. Power relies on a secure political home from which to expand outward. If the Democratic Party responds to falling poll numbers by attacking allies and demanding unquestioning obedience, it risks making the same fatal miscalculation: tearing down its own foundations in the name of loyalty.
