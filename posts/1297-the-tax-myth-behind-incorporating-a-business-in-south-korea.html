The Tax Myth Behind Incorporating a Business in South Korea

October 19, 2026

Converting a sole proprietorship into a corporation in South Korea does not magically slash your tax bill in half, and treating company funds like personal spending money can quickly trigger criminal charges.

  • The tax rate illusion: South Korea's top individual income tax rate reaches 49.5% (including local tax) compared to nominal corporate tax rates starting at 9.9%, but extracting profits through salaries or dividends triggers double taxation that eliminates expected savings.
  • Strict legal separation: South Korean law treats a corporation as a completely separate legal person from its owner; using corporate accounts for private investments or personal living expenses constitutes criminal embezzlement.
  • Automated tax surveillance: The National Tax Service (NTS), South Korea's tax agency, actively audits corporate credit card transactions made near an owner's personal residence or during late-night hours.
  • Governance deadlocks: Co-founders who split equity 50:50 frequently trap their business in total administrative paralysis if a dispute arises, leaving the company unable to perform basic legal duties.

Why Corporate Tax Savings Are an Illusion

Many small business owners in South Korea rush into corporate registration lured by the gap between individual tax rates—which top out at 49.5%—and corporate tax rates, which sit at 9.9% for taxable income under 200 million won (about $150,000) and 20.9% for amounts under 20 billion won (about $15 million). However, this ignores the structural reality of corporate taxation. The lower corporate tax rate is a policy incentive intended to encourage reinvestment and hiring. The moment a founder attempts to move that profit into their personal bank account via salary or dividends, personal income taxes and health insurance surcharges apply. Dividend income exceeding 20 million won annually is aggregated into global income tax, subjecting the owner to top-tier personal tax rates on top of the corporate tax already paid.

Recent tax investigations involving Korean celebrities highlight the severe legal risks of mismanaging single-person corporations. When actress Hwang Jung-eum drew scrutiny for transferring company funds into personal cryptocurrency investments, it exposed a common misconception among business owners who assume 100% ownership entitles them to spend company cash freely. Under South Korean law, diverting corporate capital without proper board resolutions or authorization specified in the corporate charter is classified as breach of duty and criminal embezzlement. Furthermore, the NTS uses automated surveillance to flag corporate card expenses incurred near a representative's home neighborhood or outside business hours.

Regulatory Compliance and Equity Traps

Incorporating also imposes strict procedural burdens under South Korea's Commercial Act. Even single-shareholder corporations must maintain clear Articles of Incorporation and formal meeting minutes. If executive salary caps or severance pay rules are missing from the corporate charter, the tax authorities will disallow executive compensation as a legitimate business expense, slapping the founder with heavy tax penalties.

Equally dangerous is the practice of co-founders splitting company shares 50:50 out of friendship. Under Korean corporate governance rules, major corporate decisions require a majority of voting rights. A 50:50 equity split leaves no legal remedy to break a tie when founders disagree, resulting in a "zombie company" that cannot renew office leases, register board appointments, or clear bank loans if one partner refuses to cooperate.
