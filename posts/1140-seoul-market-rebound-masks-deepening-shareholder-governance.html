Seoul Market Rebound Masks Deepening Shareholder Governance Clashes

October 14, 2026

South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI broke its five-day losing streak to reclaim the 6,100 level, but renewed shareholder backlash over subsidiary carve-outs shows that structural governance distrust still plagues the market.

  • The Index Bounces: The KOSPI rallied 1.6% to 6,139 points as easing U.S. Federal Reserve rate rhetoric and a stabilizing won (around 1,338 per dollar) provided a relief rally for beaten-down retail accounts.
  • Controversial Solidigm IPO: SK Hynix ignited fury by preparing a Nasdaq initial public offering for its revived U.S. NAND and SSD subsidiary Solidigm, reigniting debates over parent-company valuation dilution and the "Korea Discount."
  • Samsung Insider Signal: Samsung Electronics Chairman Lee Jae-yong deployed about $1.4 billion (1.9 trillion won) of personal funds to buy shares off-market, alongside an impending $1.9 billion-plus share gift from his mother, signaling an insider bottom call.
  • Supply Chain Realignment: Both Samsung and SK Hynix accelerated outsourcing for legacy chip packaging to domestic assembly and test partners, inching closer to Taiwan’s collaborative semiconductor ecosystem.

Chaebol Habits Die Hard: The Double-Listing Trap

While macro relief gave Seoul equities room to breathe, the backlash against SK Group illustrates why retail investors remain skeptical of index rallies. SK Hynix acquired Solidigm (formerly Intel’s NAND unit) for $9 billion in 2021. After years of deep losses absorbed by SK Hynix shareholders, the subsidiary is booming on enterprise SSD demand. Instead of letting parent shareholders reap the reward, SK is pursuing a separate Nasdaq listing. Watchdogs like the Korea Corporate Governance Forum view this as exporting bad chaebol habits abroad—diluting SK Hynix while routing upside to holding firm SK Inc., where Chairman Chey Tae-won holds direct equity.

This dynamic highlights why past capital market reform drives during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s term largely fell flat with the public: cosmetic value-up campaigns fail when controlling families can still carve out prime assets at will. Korea's double-counting ratio of listed corporate earnings stands at 15.5%—far above Japan's 11.1% and the U.S. level of 1.1%—proving that structural minority-investor protection remains 여전히 unresolved.

Industrial Shifts Offer a Healthier Path

More encouraging for long-term investors is the quiet operational shift underway inside Korea's chip giants. Strained by explosive demand for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), Samsung and SK Hynix are relinquishing their historic insistence on closed, vertical integration. Through initiatives like the SAFE OSAT Alliance, Samsung is transferring standard packaging and testing work to local specialists, including Hana Micron and SFA Semiconductor.

Emulating the collaborative model pioneered by Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC) gives Korean suppliers room to expand operating margins beyond the single digits. Coupled with Chairman Lee’s aggressive personal share purchases, the underlying industrial engine is adapting rapidly. However, until Seoul aligns board accountability with minority shareholder interests, technological excellence will continue to trade at a governance discount.
