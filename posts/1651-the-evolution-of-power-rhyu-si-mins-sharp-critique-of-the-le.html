The Evolution of Power: Rhyu Si-min’s Sharp Critique of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 29, 2026

Prominent political commentator Rhyu Si-min has sparked a fierce debate within South Korean political circles by arguing that President Lee Jae-myung is isolating himself from his own supporters, effectively dismantling the very coalition that brought him to power.

  • What happened: In a recent appearance on the political broadcast "Park Si-young TV," Rhyu criticized the current Lee Jae-myung administration across three core areas: personnel, political direction, and policy.
  • The core argument: Rhyu suggests that the administration is falling into a "seclusion trap," favoring internal loyalists over the broader public and alienated supporters.
  • What it means: The critique serves as a stark warning that without a shift toward broader public engagement and a correction of its current course, the administration risks a collapse in support similar to that of the previous, now-imprisoned, conservative leader, Yoon Suk-yeol.

The Chimpanzee Model of Governance

Rhyu draws a striking parallel between human politics and the behavior of chimpanzees. He notes that in primate societies, the "alpha male" does not maintain power through blind favoritism. Instead, successful leaders act as "sheriffs" who intervene in disputes by siding with the weaker party 70% to 80% of the time. When a leader abandons this impartiality in favor of exclusively rewarding a small circle of personal allies, the rest of the group invariably unites to overthrow them.

This is a warning against the "privatization" of power. Rhyu argues that for the Lee Jae-myung administration, current internal conflicts and dwindling approval ratings are not merely the result of media bias or external opposition. They are the natural consequence of a leadership that has become detached from the daily struggles of the people. When a government prioritizes internal loyalty tests over the tangible economic realities of its citizens—such as rising costs of living and housing—it loses its legitimacy. By ignoring these warnings, the administration risks turning the "support coalition" that once fueled its rise into a source of its own political demise.

Beyond Ideology: A Call for Reality

Rhyu’s critique goes beyond simple partisan advice; it is a call for a return to political pragmatism. He likens the current state of political discourse—where internal critics are labeled as "traitors" or "internal enemies"—to a dangerous echo chamber. Much like the catastrophic downfall of Yoon Suk-yeol, who alienated his own conservative base through stubbornness and isolation, the current liberal leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party) is being warned not to ignore its own cracks.

Ultimately, Rhyu suggests that the durability of any power relies on how well a leader manages the public interest, not how well they insulate themselves from dissent. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, the path forward requires a difficult, uncomfortable pivot: acknowledging that the loudest critics are often those most desperate for the administration to succeed. If the government continues to view internal reflection as an act of betrayal, history suggests the end will not be kind.
