Uncovering the Dark Secrets of South Korea's 1974 First Lady Assassination

October 21, 2026

Decades after South Korean First Lady Yuk Young-su was killed by an assassin's bullet on August 15, 1974, explosive new eyewitness testimony from inside the original investigative task force is dismantling the official state narrative.

  • The Inside Account: Former young investigator Choi Jong-sun has come forward with detailed evidence revealing massive cover-ups, botched identities, and severe information blackouts inside the powerful Korean Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA).
  • The Assassin's Props: The assassin, Moon Se-gwang, carried a Smith & Wesson revolver hidden inside a hollowed-out transistor radio—alongside an implausible fake Japanese passport under the alias "Yukio Yoshii" that immediately sent investigators chasing ghosts.
  • The KCIA's Internal War: While the domestic investigation team scrambled in the dark, the KCIA's overseas operations bureau already possessed exact profiles of Moon's Osaka home, proving the agency intentionally withheld critical facts from its own detectives.
  • The Mysterious Agent 'Y': An unidentified foreign operative code-named 'Y' casually strolled into KCIA headquarters during a national state of emergency on fake "vacation leave," heavily implying direct coordination or handling of the gunman.

A Botched Investigation Built on Deliberate Deception

When Moon Se-gwang stepped onto the stage on Liberation Day in 1974 and fired the shot that killed First Lady Yuk Young-su, the official story framed him as a lone, disgruntled pro-North Korean fanatic. But Choi's firsthand look inside the KCIA's basement interrogation rooms paints a far darker picture of institutional gaslighting. Moon was found bound in handcuffs on a military cot with a gag in his mouth to prevent suicide, dressed in forced military fatigues to strip away his civilian rights—a stark example of state violence designed to control the narrative rather than unearth the truth.

The investigation was plagued by systemic friction between domestic investigators and overseas espionage units. While top KCIA officials leaked the fake Japanese identity to the foreign press, major daily newspapers like the Chosun Ilbo somehow published intimate details of Moon's Osaka life hours before the intelligence agency officially updated its own files. This glaring discrepancy exposed how information was strictly filtered and weaponized by senior officials.

Demanding Accountability Under the Current Administration

The resurfacing of these suppressed files serves as a crucial reckoning for South Korea's intelligence history. Much like the abuses of power seen during the later administration of imprisoned former leader Yoon Seok-yeol, the KCIA's total information monopoly in 1974 shows how unchecked security agencies can manipulate national tragedies to protect regime interests.

Choi's extensive documentation and willingness to testify—despite personal tragedies, including his own brother's suspicious death under KCIA torture—provide a roadmap for a formal, state-led reinvestigation. As current progressive leadership continues pushing for judicial and institutional reforms, digging into the shadowy role of agent 'Y' and the KCIA's true level of prior knowledge is essential to ensuring state-sponsored cover-ups belong strictly to the past.
