South Korea's Academic Pressure Explains an Alarming Surge in Youth ADHD Prescriptions

September 13, 2026

  • Prescriptions for ADHD medications among South Korean youths more than doubled over five years, jumping from about 47,000 in 2020 to over 122,000 in 2024.
  • The drugs are increasingly being used as cognitive enhancers for test preparation, with about 23.5% of the total number of students taking the South Korean college entrance exam receiving prescriptions in 2024.
  • Brain science and behavioral economics suggest that human attention cannot be forced by willpower alone, but is heavily driven by environment and survival instincts.

South Korea’s hyper-competitive education system has created an unexpected pharmaceutical side effect. Recent health data shows that nearly a quarter of all test-takers for the country's grueling college entrance exam are now prescribed attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) medications. While these drugs are essential for diagnosed patients, their widespread use as a shortcut for studying exposes deep systemic flaws in how the country treats young minds.

The fixation on forced concentration ignores basic neuroscience. Human brains evolved to scan for threats rather than lock onto a single static task through sheer grit. Psychologists have long noted the "white bear paradox"—telling yourself not to think about something only makes you think about it more. When students are berated to focus under intense academic pressure, their brains often become more scattered, leading families to seek chemical interventions rather than examining the stressful environment itself.

Willpower Fails, but Environment Shapes Focus

Behavioral research demonstrates that trying to command attention through strict willpower is largely ineffective. Experiments in psychology show that forcing calm or suppressing distractions often backfires, creating mental friction. Instead of relying on brute mental force, lasting focus is a byproduct of proper conditions and environmental design.

This principle of "nudge theory"—shaping behavior by altering surroundings rather than demanding compliance—applies just as much to studying as it does to everyday habits. South Korea's soaring private education costs and skyrocketing ADHD prescription rates, particularly in high-pressure administrative hubs like Sejong City, suggest that the relentless pursuit of test scores is literally sickening students. Rather than medicating children to fit into a broken system, society needs to redesign the daily environments that trap them in cycles of chronic distraction and anxiety.
