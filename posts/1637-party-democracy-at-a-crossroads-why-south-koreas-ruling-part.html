Party Democracy at a Crossroads: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Must Listen to Its Base

October 28, 2026

As President Lee Jae-myung’s administration faces stagnating approval ratings in the 30% range, political analysts are warning the ruling Democratic Party of Korea against repeating the top-down party management mistakes that led to the collapse of the conservative opposition.

  • What happened: The opposition People Power Party ruined its internal governance during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency by repeatedly purging elected leaders, culminating in his impeachment, removal from office, and imprisonment.
  • The current challenge: President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings have stalled in the 30% range, driving concerns over an echo-chamber atmosphere in the presidential office that resists critical feedback.
  • What it changes: Lingering dissatisfaction threatens ruling-party unity ahead of upcoming general elections, raising the risk of internal factional strife if public trust is not restored.
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), its party members, and progressive allies such as the Rebuilding Korea Party, which serves as a tactical ally but cannot replace the DPK as the primary governing platform.

The Perils of Suppressing Member Sovereignty

The recent history of the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), is an instructive cautionary tale. Under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency, executive power systematically dismantled party democracy by overriding member votes to purge unwanted party leaders. That disregard for internal member sovereignty destabilized the conservative camp, accelerating the constitutional crisis that ended in Mr. Yoon’s impeachment and imprisonment. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, protecting internal democratic mechanisms—even when minority factions mount strong challenges—remains essential to preserving institutional resilience.

Escaping the Echo Chamber Ahead of Elections

Top leaders rarely fail from isolation; they fail when executive power is insulated from hard realities. Critics note that the presidential office under President Lee risks fostering a culture where loyalty outweighs candid appraisal, ignoring warnings embedded in public opinion polling. Historically, when presidential approval drops into the 30% corridor ahead of general elections, ruling-party lawmakers inevitably face pressure to distance themselves from the administration or demand emergency leadership changes.

To avoid repeating the factional ruptures seen during past administrations, President Lee and DPK leadership must demonstrate inclusive management. Minor allies like the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party can act as tactical icebreakers, but long-term governance depends entirely on maintaining the DPK as a broad, stable platform. Rebounding in public standing requires heeding harsh data, inviting constructive critique, and treating party member sovereignty as an operational compass rather than a symbolic slogan.
