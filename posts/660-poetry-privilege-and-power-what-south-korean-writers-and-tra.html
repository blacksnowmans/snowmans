Poetry, Privilege, and Power: What South Korean Writers and Traffic Experiments Tell Us About Human Nature

October 04, 2026

A viral video discussion pairing the work of mid-century South Korean poet Kim Soo-young with modern sociological concepts offers a sharp look at how power, wealth, and everyday anxiety shape human morality.

  • The Ordinary Push for Change: Examining Kim Soo-young's poetry, commentators argue that monumental social shifts—such as South Korea's historic April 19 Revolution—are driven not by grand heroes, but by ordinary, anxious people pushed to their limits.
  • The Luxury Car Effect: Citing social experiments from the book Humankind, discussions highlight how drivers of expensive vehicles, particularly BMWs, are far more likely to violate traffic laws and ignore pedestrians than drivers of older cars.
  • The Trap of Authority: The commentary explores "social sociopathy"—the tendency for people given even minor authority or immense political power to turn into self-serving controllers.

The Paradox of Petty Worries and Historical Change

It is easy to feel small when faced with daily financial pressures. Whether worrying over a minor parking fine or stressing about grocery receipts, the grind of everyday life often makes people feel insignificant. Yet, South Korean literary tradition has long found truth in this pettiness. Kim Soo-young's poetry—particularly works like Exiting the Palace One Day—satirizes the intellectual who remains silent during major historical injustices while venting misplaced anger at ancient royal palaces. The poet's willingness to expose his own hypocrisy and small-mindedness is viewed as a form of genuine courage.

This perspective reframes how we view mass movements. The citizens who filled the streets during the April 19 Revolution were not infallible warriors; they were ordinary shopkeepers, workers, and family members terrified of the risks they were taking. Yet, their collective, honest daily anger is what ultimately overturned an unjust status quo. The commentary suggests that society is saved not by perfect heroes, but by the accumulated conscience of ordinary people trying to hold the line in their everyday lives.

From Luxury Cars to Political Power: The Making of "Social Sociopaths"

That fragile morality, however, easily dissolves under the influence of wealth and status. Reference to a U.S. experiment from Humankind reveals a troubling correlation: drivers of modest cars generally obeyed traffic laws and yielded to pedestrians, while those driving luxury cars consistently behaved more selfishly. In repeated tests, BMW drivers topped the list of traffic violators. When people acquire wealth or status, their empathy often plummets as they fall into the trap of believing their success is entirely self-made.

This dynamic scales up into the political sphere, manifesting as "social sociopaths"—individuals who appear kind and reasonable when subordinate, but morph into controlling figures the moment they grasp a shred of authority. The commentary contrasts this corrupting nature of power with the legacy of former South Korean President Moon Jae-in, praising his administration's exhausting adherence to democratic procedures and refusal to weaponize privilege despite intense pressure. Ultimately, the essay contends that maintaining humility and empathy in the face of power remains one of the rarest and most vital achievements in modern politics.
