Why South Korea's Stock Market Stagnates Despite Record Corporate Earnings

October 27, 2026

The persistent "Korea Discount" keeping the benchmark KOSPI index languishing near the 2,500 mark is not a failure of corporate earnings, but a structural governance crisis where controlling families treat corporate treasuries as private defense funds rather than sharing profits with investors.

  • Structural discount, not just cheap stocks: Despite global dominance in semiconductors, automotive, and batteries, Korean equities are penalized by international and domestic investors who assume minority shareholders will never see their fair share of corporate profits.
  • The buyback gap: While global giants like Apple spent over $600 billion (around 800 trillion won) over the past decade to retire shares and dramatically boost shareholder value, Korean conglomerates (chaebols) routinely hoard repurchased stock to defend family control.
  • Market punishment in real time: Memory chip maker SK Hynix saw its shares plunge within an hour of posting strong earnings simply because management announced no concrete share cancellation or capital return roadmap.
  • Push for legislative fixes: Analysts and reform-minded economists are demanding mandatory share cancellation rules and taxes on excessive corporate cash reserves, backing governance bills championed by the ruling Democratic Party under President Lee Jae-myung.

Treasury Shares as Conglomerate Shields

In mature financial markets, share repurchases exist primarily to reduce total share count and raise earnings per share (EPS). In South Korea, chaebols have turned this mechanism on its head. Companies buy back their own stock with corporate money, but instead of canceling it, they hold the shares on the corporate balance sheet. When a controlling dynasty faces a governance challenge or inheritance hurdle, it executes friendly share-swaps with allied companies. Because treasury stock loses voting rights until transferred to a third party, these backroom swaps magically resurrect those voting rights—locking in dynastic control without the family spending a single cent of their own capital.

Under the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, corporate practices of this kind were largely tolerated under the banner of deregulation and "business-friendly" policies. The predictable result was an entrenched day-trading culture among domestic retail investors, known locally as "ants." Unable to trust that corporate profits will translate into sustained long-term capital appreciation, local investors treat the domestic bourse as a short-term trading post rather than a vehicle for building generational wealth.

The Political Push for Structural Repair

Breaking this trap requires legal mandates rather than voluntary corporate governance guidelines. Commentators are increasingly advocating for public "stock hearings" to expose how boards dilute shareholder value, alongside Tokyo-style stock exchange pressure that penalizes companies trading below book value. More critically, legislative solutions are now centered on legally requiring the mandatory cancellation of repurchased shares and imposing penal taxes on dormant corporate cash hoards.

While the conservative opposition People Power Party continues to resist these measures as burdensome corporate regulation, the Lee Jae-myung administration argues that transparent governance is the only viable path to lifting the KOSPI toward the 3,000 threshold. Until Korean law protects ordinary shareholders from board-level expropriation, foreign capital will continue to treat South Korea's equity market with deep skepticism.
