South Korea’s Regional Governments Launch Aggressive Childcare Experiments as Demand Surges

November 28, 2026

Local governments across South Korea are rolling out radical childcare initiatives—including "grandparent stipends" and 24/7 care centers—to address a sharp rise in demand for state-backed babysitting services.

  • Usage is spiking: North Gyeongsang Province reported an 11.6% year-on-year increase in childcare service usage, with over 18,200 children now enrolled in the state-run program.
  • Grandparent Stipends: Hampyeong County in South Jeolla Province has introduced a monthly allowance starting at 300,000 KRW (about $215) for grandparents who care for their grandchildren, formally recognizing domestic labor as a public service.
  • 24/7 Coverage: Gimje City is piloting a 365-day, 24-hour childcare center to support parents with irregular work hours, night shifts, or sudden family emergencies.
  • Quality Benchmarks: The Ministry of Gender Equality and Family—the government agency overseeing family welfare—recently awarded "A" grades to several municipalities, including Yangju and the rural county of Yeongyang, for excellence in caregiver management.

The Shift from Private to Public Care

The surge in demand, particularly in provinces like North Gyeongsang, underscores a fundamental shift in South Korean society. Childcare is no longer viewed as a private family obligation but as a critical infrastructure requirement for economic survival. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, which has prioritized expanded social safety nets, these regional data points suggest that the traditional model of "home-only" parenting has reached its breaking point. As dual-income households become the mandatory standard rather than a choice, the pressure on the national Childcare Service (a state-run program that sends certified sitters to homes) is reaching a fever pitch.

However, this growth is a double-edged sword. While the statistics show the service is becoming more accessible, they also warn of a looming "care deficit." If the recruitment of professional caregivers does not keep pace with the double-digit growth in demand, waitlists will continue to grow, leaving many "care refugees" with no place to turn. The 11.6% spike in North Gyeongsang is less a cause for celebration and more a call for the government to aggressively increase the caregiver workforce and improve their working conditions to ensure service quality doesn't dilute with scale.

Radical Incentives: Grandparents and 24/7 Safety Nets

The most innovative developments are currently happening in smaller municipalities that are fighting to keep young families from migrating to Seoul. By paying grandparents a stipend, Hampyeong is effectively socializing domestic labor—a progressive step that acknowledges the reality that many Korean families rely on the "sacrifice" of elders to stay afloat. Similarly, Gimje’s 24-hour center addresses the reality of the modern economy where work doesn't stop at 6:00 PM. These are not merely "pro-natalist" perks to boost the birth rate; they are essential survival tools for parents currently struggling under the weight of what Koreans call dokbak yuk-a, or "solo parenting" without a support system.

Ultimately, the performance evaluations released this week show that geography dictates the quality of care. Families in "A-grade" cities like Yangju, which employs 220 certified caregivers, enjoy a level of reliability that is not yet universal. If the Lee Jae-myung government intends to fulfill its mandate of universal welfare, it must find a way to standardize these localized "experiments"—like the grandparent stipend—and turn them into national rights. The goal is a system where the safety of a child and the career of a parent do not depend on which specific county they happen to live in.
