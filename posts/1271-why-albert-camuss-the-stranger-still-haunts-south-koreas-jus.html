Why Albert Camus’s ‘The Stranger’ Still Haunts South Korea’s Justice System

October 18, 2026

Albert Camus’s 1942 masterpiece The Stranger exposes a reality that continues to resonate in contemporary South Korea: courts and society often punish individuals less for their actual deeds than for their refusal to mimic officially sanctioned emotions.

  • The demand for performance: Just as Meursault is condemned to the guillotine not primarily for homicide, but for failing to weep at his mother’s funeral, modern public opinion demands strict adherence to emotional scripts.
  • The digital pillory: In South Korea, social media algorithms and short-form video clips fuel intense online witch-hunts, dissecting micro-expressions and body language to label non-conformists as monsters.
  • An exclusionary legal cartel: The trial in The Stranger—where judges, prosecutors, and attorneys form an insular fraternity that treats the accused as a mere prop—mirrors long-standing public grievances over South Korea’s judicial establishment.
  • The urgency of reform: The novel’s indictment of selective morality and institutional hypocrisy underscores why South Koreans have repeatedly taken to the streets to demand sweeping prosecutorial and judicial overhauls.

From the Absurd to the Algorithm: Modern Witch-Hunts

Camus’s central insight is that society cannot tolerate anyone who refuses to play its hypocritical games. In contemporary South Korea, where hyper-connected social platforms amplify collective pressure, this dynamic plays out daily. When controversy strikes, viral clips on YouTube and Instagram dissect a suspect’s gait or facial expressions, concluding that they were "always evil." Investigators and the public demand that suspects project performative remorse, while victims are expected to display total helplessness. A calculated show of tears can mitigate a penalty, whereas authentic, quiet detachment is condemned as unrepentant sociopathy.

South Korea’s Legal Monopoly and the Need for Reform

The second half of The Stranger operates as a biting courtroom satire, depicting a closed legal guild that effectively strips the defendant of his voice. Defense counsel and prosecutors converse in an arcane institutional dialect, leaving the accused a bewildered spectator at his own sentencing. This dynamic strikes a nerve in South Korea, where the justice system has faced persistent criticism for operating as an elite club. Public frustration has long centered on jeon-gwan ye-u—the practice of "customary treatment for former officials," where ex-prosecutors and judges enter lucrative private practices to trade on internal connections.

This institutional insularity deepened public cynicism during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's administration, when prosecutorial networks wielded immense political influence through selective investigations and indictment monopolies. The resulting popular push for systemic judicial reform reflects the very dilemma Camus raised eight decades ago: when the law becomes the exclusive theater of an elite cartel, it ceases to serve justice and instead demands total subjugation to power. Dismantling that cartel remains an urgent democratic test.
