South Korea's Political Turbulence: Chief of Staff Resigns as Seoul Faces Tough U.S. Trade Pressure

September 24, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik has abruptly offered to resign after 15 months on the job, taking the blame for recent appointment failures and sliding approval ratings. Meanwhile, the presidential office is navigating split polling numbers and aggressive U.S. trade demands during a presidential overseas tour.
  • What it changes: The departure of a key aide leaves the administration shorthanded just as it faces a massive cabinet shake-up following the president's return. It also heightens tension around high-stakes economic negotiations with Washington.
  • Who is affected: The ruling administration, progressive and moderate voters disillusioned by recent missteps, and South Korean industrial sectors facing heavy-handed U.S. investment demands.

Approval ratings hold steady, but core support slips

Recent polling data following a presidential press conference paint a mixed picture. Phone-interview surveys put positive job evaluations ahead at 50.5%, beating negative marks, while automated ARS (audio response system) polls showed the opposite. While the administration narrowly avoided a disaster—performing better than its previous plunging numbers—a closer look reveals persistent fatigue. Polling experts note that progressives and moderates have drifted away, and a recent social media misstep by the president further dampened enthusiasm among key liberal supporters.

Compounding these domestic headaches is the sudden exit of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik. Often seen as an energetic policy driver rather than a passive aide, Kang’s resignation over botched cabinet nominations leaves a gaping hole in the Blue House. The president has yet to formally accept or reject the resignation, signaling a holding pattern until returning from a trip to the U.S. and Mexico, where a sweeping personnel overhaul is widely expected.

Navigating tough U.S. trade demands and diplomatic double-talk

Abroad, the stakes are equally high. The president's overseas tour brings potential face-to-face talks with U.S. President Donald Trump, raising anxiety in Seoul over aggressive economic pressure. Washington is reportedly pushing South Korea far beyond previous investment caps—such as demanding over $22.3 billion for a Texas gas-fired power plant project and offloading stakes in troubled firms like Westinghouse—while brushing aside commercial logic.

Former U.S. Special Representative Stephen Biegun recently visited Seoul to reassure the public, insisting that economic and security issues are separate tracks. But given Washington's track record of linking security commitments to economic concessions, few in South Korea are buying the separation. As the administration faces mounting pressure on both fronts, it will take sharp, pragmatic diplomacy—not soothing rhetoric—to protect national interests.
