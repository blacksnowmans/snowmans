South Korea's Political Turbulence: Sinking Approval, Legal Battles, and Public Frustration

October 12, 2026

South Korea's progressive government is facing a sharp political reality check as President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings drop for the second consecutive week, driven by persistent inflation, housing costs, and internal party friction.

  • Failing Approval: President Lee's negative ratings have climbed to 56%, while the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main center-left party, saw its support slide to 35.5%. Meanwhile, the conservative main opposition People Power Party has made modest gains in recent polls.
  • Good News Drowned Out: Major national achievements, such as the successful fifth launch of the Nuri space rocket, were entirely overshadowed by public fatigue over rising Seoul housing prices—which have climbed for 87 consecutive weeks—and factional disputes over key appointments.
  • Rising Alternatives: The minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party crossed the 8% support threshold for the first time, signaling a growing voter appetite for sharper, more aggressive reform within the liberal bloc.
  • Judicial Backlash: Over 50% of respondents expressed support for impeaching the Chief Justice and opposed the potential release of former conservative leader Yoon Suk Yeol, highlighting a deep disconnect between the public and South Korea's judiciary.

Economic Fatigue Overshadows State Successes

For the administration, the current poll numbers are a severe warning shot. Despite high-profile national wins like the Nuri rocket launch, ordinary citizens remain focused on their bank accounts and soaring property costs. Housing and security issues have become the primary drivers of negative public sentiment, eclipsing the government's reform agenda.

The friction surrounding candidate selections for a newly proposed anti-corruption investigative agency has added fuel to the fire, making the government look disorganized rather than decisive. If the administration wants to reverse its downward trajectory, it must pivot away from internal squabbling and deliver tangible relief to Seoul's exhausted urban voters.

A Crisis of Judicial and Political Accountability

Public anger is not limited to economic policy; it extends deeply into the justice system. More than half of surveyed citizens believe that the Chief Justice should be impeached, a remarkably rare indicator of widespread distrust in South Korea's highest court. This frustration is compounded by public anxiety surrounding former leader Yoon Suk Yeol—currently imprisoned following his impeachment—with polls showing a contentious debate over legal outcomes and the looming expiration of detention orders for figures tied to past corruption scandals.

These figures underscore a dangerous erosion of trust in the nation's democratic guardrails. When the public perceives that the legal system shields the powerful while squeezing ordinary workers, the resulting cynicism threatens the core of South Korean democracy. The ruling party and judicial authorities ignore these stark warnings at their own peril.
