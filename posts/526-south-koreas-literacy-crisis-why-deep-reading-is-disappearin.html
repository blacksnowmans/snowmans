South Korea’s Literacy Crisis: Why Deep Reading is Disappearing

September 10, 2026

  • South Korea's reading rates are plummeting, with official government data showing over 60% of adults fail to read a single book in a year.
  • Surveys of schoolteachers reveal that more than 91% believe student literacy has sharply declined compared to previous generations.
  • The rise of short-form video content and instant digital stimuli are blamed for eroding deep concentration and analytical thinking skills.
  • Experts argue that without heavy "input" through rigorous reading, human expression and comprehension naturally atrophy.

South Korea's obsession with hyper-fast digital connectivity has created a hidden casualty: the ability to read and comprehend complex text. As smartphone algorithms pump endless streams of short-form video into the pockets of everyday users, the national appetite for books is shrinking to historic lows. Official figures show that the average South Korean adult reads just 3.9 books annually, a stark reflection of a society increasingly alienated from long-form thought.

This crisis extends directly into classrooms, where teachers report alarming misunderstandings of basic vocabulary and context among students. While generative artificial intelligence tools can instantly generate essays and reports, experts warn that human brains cannot shortcut the learning process. Just as AI requires massive datasets to function, human cognitive power relies entirely on the heavy lifting of deep reading. Without it, society risks losing the capacity to grasp nuance, context, and cause-and-effect reasoning.

How to Rebuild Your Brain: Practical Rules for Deep Reading

Overcoming this modern literacy slump requires abandoning perfectionism and changing how we approach text. Readers often quit heavy books because they try to digest every single sentence on the first pass. Instead, experts recommend focusing on the overarching narrative arc or core themes, using chapter summaries to anchor comprehension. Just as a heavy vehicle digs deeper into the earth with repeated passes, reading a challenging book multiple times unlocks layers of meaning that a single glance will always miss.

Different formats also demand entirely different tactics. Novels require understanding the author's historical and personal background to unlock their emotional weight, while essays and nonfiction are best approached through selective reading—pulling out specific insights rather than forcing a cover-to-cover read. By treating books not as static objects of information, but as living organisms that change shape depending on the reader's own life experiences, individuals can slowly reclaim their attention spans from the grip of the digital screen.
