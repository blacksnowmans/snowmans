Opposition Lawmaker Slams President Lee Over Residence Privilege and Scrapped Petitions

October 30, 2026

Cheon Ha-ram, a lawmaker from South Korea’s minor opposition Reform Party, has launched a sharp attack on President Lee Jae-myung’s administration during parliamentary audits, accusing the president’s family of enjoying unearned privilege and exposing tens of thousands of ignored citizen petitions.

  • Presidential residence controversy: Cheon revealed that President Lee’s adult second son is residing rent-free at the official presidential residence in Seoul's Hannam-dong district, labeling the state-funded housing arrangement an unfair "Dad Chance."
  • Scrapped citizen petitions: The lawmaker exposed that the administration discarded 38,000 public petitions submitted to its official feedback channel without answering them or transferring them to central government databases as promised.
  • Opposition strategy resolved: The Reform Party leadership agreed on October 29 to back Cheon’s bid to run in Suncheon—a traditional stronghold of the ruling Democratic Party—ending internal friction over party strategy for the 2028 general elections.

Privilege and Communication Breakdown

Cheon’s revelations target the administration’s core claims of fairness and public responsiveness. For young Koreans grappling with high living expenses and a tough housing market, the image of an adult presidential family member staying in high-security, state-funded housing stokes deep resentment over elite privilege. Framing the arrangement as a "Dad Chance"—a phrase heavily invoked during the administration of Yoon Suk-yeol to denounce political favoritism—hits the ruling Democratic Party where it hurts most. Although Democratic Party Lawmaker Lim Gwang-hyun dismissed the phrase as an exaggeration, the optics remain difficult to defend.

The controversy is compounded by the revelation that 38,000 public petitions submitted directly to the president's administrative suggestion box were quietly dumped. Rather than migrating the requests to central government portals as promised, the administration deleted them without feedback. For a government that pledged transparent communication, scrapping tens of thousands of citizen appeals risks turning its feedback platforms into little more than political theater.

A Calculated Third-Party Gamble

Meanwhile, Cheon’s successful push to run in Suncheon demonstrates his rising clout within the Reform Party. Party leadership had originally urged him to contest a more strategic district in southern Gyeonggi Province. By insisting on running in the southwestern Honam region—a hostile battleground dominated by the ruling Democratic Party—Cheon is betting that a principled fight against regional monopolies will resonate with voters fatigued by two-party dominance. If Cheon can turn public dissatisfaction over presidential missteps into local political momentum, he stands to cement his role as a key leader in South Korea's third-party movement.
