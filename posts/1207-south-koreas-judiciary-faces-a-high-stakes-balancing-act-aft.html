South Korea's Judiciary Faces a High-Stakes Balancing Act After Yoon Suk-yeol's Ouster

October 16, 2026

Following the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol on insurrection charges, South Korea’s judiciary is navigating a delicate political landscape that could decide the fate of both the ousted former leader and current President Lee Jae-myung.

  • Extreme Sentencing Speculation: Legal observers suggest Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s Supreme Court could issue maximum penalties, such as life imprisonment or the death penalty, against Yoon Suk-yeol over his unconstitutional martial law declaration.
  • Judicial Leverage: Delivering a decisive verdict against Yoon would boost public trust in the courts, giving judges the standing to resume pending criminal trials against President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Public Fatigue: Unlike the massive candlelight rallies that ousted former President Park Geun-hye in 2017, South Korean citizens show growing reluctance to take to the streets to shield current political leaders from legal scrutiny.
  • Executive Power Concerns: Critics across the political spectrum are raising alarms over President Lee’s efforts to consolidate control over newly established law enforcement and investigative agencies.

The Judiciary's Calculated Gamble on Judicial Independence

Assuming the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae will protect Yoon Suk-yeol misjudges how South Korea's judiciary operates. Historically, the courts are sensitive to shifting political power and prioritize institutional survival above all else. By handing down an uncompromising sentence against Yoon for his brief declaration of martial law, the high court could burnish its reputation as an impartial defender of constitutional order.

However, this gambit carries profound implications for President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Once the judiciary establishes its authority by punishing the ousted leader, it gains significant leverage to reopen ongoing criminal proceedings against President Lee. Should the courts assert that justice applies equally across party lines, the public may prove far less willing to mobilize in defense of the current administration.

Public Fatigue and the Push Toward a New Constitutional Order

The political atmosphere in Seoul today differs sharply from previous protest movements. While minor progressive groups, including the opposition Cho Kuk Innovation Party, call for public mobilization, voters are exhibiting deep cynicism toward using street demonstrations as political shields. Citizens are increasingly weary of partisan warfare and hesitant to back executive overreach, particularly regarding presidential influence over planned law enforcement bodies like the Serious Crime Investigation Agency.

In effect, South Korea may be heading toward a judicial cleanup—a scenario where court rulings resolve the deadlock between the past and present administrations. As political circles debate a transition toward a "Seventh Republic" under a revised constitutional framework, ultimate authority during this transition may rest not in street demonstrations or executive decrees, but in the decisions handed down from the bench.
