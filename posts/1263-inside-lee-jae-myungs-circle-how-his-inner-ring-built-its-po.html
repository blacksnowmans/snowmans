Inside Lee Jae-myung’s Circle: How His Inner Ring Built Its Policy Blueprint

October 18, 2026

During his path to power, President Lee Jae-myung’s campaign sought out economic scholar Choi Bae-geun not simply as an adviser, but as a grand architect of state policy likened to Jeong Do-jeon, the mastermind behind the founding of the Joseon Dynasty.

  • A year-long vetting process: Rather than chasing quick political endorsements, Lee’s inner sanctum spent a full year in policy dialogue with Choi to align on core reforms.
  • Distance from hardline factions: Despite conservative accusations linking Lee to the Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance—a local radical-left grassroots network—campaign insiders maintain Lee kept strict boundaries to preserve a pragmatic, non-dogmatic platform.
  • Refusing official spoils: Offers from top aides to create custom government posts or grant senior civil service roles to associates were turned down to protect ethical credibility.

The Inner Sanctum: Jeong Jin-sang and Lee Han-joo

Key to Lee's early policy architecture was Jeong Jin-sang, then chief of staff and widely regarded as Lee’s closest gatekeeper—a figure comparable to Yang Jeong-cheol under Moon Jae-in. Rather than functioning as a transactional political broker, Jeong focused intensely on whether scholars could translate structural ideas, such as educational reform and reducing inequality, into Lee’s pragmatic governance model. This was followed by outreach from Lee Han-joo, the campaign's lead policy brain, who even offered to reshape administrative organs to create a suitable position if Choi agreed to join the administration's blueprint effort.

Policy Over Patronage

The campaign sought more than ordinary campaign trail advice: they requested a total overhaul plan, mirroring the foundational role of historic architect Jeong Do-jeon. Yet when offered Gyeonggi provincial project backing and a Grade 3 civil service appointment (roughly director-general level) for a senior researcher to assist the work, the offers were declined. The rationale was simple: accepting bureaucratic perks or placing proteges into public office invites allegations of patronage, creating an ethical liability for the leadership.

In South Korean campaign politics, where academics often trade partisan loyalty for ministerial appointments, this deliberate refusal to trade policy for power reveals how the core governing blueprint of the current Democratic Party of Korea administration took shape: through ideological independence and structural ambition rather than political spoils.
