How a 21-Year-Old College Student Cracked South Korea's Brutal Property Market

September 08, 2026

  • A 21-year-old college student in South Korea successfully bought an apartment through a court property auction for under $70,000 (95.9 million won) after just three months of study.
  • The case highlights how South Korea's younger generation is bypassing the country's skyrocketing housing prices—where wage growth alone can no longer secure a home—by mastering alternative methods like court auctions.
  • Experts note the case proves that execution, site visits, and practical risk analysis matter far more than waiting for a large nest egg or complete theoretical knowledge.

For South Korea's younger generation, the dream of buying a home feels increasingly out of reach. With stagnant salaries and high deposit requirements for standard rentals, a sense of hopelessness has settled over many young adults. But one 21-year-old student, Shin Ji-ye, decided to bypass traditional routes entirely, diving headfirst into South Korea's court-managed property auction system to secure her first apartment.

Shin's success story offers a sharp look at how capitalism actually rewards those willing to master its mechanics. Starting with zero real estate background, she spent three months studying under a mentor alongside her father before tackling a real court auction for a small apartment in Siheung, Gyeonggi Province. The property—valued at around $100,000 (95.9 million won) as a starting bid—came with potential complications, including a tricky title involving the owner's mother listed as a tenant. By carefully analyzing the registry and confirming a top-tier bank's mortgage took precedence, Shin cleared the legal hurdles and placed a winning bid.

Action Beats Endless Hesitation in South Korea's Housing Market

The real takeaway from Shin's auction win isn't just about scoring a bargain; it’s a critique of how adults often paralyze themselves with excuses. While many prospective buyers sit on the sidelines complaining about high entry barriers, Shin visited local real estate offices, checked management fees with apartment administrators, and walked the neighborhood to assess future infrastructure projects like nearby housing developments.

Court auctions in South Korea are transparent, legal processes governed by the courts, which eliminates much of the fraud risk found in private transactions. Yet, beginners stumble over minor details. Shin's preparation extended to practical courtroom logistics: placing an "X" mark in unused spaces on her bidding form to prevent a catastrophic decimal or zero-addition error, and bringing a cashier's check to avoid wasting time with cash counting.

Ultimately, this case underscores a harsh truth about modern wealth in South Korea. Sitting at home and complaining about unaffordable housing changes nothing. For those willing to master legal titles, perform rigorous on-the-ground research, and control their emotions in the auction room, alternative markets like court auctions remain a viable escape hatch from generational poverty.
