South Korea's Ruling Party Fights Falling Approval Ratings Amid Scathing Rival Clashes

October 12, 2026

  • What happened: The ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party led by Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok, hit a record-low approval rating of around 35% under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, sparking an aggressive push into grassroots local organizing.
  • What it changes: Stalled policy momentum and intra-party friction are forcing the leadership to scramble for tangible results, with delivery app fee caps and livelihood bills moving ahead alongside stormy parliamentary audits.
  • Who is affected: Everyday citizens and small business owners dealing with inflation and high platform fees, as well as lawmakers caught up in toxic tit-for-tat family privilege investigations.

South Korean politics is stuck in the mud. As the annual parliamentary audit season enters its second week, the ruling Democratic Party finds itself caught between plunging public approval and a relentless cycle of petty political warfare. Rather than debating policy, both sides of the aisle are busy dragging each other's families into the spotlight, trading accusations over alleged essay-ghostwriting involving former conservative opposition leader Han Dong-hoon’s daughter, and sparring over past political scandals.

This mudslinging is drowning out what voters actually care about. While politicians throw punches over foreign policy—such as a delayed U.S. visa for ruling party lawmaker Jung Chung-rae—crucial institutional reforms and economic pressures are getting sidelined. The opposition People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, is seizing on the government's vulnerabilities, slamming the administration over judicial reform moves and efforts targeting the judiciary and prosecution.

Yet, amidst the political noise, there are glimmers of legislative survival. Talks on capping delivery app commissions are gaining traction to throw a lifeline to struggling small business owners, alongside tax relief bills for ordinary workers. If the ruling party wants to climb out of its 35% approval hole, thumb-sucking and finger-pointing won't cut it. True political survival requires delivering real economic relief rather than empty theater.
