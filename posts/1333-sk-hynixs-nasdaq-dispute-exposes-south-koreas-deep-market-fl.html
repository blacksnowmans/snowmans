SK Hynix’s Nasdaq Dispute Exposes South Korea’s Deep Market Flaws

October 19, 2026

SK Hynix’s stock slid despite an AI memory boom, laid low by forced margin liquidations and outrage over plans to list its lucrative US subsidiary, Solidigm, on the Nasdaq.

  • What happened: SK Hynix, South Korea's premier AI memory maker, lagged the benchmark Kospi index following reports that its US NAND flash unit Solidigm is preparing an independent Nasdaq debut valued at up to $150 billion (200 trillion won).
  • What it changes: Retail investors are mounting a fierce backlash against the practice of "carve-out listings," which strips parent companies of prime growth engines and forces a severe holding company discount on domestic shareholders.
  • Who is affected: Retail traders face compounding losses as margin trading debt surged by nearly $1.5 billion (2 trillion won) in just three days, triggering automated forced liquidations in SK Hynix and rival Samsung Electronics.

Governance Woes and the "Carve-Out" Curse

SK Hynix nurtured Solidigm through years of heavy losses after acquiring Intel’s NAND flash unit, pouring in capital to build it into an enterprise SSD powerhouse. Now that Solidigm is turning massive profits, management's push to list it separately in New York feels to local investors like a classic corporate betrayal. Under SK Group's convoluted multi-tiered ownership structure, listing subsidiaries separately dilutes parent stock value—a practice long blamed for the persistent "Korea Discount." Local retail shareholder advocacy group ACT has already delivered formal letters demanding board accountability.

Margin Debt Cascades and Structural Bottlenecks

Exacerbating governance fears is an explosive surge in margin trading. As retail investors rushed to buy semiconductor dips, margin debt skyrocketed, leaving individual accounts vulnerable to forced broker sell-offs (margin calls) during market swings. Because over 70% of South Korean household wealth is locked up in real estate, stock market liquidity remains thin and prone to sudden leverage cascades whenever foreign institutional investors offload blue-chip shares.

Compounding domestic fragility are growing global doubts over AI infrastructure spending, where rising US power grid constraints and elevated borrowing costs are squeezing data center operators. Yet Korea's market vulnerabilities remain primarily self-inflicted. Unless Seoul amends corporate laws to enforce directors' fiduciary duty to all shareholders and eliminates tax structures that incentivize controlling families to depress stock prices for tax breaks, semiconductor booms will continue to leave retail investors behind.
