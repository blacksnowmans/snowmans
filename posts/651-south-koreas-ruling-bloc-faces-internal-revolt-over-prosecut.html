South Korea’s Ruling Bloc Faces Internal Revolt Over Prosecutor Reform and Controversial Appointees

October 03, 2026

  • What happened: Two heavyweights of South Korea's progressive movement—former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae and veteran political commentator Rhyu Si-min—have launched scathing public criticisms of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration over flawed political appointments and a diluted prosecution reform agenda.
  • The flashpoint: Choo publicly confessed to a grave error in judgment after the presidential office nominated Kim Ji-yong, a career prosecutor she once promoted during her tenure, to head the newly established Serious Crime Investigation Agency.
  • Wider fallout: Rhyu accused the administration of betraying its own reform promises on separating investigative and indicting powers, comparing the government's approach to "a cow being dragged to the slaughterhouse," while also blasting systemic nepotism within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.
  • Who is affected: President Lee's administration, the ruling Democratic Party, and progressives who fear that infighting and compromised leadership are derailing crucial checks on prosecutorial power.

A Progressive Civil War Over Reform and Patronage

South Korea’s progressive camp is grappling with a rare and bitter internal reckoning. When Choo Mi-ae—now a provincial governor—apologized for previously promoting Kim Ji-yong, she punctured the administration's narrative that Kim's appointment is justified because he was once sidelined under the previous conservative government. Choo argued that labeling officials as pro- or anti-reform based on superficial political frames misses the point, warning that installing compromised figures at the helm of a key investigative body sabotages the entire reform mission.

Compounding the pressure, Rhyu Si-min returned to the public spotlight with an equally blunt assessment of President Lee’s leadership. Rhyu claimed that the administration's revised plans for prosecutorial reform fall far short of the sweeping structural overhaul promised to voters, effectively giving prosecutors a backdoor to preserve their influence. Beyond policy, Rhyu lambasted the Democratic Party’s internal candidate selection processes, pointing to a string of controversial parachuted appointments that he argued expose a party culture grown arrogant and insulated from dissent.

The Danger of Shutting Down Dissent

The core issue highlighted by these high-profile critiques is not merely a few bad personnel picks, but an administration increasingly allergic to internal criticism. Rather than addressing substantive arguments about policy dilution and vetting failures, ruling camp figures have responded defensively, with some even floating legal action against critics. When a government attempts to neutralize debate by slapping loyalty frames on dissent or threatening legal blowback against its own allies, it invites a severe loss of public trust. For a progressive administration that came to power on promises of transparency and institutional overhaul, ignoring these warning signs risks alienating the very base that made reform possible in the first place.
