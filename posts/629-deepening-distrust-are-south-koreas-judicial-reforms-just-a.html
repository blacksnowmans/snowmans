Deepening Distrust: Are South Korea’s Judicial Reforms Just a Facade?

September 10, 2026

Supporters of the Lee Jae-myung administration are sounding the alarm, accusing the government of betraying its campaign promise to strip the prosecution of its unchecked power and instead expanding its influence under the guise of reform.

  • The Core Dispute: New plans to reorganize the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO)—the agency created to curb prosecutorial abuse—are being criticized as mere "window dressing" that grants the office more power rather than true independence.
  • Institutional Overreach: Critics argue that revised investigative rules allow prosecutors to pull suspects from police custody at will, effectively subordinating the police to the prosecution.
  • Controversial Appointments: The proposed nomination of a "prosecution loyalist"—who historically opposed the separation of investigation and indictment—to lead the new Major Crimes Investigation Agency has sparked outrage among party members.
  • Budgetary Neglect: Despite plans to transfer 170,000 pending cases from the prosecution to the police, the government increased the police investigative budget by only about $220,000 (300 million won), a move critics call an intentional sabotage of police capacity.

The Illusion of Reform

The fundamental promise of the current administration was the "separation of investigation and indictment." The goal was to prevent a single organization from holding the power to both build a case and force a trial, a structure that has led to decades of selective investigations and political weaponization of the law. However, the latest bureaucratic maneuvers appear to be doing the opposite. By creating new departments within the CIO and granting prosecutors greater access to suspects, the government is building a more sophisticated apparatus for control rather than dismantling the old one.

Even more alarming to supporters is the personnel strategy. Placing a staunch defender of the existing prosecutorial system at the head of the newly formed Major Crimes Investigation Agency is seen as a tactical surrender. It suggests that the government is not interested in breaking up the "legal cartel"—the entrenched network of former prosecutors and judges—but rather in capturing it for its own use. For the grassroots supporters who campaigned on the "candlelight revolution" to demand fairness, this feels like a betrayal of the movement’s original intent.

A Critical Juncture

The urgency in the recent protests by Democratic Party members stems from the fear that the administration is squandering its "golden time" for reform. There is a palpable sense of betrayal; supporters feel that the government is being captured by the very establishment they were elected to reform. The administration's rhetoric regarding "restraint"—arguing that they must avoid overreaching—is being interpreted by activists as a convenient excuse to avoid the politically difficult work of cleaning up the judicial system.

If the government continues to prioritize the preservation of the current legal hierarchy over genuine structural change, it risks losing its base. The message from party members is clear: they are not just spectators, but stakeholders who expect the administration to fulfill its mandate. By failing to ensure that investigations are transparent and detached from prosecutorial bias, the government is not only breaking a promise—it is actively strengthening the very institutions that have historically operated above the law.
