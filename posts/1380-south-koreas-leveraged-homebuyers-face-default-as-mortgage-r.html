South Korea’s Leveraged Homebuyers Face Default as Mortgage Rates Threaten 9%

October 21, 2026

South Korea’s pandemic-era property boom has turned into a slow-motion debt trap, as mortgage rates climbing toward 9 percent trigger a surge in court-ordered foreclosures and leave overleveraged young homeowners trapped with unsellable assets.

  • What happened: Borrowers who took on massive floating-rate loans during the 2020–2021 ultra-low-rate period are being crushed by debt service costs; at a 9 percent rate, interest alone on a typical 500 million won (about $375,000) mortgage reaches 3.75 million won (roughly $2,800) per month.
  • What it changes: A complete transaction freeze has gripped outer districts of Seoul—including Eunpyeong, Nowon, and Gangseo—as asking prices remain detached from what buyers can finance, pushing distressed properties straight into judicial auctions.
  • Who is affected: Primarily buyers in their 20s and 30s, widely dubbed the yeong-kkeul ("soul-pulling") generation, who exhausted every credit line to buy apartments at the peak of the market.

From Easy Credit to the Auction Block

The current shock is far more severe than past tightening cycles because the sheer size of the principal borrowed has grown dramatically. During the frenzy of 2020 and 2021, buyers took out hundreds of thousands of dollars under the assumption that low borrowing costs were permanent. Because most South Korean mortgages carry variable interest rates, global monetary tightening—anchored by elevated US Treasury yields—has transferred directly to household bank accounts, wiping out discretionary income and dampening broader domestic consumption.

With market hopes of a rapid pivot to rate cuts dashed, the reality of "higher for longer" borrowing costs has broken the myth of real estate as an invincible asset class. Sellers are unwilling to take steep realized losses, while potential buyers cannot pass banking stress tests. The result is a liquidity drought where apartments function less like wealth and more like non-negotiable liabilities.

The Policy Dilemma for the Lee Administration

This structural hangover traces back in large part to the policy choices made during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term, when the government repeatedly injected subsidized policy loans to prop up housing prices rather than allowing the market to absorb a natural price correction. That intervention created a temporary rebound in late 2022 and 2023, encouraging a second wave of young buyers to enter the market out of panic.

President Lee Jae-myung's administration now faces a precarious balancing act. South Korea's household debt has reached an inflection point where further state-backed credit expansion risks systemic financial stability, yet withdrawing support too quickly will accelerate defaults and auction fire-sales. For an entire generation of young Korean professionals, the price of admission into the property market has turned into a punishing lesson in the risks of unhedged leverage.
