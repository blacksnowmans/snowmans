Why South Korea's Obsession with Cram Schools is Failing Kids—And What Experts Say Works Instead

September 03, 2026

  • What happened: Child education experts in South Korea are pushing back against the country's relentless early cram-school culture, arguing that stuffing children's heads with advanced academic material before they are ready damages their capacity to learn.
  • What it changes: The focus shifts away from early test preparation and toward building fundamental "inquiry skills," problem-solving resilience, and physical stamina.
  • Who is affected: South Korean elementary school students and anxious parents navigating the country's notoriously cutthroat education system.

South Korea’s hyper-competitive education landscape is famous for driving toddlers into after-school academies, known as hagwons, to get a head start on elite universities. But a growing counter-movement argues that parents are doing it all wrong. Instead of endlessly drilling children with advanced subjects, experts say the priority should be building "the brain's vessel"—the cognitive foundation and curiosity required to process complex ideas later in life. Rote memorization and early cramming, they warn, simply leak out of a broken foundation.

The core of this alternative approach relies on changing how parents talk to their children and redefining what counts as "studying." Activities as mundane as origami teach crucial debugging and software-like thinking when a child makes a mistake and figures out how to fix it. Meanwhile, physical stamina—often dismissed in favor of extra study hours—is framed as a vital prerequisite for enduring the grueling marathon of high school exams. Ultimately, these educators argue that true academic endurance comes not from forced repetition, but from encouraging children to ask "why" and letting them build problem-solving muscles on their own.
