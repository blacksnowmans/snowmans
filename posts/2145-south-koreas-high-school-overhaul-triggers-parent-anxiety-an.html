South Korea's High School Overhaul Triggers Parent Anxiety and Teacher Pushback

November 09, 2026

South Korea’s rollout of the High School Credit System and the looming 2028 college entrance reform are stoking deep anxiety among parents and fierce resistance from teachers over classroom staffing shortages.

  • Parent information rush: Regional education offices in Jeonnam-Gwangju, Suwon, and Gwangmyeong held crowded briefings for parents of ninth-graders facing major structural shifts in high school course selection and university admissions criteria.
  • Teacher backlash against deregulation: Public school teachers in Gyeonggi Province strongly condemned proposals by the National Education Commission to fill specialized course openings with outside contractors and uncertified instructors, calling it a stopgap measure that undermines educational quality.
  • Public push on AI and new careers: Local governments and universities are launching public consulting sessions, AI literacy workshops, and non-traditional career events—spanning drone piloting and e-sports—to alleviate reliance on aggressive private tutoring marketing.

A High-Stakes Shift to Choice-Based Learning

The High School Credit System is designed to mirror university-style curriculum flexibility, allowing students to tailor their coursework toward individual career paths rather than memorizing a rigid national syllabus. However, giving students the freedom to choose diverse electives requires a vast, adaptable pool of qualified teachers. In response, education officials under President Lee Jae-myung have considered allowing uncertified external specialists and non-regular teachers into public classrooms, provoking immediate outrage from established teaching unions who demand funding for permanent, fully qualified faculty instead.

The policy tensions reflect an ongoing effort by the current administration to stabilize public education after policy turbulence and unpredictability during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency. Yet, for parents of ninth-grade students—the first cohort fully subject to the revised 2028 entrance exam structure—the lack of permanent classroom staffing signals that schools are ill-prepared to deliver on the system's promises.

The Battle Between Public Consulting and Private Fear

The gap between policy ambition and classroom reality continues to feed South Korea's massive private education industry. Commercial cram schools, known locally as hagwons, routinely weaponize structural changes to sell costly admissions consulting, warning parents that selecting the wrong electives could ruin their children's university prospects. In response, municipal governments in cities like Suwon and Gwangmyeong have stepped in to offer free, public career-counseling clinics to level the information playing field.

While public initiatives—ranging from AI-assisted writing clinics to tech-focused career expos—offer temporary relief for middle-class budgets, they cannot substitute for structural reform inside regular high schools. Without long-term fiscal commitments to recruit permanent teachers and bridge curriculum gaps between wealthy metropolitan districts and regional schools, the transition to choice-based secondary education risks worsening the educational inequality it was supposed to solve.
