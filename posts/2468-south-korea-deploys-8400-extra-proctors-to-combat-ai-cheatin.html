South Korea Deploys 8,400 Extra Proctors to Combat AI Cheating on College Entrance Exam

November 18, 2026

South Korea's Lee Jae-myung administration has deployed 8,400 additional proctors and partnered with global tech firms to track AI-assisted cheating during the Suneung, the nation's high-stakes annual college entrance exam.

  • A massive staffing surge: On November 17, Prime Minister Han Seong-sook launched a pan-government emergency task force, adding 1,800 smart-device specialists and 6,600 hallway proctors nationwide, while the education office in Gyeonggi Province—home to South Korea's largest student population—deployed 702 dedicated monitors of its own.
  • Strict checks and deferred penalties: Staff inspect every student's glasses and watches before every test period and send emergency alert texts mid-exam to expose hidden phones; merely wearing a smart device, using it during breaks, or refusing inspection counts as cheating, though Gyeonggi Superintendent Ahn Min-seok said authorities will investigate after the exam rather than eject suspects mid-test.
  • Post-exam digital tracking: Deputy Prime Minister and Science Minister Bae Kyung-hoon noted that tuning global AI models to block answers solely for Korea's exam is technically impractical, shifting the strategy toward working with Big Tech firms to trace leaked test images alongside a proposed 10-year exam ban.
  • Mixed political reactions: Seongnam City Councilor Lee Se-mi, from the ruling Democratic Party, criticized the rollout as rushed given that ministries flagged technical gaps back in July, whereas Democratic Party lawmaker Park Ji-won argued the government must use every tool available—from metal detectors to thermal sensors—to protect student trust.

The Limits of Playing Defense

In South Korea, where 12 years of preparation culminate in a single exam day, any suspicion of unfair advantage threatens the legitimacy of the entire system. Yet relying on thousands of human monitors and emergency text alerts underscores an uncomfortable truth: visual checks can barely keep up with cameras hidden inside ordinary-looking eyeglass frames. Even with Big Tech helping trace digital footprints afterward, a proposed 10-year ban only deters students if they believe they will actually be caught.

The timeline also exposes bureaucratic friction. Because the Suneung takes place every November, recognizing technical limitations at a July meeting involving the personnel and labor ministries left months to prepare before radio-frequency detectors were finally ordered in October. Still, Gyeonggi's policy of waiting until the final bell to investigate suspected students offers a sensible safeguard, ensuring a proctor's misjudgment over thick glasses does not ruin an innocent student's year.

Rethinking an Exam Built on Memorization

Ultimately, adding thousands of proctors every year is an unsustainable arms race. As Ahn Min-seok warned, hardware is shrinking toward smart lenses and microchips that will easily defeat hallway inspections. Rather than endlessly trying to block technology, South Korea faces growing pressure to overhaul the Suneung itself—replacing multiple-choice memorization with essay or project-based assessments that test how students critique and apply AI-generated answers.
