2,400 Local Councilors Rally at South Korea’s Parliament Demanding Autonomy from Mayors and Governors

October 28, 2026

About 2,400 local council members from across South Korea converged outside the National Assembly in Seoul on October 27, demanding the immediate passage of a standalone Local Council Act to grant assemblies independent budgetary and organizational authority.

  • Mass mobilization: In a rare show of cross-party solidarity, councilors representing provincial, municipal, and district legislatures nationwide rallied in Yeouido, Seoul, urging parliament to pass the reform bill before the end of the year.
  • The core grievance: Although local autonomy was revived 35 years ago in 1991, local councils remain legally and financially subordinated to regional mayors and governors, leaving them unable to effectively audit local executive branches.
  • What changes: The proposed Local Council Act would provide a legal foundation—comparable to the National Assembly Act for the national parliament—allowing local assemblies to control their own operational budgets and hire specialized oversight staff without executive branch interference.
  • The stakeholders: South Korea’s regional councilors, local government executives managing budgets worth tens of billions of dollars, and taxpayers whose funds currently face lopsided oversight.

A 35-Year Struggle Against 'Rubber-Stamp' Assemblies

South Korea’s local self-governance has long suffered from a structural imbalance known domestically as the "strong executive, weak council" system. While local governments now administer massive budgets running into trillions of won (equivalent to billions of dollars), the assemblies tasked with scrutinizing those expenditures do not even possess the power to formulate their own administrative budgets. Under the current framework, local council operating funds are dictated by the mayors and governors they are supposed to hold accountable, creating an inherent conflict of interest whenever councilors attempt aggressive investigations.

Staffing and organizational restrictions further compound the problem. Despite minor revisions to the Local Autonomy Act in recent years, core organizational powers—such as creating departments and managing permanent staffing caps—remain tightly regulated by the Ministry of the Interior and Safety and local chief executives. Unable to recruit adequate specialized policy support staff independently, local lawmakers find themselves severely outgunned when vetting hundreds of complex municipal projects.

Breaking Central Subordination in the Lee Administration Era

The demonstration, led by Gyeonggi Provincial Council Chairman Nam Jong-seop, brought together politicians from both the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the main conservative opposition People Power Party. Historically, National Assembly members have sidelined local council legislation, treating regional legislators as subordinate campaign foot soldiers rather than autonomous lawmakers. This dynamic has kept local politics firmly chained to central party hierarchies in Seoul.

With President Lee Jae-myung’s administration actively emphasizing balanced regional development and greater decentralization—drawing a deliberate contrast with the top-down centralism witnessed during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office—local leaders argue that parliament has run out of excuses to stall. While critics often worry about potential budget bloat at the municipal level, councilors contend that real local democracy cannot function if local watchdogs are forced to ask their targets for permission to operate.
