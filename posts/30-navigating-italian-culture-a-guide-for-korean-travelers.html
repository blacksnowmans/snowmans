Navigating Italian Culture: A Guide for Korean Travelers

September 24, 2026

For South Korean travelers, the cultural nuances of Italy—specifically regarding dress code and dining etiquette—can often feel like a series of misunderstandings, but they are simply expressions of local values that prioritize formality and the "slow food" philosophy.

  • Dress Standards: In Italy, flip-flops and casual house slippers are strictly for indoor use; wearing them in public is often viewed as a lack of consideration for one's surroundings.
  • Dining Pace: Waiters clearing empty plates promptly is a sign of professional service designed to keep the table clean for the next course, not a hint to leave.
  • The "Coperto": Most Italian restaurants charge a small service fee (coperto, roughly $1 to $4), which replaces the need for individual tipping.
  • Customizing Meals: Salads and sides are often served "naked," as locals prefer to season their food individually with olive oil and vinegar provided at the table.

Beyond Convenience: The Italian Philosophy

In South Korea, where the "Pali-pali" (hurry-hurry) culture emphasizes efficiency and rapid turnover, the Italian approach to dining can be disorienting. Italian restaurants generally view a meal as an event—an essential social ritual rather than just a transaction. If you find a waiter clearing your plate, realize that they are maintaining the rhythm of a multi-course experience. Similarly, don't feel pressured to rush out; in many local establishments, it is perfectly normal to linger for hours over conversation and coffee.

When it comes to fashion, the Italian emphasis on presentation is not about being elitist, but about showing respect for public spaces and the people you encounter. While stylish sandals are perfectly acceptable for summer outings, trading your rubber house slippers for a pair of decent shoes will often result in a warmer reception from locals and better service at restaurants.

Mastering the Menu

Korean dining culture often favors putting all dishes on the table at once, but Italian service follows a strict hierarchy: Antipasto (appetizer), Primo (first course, like pasta), Secondo (main meat or fish), and Contorno (side dish). If you prefer your food to arrive simultaneously, simply say, "All together."

Finally, remember that the table is your space. You are not obligated to order an entire course if you aren't hungry, and you can decline dessert with a polite "Va bene così" (I'm fine, thank you). By embracing these differences rather than viewing them as obstacles, you shift your experience from merely observing a foreign country to actually participating in its culture.
