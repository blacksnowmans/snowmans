Seoul’s Defense Ministry Asserts South Korea Is Ready for Immediate Wartime Command Transfer

October 16, 2026

South Korea’s defense ministry has officially asserted that reclaiming wartime operational control from the United States would pose no risk to national security or the bilateral alliance, even if executed immediately.

  • What happened: Testifying before the National Assembly’s defense committee, Vice Defense Minister Kang Shin-chul affirmed that the military is prepared to assume wartime operational control (OPCON) immediately, backing recent remarks by President Lee Jae-myung.
  • The political clash: Lawmaker Yoon Sang-hyun of the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition, fiercely challenged the timeline, citing cautionary remarks by Gen. Xavier Brunson, commander of U.S. Forces Korea, regarding changing strategic criteria and command-and-control preparedness.
  • The planned command structure: The transfer does not dismantle the alliance or prompt a U.S. troop withdrawal; rather, it inverses leadership within the bilateral Combined Forces Command, placing a South Korean four-star general in the top post supported by an American deputy commander.
  • The shifting criteria: The defense ministry pushed back against perpetual delays, arguing that transition conditions cannot be treated purely as an unreachable quantitative checklist ("science"), but must ultimately rely on strategic political resolve ("art").

Breaking the Cycle of Endless Conditions

The ministry’s decisive testimony marks a deliberate pivot away from years of administrative foot-dragging. Ever since a "condition-based" transition framework was established a decade ago, critics argue that security bureaucracies in both Washington and Seoul have repeatedly moved the goalposts. Whenever South Korea met baseline goals for command systems or counter-artillery capabilities, new hurdles were introduced—from advanced surveillance fidelity to broader integration with U.S. Indo-Pacific strategy. During Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, this conditional framework effectively placed the transfer on indefinite hold. By framing readiness as a strategic judgment call rather than an endless technical audit, the Lee Jae-myung administration is attempting to cut through bureaucratic inertia.

Alliance Equality Versus Strategic Dependency

The confrontation in parliament highlights the perennial ideological divide over South Korea's military standing. The conservative opposition views independent command as an unnecessary gamble that risks alienating Washington and undermining deterrence against North Korea. Lawmakers have leaned heavily on cautious rhetoric from U.S. defense circles to argue Seoul is unready. From the perspective of the current military leadership, however, maintaining a system where the world's fifth- or sixth-ranked military surrenders operational command of its own forces during wartime remains an abnormal relic. Restructuring the command puts the country with the most at stake in the lead, supported by American capabilities, moving the bilateral alliance closer to a mature partnership rather than a relationship of dependency.
