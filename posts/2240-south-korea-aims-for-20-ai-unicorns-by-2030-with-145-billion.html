South Korea Aims for 20 AI Unicorns by 2030 with $1.45 Billion R&D Push

November 12, 2026

The South Korean government has announced an aggressive new strategy to foster 20 AI "unicorns"—startups valued at over $720 million (1 trillion KRW)—and at least one "decacorn" by 2030, backed by a record-breaking R&D budget.

  • The Ministry of SMEs and Startups will provide over $7.2 million (10 billion KRW) in direct scale-up funding to selected high-potential AI firms to bridge the "valley of death" between initial tech development and global expansion.
  • Total AI and ICT (Information and Communication Technology) research and development spending will exceed $1.45 billion (2 trillion KRW) for the first time in 2025.
  • The strategy prioritizes "Physical AI"—the integration of artificial intelligence into robotics and manufacturing—to leverage South Korea's existing industrial strengths.
  • The Lee Jae-myung administration views tightening global regulations, such as the EU AI Act, as a market opportunity for Korean firms specializing in AI security, ethics, and "explainable AI" (XAI).

A Shift from Subsidies to Concentrated Betting

The Lee Jae-myung administration is pivotally shifting away from the "scattershot" funding model that often characterized previous startup support. By promising over $7.2 million per company, the government is attempting to function more like a late-stage venture capital firm than a traditional bureaucracy. This "selection and concentration" approach acknowledges that Korean AI firms have historically struggled with the massive infrastructure costs and high-priced talent required to compete globally. Currently, South Korea ranks 9th in the world with only six AI unicorns; the goal to more than triple that number in six years is an admission that the current ecosystem lacks the scale to challenge the U.S. or China.

The focus on Physical AI and AX (AI Transformation) is a calculated move. South Korea is unlikely to win the foundational Large Language Model (LLM) race against global giants like OpenAI or Google. Instead, by embedding AI into its world-class manufacturing and robotics sectors, the government is playing to the nation's structural advantages. The success of this plan will depend on whether these R&D investments result in actual industrial productivity—such as lowering defect rates or automating complex logistics—rather than just academic papers and patents.

Recovering from R&D Contraction

The record-breaking $1.45 billion R&D budget for 2025 marks a definitive reversal of the R&D funding cuts and fiscal uncertainty that defined the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. This massive influx of capital is intended to serve as "priming water" to de-risk private sector investment, which has cooled globally. However, the government must avoid the "bureaucratic trap." If the Ministry of SMEs and Startups uses traditional civil service metrics—like the number of patents filed—to judge these startups, it will fail. To create a $7.2 billion decacorn, the government must allow its chosen champions the flexibility to pivot and fail fast in the global market.

"Global AI regulation is not a barrier, but a unique opportunity for prepared Korean firms to lead the market in trust and safety," says Ryu Je-myeong, the Second Vice Minister of Science and ICT.

Ultimately, the "2030 AI Leap" strategy is a race against time. As global tech giants consolidate their power, South Korea’s window to establish a "K-AI" niche is closing. The government’s role in opening overseas sales channels and helping startups navigate international regulations will be just as critical as the cash injections. If the administration can successfully link its manufacturing DNA with these new AI investments, South Korea may finally move beyond being a fast follower to becoming a primary architect of the industrial AI era.
