South Korea’s Parliamentary Audits Gridlocked by Judiciary Clashes, Scandals, and Presidential Reshuffle

October 18, 2026

South Korea’s annual parliamentary audits have descended into bitter political warfare as the ruling Democratic Party floated impeaching the Supreme Court Chief Justice, while the conservative opposition fought back against what it termed an assault on constitutional order.

  • Judiciary in the crosshairs: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) escalated threats to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, prompting the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) to accuse the majority of undermining judicial independence to protect its own leaders.
  • Blue House reshuffle: President Lee Jae-myung named former National Tax Service deputy commissioner Lim Gwang-hyun as his new presidential chief of staff, a technocratic move aimed at boosting the economy that opposition critics dismissed as unimaginative recycling.
  • Committee breakdowns: Proceedings in the science and media committee collapsed amid shouting matches over the attendance of Korea Communications Commission chair Lee Jin-sook and closed-door audit sessions for public broadcaster MBC.
  • Dueling legal fronts: Prosecutors dropped all charges against former President Moon Jae-in and top aides over a 2020 border incident, effectively ending a high-profile inquiry launched during Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, even as police referred prominent conservative figure Yoo Seong-min to prosecutors over allegations of hiring favors for his daughter.

Partisan Crossfire Engulfs the Courts

The annual audit, intended to serve as a rigorous check on government administration and policy spending, has instead become a stage for existential legal combat. The fiercest clash erupted in the parliamentary judiciary committee, where the ruling DPK openly signaled impeachment discussions against Chief Justice Cho. The PPP condemned the move as an attempt to intimidate the judiciary and shield the ruling bloc, igniting fierce legal debates over Article 84 of the Constitution regarding presidential immunity from prosecution and ongoing court trials.

The hostilities spilled across committees. Proceedings ground to a halt in the science and broadcasting committee as lawmakers traded accusations over regulator qualifications and media oversight, abandoning substantive inquiries into telecom policy and public broadcasting governance. Instead of policy scrutiny, televised hearings delivered shouting matches, gavel-banging, and tactical walkouts designed primarily to rally each party's core political base.

Reshuffling the Deck Amid Lingering Feuds

President Lee Jae-myung sought to project administrative stability by appointing tax expert Lim Gwang-hyun as his second chief of staff. The administration is pitching the appointment as a practical step to revitalize the sluggish economy and improve coordination between the presidential office and the National Assembly. However, the move has drawn sharp skepticism from the PPP and the minor opposition Reform Party, who argue a career bureaucrat lacks the political clout needed to bridge the widening rift between rival factions.

Meanwhile, the clearing of Moon administration officials over the 2020 killing of a fisheries official by North Korean forces underscores how political tides dictate justice in Seoul. The closure of the inquiry delivers a quiet rebuke to the aggressive investigations pursued during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. Yet, with conservative rival Yoo Seong-min now facing prosecution and lawmakers weaponizing past scandals on both sides, weaponized justice remains the primary currency of South Korean politics—drowning out rare moments of bipartisan focus on pressing livelihood issues like industrial pollution and emergency worker safety.
