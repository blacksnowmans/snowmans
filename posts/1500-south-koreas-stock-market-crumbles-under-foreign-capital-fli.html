South Korea’s Stock Market Crumbles Under Foreign Capital Flight and Weak Domestic Fundamentals

October 26, 2026

South Korean equities have entered a severe downturn as persistent foreign selling and exhausted domestic buying power leave both the main board KOSPI and tech-heavy KOSDAQ vulnerable to compounding losses.

  • Foreign sell-off triggers systemic slide: A single-day foreign net sell-off of 700 billion won (about $520 million) wiped out support lines, pushing the benchmark KOSPI down to 5,469 points as domestic buyers failed to step in.
  • SK Hynix treasury share retirement fails to boost sentiment: Semiconductor giant SK Hynix announced the cancellation of 23.7 million existing treasury shares worth roughly 40 trillion won ($30 billion), but the market brushed it off as an accounting exercise rather than a fresh buyback.
  • KOSDAQ faces crisis as zombie firms linger: The tech-heavy KOSDAQ plunged 3.26% to 727 points, accelerating plans by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea to revise the Capital Market Act and force out underperforming companies.
  • Macro headwinds and derivative liquidations: Goldman Sachs slashed its KOSPI target by 25% to 9,000 points, while extreme volatility led to the total liquidation of a US-listed 2x leveraged SK Hynix ETF after just three months.

A Liquidity Cushion That Has Completely Evaporated

The Korean stock market's recent collapse underscores a structural fragility that quick corporate fixes can no longer conceal. In previous cycles, domestic institutions and retail investors could absorb foreign sell-offs exceeding a trillion won. Today, an outflow of just $520 million causes widespread market panic. With almost all sectors declining except for bank stocks riding high on corporate value-up promises, foreign funds continue to treat South Korean equities as a short-term parking spot rather than a long-term investment destination.

Corporate Governance Deficits and Regulatory Reckoning

The gap between corporate announcements and investor expectations remains a core vulnerability. SK Hynix’s decision to retire 40 trillion won ($30 billion) in existing treasury stock—while remaining silent on special cash dividends from its lucrative Kioxia investment—left investors underwhelmed compared to Wall Street's active share buyback models. Meanwhile, liquidations of high-leverage ETF products in foreign markets highlight how quickly derivative bets implode when underlying stock prices fall sharply.

On the junior KOSDAQ exchange, nearly 1,800 listed companies share a collective market capitalization of just 407 trillion won ($300 billion). Years of lenient listing standards have allowed distressed "zombie" firms to linger, diluting capital that should flow to legitimate tech innovators. While the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is advancing legislative reforms to restructure the exchange and expedite delistings, repairing investor confidence will take far more than administrative reorganizations.
