South Korea’s Ruling Party Slips Behind Conservatives as Core Voters Send a Warning

November 17, 2026

In a regular poll for the second week of November, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating dropped to a post-inauguration low of 35.0%, while his ruling Democratic Party fell to 27.2% against 41.5% for the conservative People Power Party.

  • Broad erosion of support: Disapproval of President Lee climbed to 61.9%, with majorities turning negative across every age group and region—including Honam, the southwestern stronghold that traditionally anchors liberal politics, and voters in their 40s, whose approval ratings neared the 20s.
  • A protest surge on the left: The Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive opposition party, topped 10% in a regular poll for the first time at 10.1%—reaching 14.8% in Honam—with more than 90% of its backers expressing disapproval of the Lee administration.
  • Competing explanations inside the liberal camp: Prominent liberal commentator Yoo Si-min attributed the slump to collapsing trust, stalled prosecution reform, and outreach to centrists, whereas Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok and lawmaker Kim Young-jin rejected divisive rhetoric and urged coalition-building.
  • Methodological caveats and policy pressures: Kim Jong-won, CEO of polling firm EveryResearch, noted that automated phone surveys amplify rapid shifts and reluctant-voter effects compared with live interviews, while citing capital-area housing policy, living costs, appointment fairness controversies, and internal disputes over troop deployments to the Strait of Hormuz as key risks ahead of the 2028 parliamentary elections.

What the Split on the Left Means

The headline numbers point less to a sudden migration of liberal voters into conservative ranks than to an internal warning shot from the ruling camp’s own base. Yoo Si-min’s diagnosis—that frustrated supporters are using the Rebuilding Korea Party as a protest signal and a political “lifeboat”—captures why a 27.2% and 10.1% split feels alarming to party insiders. Even so, Democratic lawmakers are right to question whether retreating into ideological purity is viable when a sitting government must pass legislation and appeal beyond its core voters.

The Governing Test Ahead

Yoo pointed to the collapse of conservative unity during the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later removed from office via impeachment, as proof that alienating a core coalition can doom a presidency. Yet centrist observers and ruling-party pragmatists make an equally grounded point: governing a divided parliament requires compromise, and delays on issues like prosecution reform reflect real institutional and legislative hurdles rather than a simple lack of presidential will.

Ultimately, debates over polling methods or ideological framing matter far less to voters than tangible outcomes. Whether tracked by automated surveys or live interviewers, public trust will likely recover only if the Lee administration delivers credible results on housing, everyday economic pressures, and perceived fairness in government appointments—especially as the People Power Party weighs its own leadership future before 2028.
