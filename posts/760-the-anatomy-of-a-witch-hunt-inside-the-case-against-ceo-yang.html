The Anatomy of a Witch Hunt: Inside the Case Against CEO Yang Soo-jin

September 15, 2026

The controversy surrounding South Korean entrepreneur Yang Soo-jin has evolved into a case study on how aggressive state power, media sensationalism, and online misinformation can collide to dismantle an individual’s reputation.

  • Questionable Prosecutions: Prosecutors reportedly struggled to build a case, at one point dropping charges only to reopen investigations, sparking allegations of politically motivated "target investigations."
  • Media Framing: Outlets and YouTubers have been accused of labeling Yang as a "room salon madam" based on her past ownership of unremarkable, legal businesses.
  • Guilt by Association: A photograph of Yang alongside major corporate leaders at a 2023 government event was weaponized by critics to allege illicit ties to power.

A Pattern of Prosecutorial Overreach

The investigation into Yang has faced severe criticism for its lack of procedural integrity. Critics point to the tenure of former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon—a prominent conservative figure—as a period when the prosecution appeared hyper-focused on specific targets, often cycling through investigation teams to ensure an indictment. This heavy-handed approach, characterized by aggressive "dust-clearing" investigations—a Korean term for scouring a target’s life for any minor irregularity—has left many wondering if the legal process is being used as a weapon against private citizens.

The fact that the prosecution repeatedly fumbled, at times essentially telling the accused they were cleared only to pivot back to hostility, suggests a lack of substantive evidence. When a state agency acts with such inconsistency, it fuels the perception that the institution is operating on political directives rather than the rule of law. The human cost of these "fishing expeditions" is immense, yet there is little accountability for the damage inflicted when a case ultimately collapses.

Misinformation and the "Room Salon" Myth

Much of the public vitriol against Yang stems from the deliberate mischaracterization of her past business ventures. Yang once operated two establishments—a general eatery and a seafood restaurant—near local schools. While these were entirely legal, conventional businesses, they were quickly rebranded by sensationalist media as "room salons" (high-end hostess clubs). This label was designed to trigger moral outrage, effectively stripping away the nuance of reality for the sake of clicks and engagement.

The narrative was further bolstered by a photo taken at a 2023 event hosted by the government for small and medium-sized enterprises. Because Yang was pictured in the same sphere as titans like Samsung’s Lee Jae-yong, bad actors spun a web of conspiracy theories, painting her presence as proof of "cronyism." In reality, she was simply one of many female entrepreneurs invited to a standard industry event. By ignoring the context of her professional status, critics turned a routine networking opportunity into a scandal. In a climate where the line between fact and algorithm-driven outrage has blurred, Yang’s case highlights a dangerous trend: if the truth isn't scandalous enough, the media will simply invent a fiction that is.
