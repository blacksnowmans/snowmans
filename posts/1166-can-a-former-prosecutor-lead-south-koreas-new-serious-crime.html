Can a Former Prosecutor Lead South Korea’s New Serious Crime Investigative Agency?

October 15, 2026

Kim Ji-yong, the nominee to lead the newly established Serious Crime Investigative Agency (SCIA), faced a grueling confirmation hearing on Thursday, highlighting the deep-seated tensions in President Lee Jae-myung’s ongoing campaign to overhaul the nation's legal system.

  • Kim pledged to end the "political prosecution" era, specifically criticizing the legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president currently imprisoned after his impeachment—for damaging the integrity of the investigative process.
  • The SCIA is a central pillar of the Democratic Party (DP) government’s reforms, designed to strip the prosecution service of its long-held monopoly on both investigating and indicting major crimes.
  • Critics from both sides of the aisle questioned Kim's past, pointing to his history of approving controversial indictments and participating in collective actions by senior prosecutors during the previous administration.
  • Structural independence remains a major concern, as the SCIA will operate under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, leading to fears that the agency could become a tool for the executive branch rather than an independent watchdog.

Breaking the "Cartel" of the Prosecution

Kim’s nomination is being viewed as a barometer for whether the Lee administration can move beyond political rhetoric to achieve functional reform. During the hearing, Kim admitted that the "fishing expedition" style of investigations prevalent during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure as Prosecutor General had left "deep scars" on Korean society. He promised that the SCIA would focus on the strict separation of investigation and indictment, ensuring that the agency does not repeat the old habit of deciding on a target first and finding the crime later.

However, Kim’s transition from a career prosecutor to the head of a body meant to check the prosecution's power has met with skepticism. Lawmakers like Park Joo-min of the ruling Democratic Party (South Korea's liberal party) characterized Kim’s past as that of a "silent accomplice" who failed to stop the politicization of the law from within. For the SCIA to succeed, it must be more than just the old prosecution service with a new nameplate; it requires a leader willing to dismantle the very institutional culture that raised him.

The Dilemma of Political Control

A significant portion of the debate focused on the SCIA’s reporting structure. Unlike the National Investigative Headquarters, which has a degree of insulation from the police chief, the SCIA is subject to the "specific case direction" of the Minister of the Interior and Safety. Kim defended this as a form of "democratic control," but opposition members from the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition) warned it could turn the SCIA into a "loyal dog" of the current administration.

Ultimately, the success of the SCIA—and Kim’s potential tenure—will depend on whether the agency can prove its neutrality in high-stakes cases. Kim’s assertion that he would "keep a resignation letter in his pocket" to resist political pressure is a common refrain in Korean confirmation hearings, but it offers little comfort to those worried about structural vulnerabilities. If the SCIA fails to establish itself as a truly independent body, it risks becoming another weapon in South Korea's cycle of political retribution rather than a guardian of judicial justice.
