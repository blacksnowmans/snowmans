APEC Summit: The High-Stakes Diplomacy of Lee Jae-myung and Global Power Brokers

November 19, 2026

The APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) summit in Shenzhen has transformed from a routine multilateral forum into a high-stakes diplomatic furnace where the global order is being rewritten. South Korean President Lee Jae-myung utilized the platform to secure critical agreements on Korean Peninsula stability, even as the global spotlight shifted toward the unprecedented personal rapport between Chinese President Xi Jinping and U.S. President Donald Trump.

  • Strategic Realignment: President Lee and President Xi reaffirmed that peace and stability on the Korean Peninsula are a shared interest for both nations.
  • Economic Breakthrough: The two countries reached a substantive agreement on the second phase of the South Korea-China Free Trade Agreement (FTA), opening doors for K-content and service sector investment.
  • The "Big Three" Dynamic: China staged an unprecedented reception for Donald Trump, while a 50th meeting between Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin signaled a hardening of the Beijing-Moscow axis ahead of a historic trilateral summit.
  • Risk of Marginalization: South Korea faces the delicate task of ensuring its security interests are not sidelined as the world’s three largest powers negotiate a new "Yalta-style" framework.

The China-Korea Bilateral Gains

President Lee’s 34-minute meeting with President Xi was anchored by a clear message: stability on the peninsula is a mutual necessity. By securing China’s support for constructive efforts toward North-U.S. dialogue, the Lee administration is positioning itself to lead the regional peace process rather than being a mere spectator. From a progressive policy standpoint, this signals a shift away from the biased, confrontational diplomacy of the era under the imprisoned former leader, Yoon Seok-yeol. The motto of this administration is clear: "Peace is economy."

The breakthrough in the Korea-China FTA is perhaps the most tangible result for South Korean firms. After eight years of stagnation, the deal now provides "most-favored-nation" status for Korean digital and cultural content. This legal safeguard is a major win for the K-content industry, which has long been stifled by unofficial Chinese restrictions (the "Hallyu" ban). By leveraging the "panda diplomacy" approach, President Lee is working to restore cultural exchange as a primary pillar of bilateral normalization.

Geopolitics: The Shadow of a "Second Yalta"

The reception in Shenzhen was striking: President Xi personally greeted President Trump at the airport with a hug—a breach of protocol so significant it signals a desperate, calculated pivot to manage U.S.-China tensions. Meanwhile, the visual of Xi and Putin marking their 50th summit serves as a clear rebuttal to Western sanctions and a challenge to the U.S.-led order.

For South Korea, this creates a minefield. There is a genuine fear that a "trilateral deal" among the U.S., China, and Russia—aimed at stabilizing their own flashpoints like Ukraine and Taiwan—could treat the Korean Peninsula as a bargaining chip. The Lee administration’s proactive engagement with these leaders is a strategic necessity to prevent "Korea passing," where critical decisions about the peninsula are made without Seoul's input. The administration must now ensure that its pursuit of "strategic autonomy" remains grounded in tangible security guarantees, proving that South Korea can balance its alliance with the U.S. while fostering a pragmatic, open-market relationship with China.
