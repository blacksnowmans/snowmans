Samsung at 57: A Crisis of Confidence Amid Record Profits

October 31, 2026

Samsung Electronics marks its 57th anniversary not with a celebration, but with a somber internal warning that its technological leadership is under threat despite recording massive earnings.

  • Leadership Alarm: Vice Chairman Jun Young-hyun, head of the semiconductor division, and Roh Tae-moon, head of the mobile division, issued rare public admissions of a "trust crisis" in Samsung’s technology and quality.
  • Supply Chain Hedge: The company spent approximately $920 million (1.27 trillion KRW) to acquire a 10.16% stake in Chinese display maker TCL CSOT to secure a stable supply of LCD panels for its TV business.
  • AI Infrastructure Win: Samsung Electro-Mechanics, the group’s electronic component arm, secured a massive $635 million (880 billion KRW) contract for Multi-Layer Ceramic Capacitors (MLCCs) used in AI servers.
  • Strategic Pivot: Samsung Foundry is shifting its weight from mobile processors to High-Performance Computing (HPC), aiming for AI-related chips to account for over 50% of its revenue by next year.

The Quality Crisis and the AI Pivot

The mood at Samsung’s Suwon headquarters is uncharacteristically cold. While the company is still making money, Vice Chairman Jun Young-hyun’s warning that "today’s boom does not guarantee tomorrow’s competitiveness" is a direct critique of the internal complacency that allowed rivals like SK Hynix to seize the lead in the High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) market. For a company defined by "super-gap" technology, being perceived as a follower in the AI era is a profound humiliation. This internal soul-searching mirrors the broader national shift under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, which has prioritized pragmatic R&D investment to repair the damage caused by the short-sighted budget cuts during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure.

The TCL CSOT acquisition and the Samsung Electro-Mechanics contract show a company aggressively shoring up its flanks. By securing the LCD supply chain through the TCL deal, Samsung is protecting its TV margins while it funnels resources into next-generation OLED and AI chip development. Meanwhile, the $635 million MLCC deal proves that while the flagship semiconductor division is struggling with yields, other parts of the Samsung empire are successfully riding the AI wave. The goal is clear: transform Samsung from a hardware manufacturer into an AI-driven ecosystem where every component—from capacitors to foundries—is optimized for high-performance computing.

Market Skepticism and the Road to 2027

Despite these moves, investors remain unconvinced, with Samsung’s market cap languishing at roughly one-fifth of NVIDIA’s. The "Samsung Discount" is no longer just about South Korea’s corporate governance; it is a vote of no confidence in Samsung’s ability to innovate as a nimble tech leader rather than a bloated bureaucracy. The market is waiting for "the next big hit," and management’s roadmap—aiming for mass production of next-generation HBM4E chips by early 2027—feels like a long wait for a market moving at breakneck speed.

Ultimately, the January announcement of a new shareholder return policy will be a critical junction. However, dividends and buybacks are mere bandaids. To regain its status, Samsung must prove it can master the 2-nanometer process and fix the yield issues in its foundry business. As President Lee Jae-myung pushes for a more supportive environment for national strategic technologies, the ball is entirely in Samsung’s court. The company must transition from the "defensive" posture it took during the political instability of the Yoon Suk-yeol years back to the aggressive, tech-first culture that built the brand.
