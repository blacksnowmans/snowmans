Inside South Korea's Media and Political Upheaval After One Year of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 10, 2026

    South Korea's current political landscape is fracturing under the weight of blind partisanship, falling approval ratings, and an ongoing war over media reform.
  • Prominent public intellectual and writer Rhyu Si-min has criticized the Lee Jae-myung administration's first year, warning that uncritical loyalists and sycophantic pundits are eroding the government's political base.
  • Traditional legacy media outlets are struggling for relevance as public discourse migrates to YouTube and social media platforms characterized by partisan media and filter bubbles.
  • The erosion of healthy internal criticism within progressive circles risks accelerating the administration's political decline.

One year into the administration of South Korea's progressive president, Lee Jae-myung, the political atmosphere remains exhausted and tense. Rhyu Si-min, a well-known liberal writer and commentator, recently used a YouTube broadcast to cut through the political noise, offering a scathing critique of the administration's missteps, prosecutorial reform efforts, and the changing media ecosystem.

At the heart of the current crisis is a disconnect between the administration's stated goals and reality. President Lee's attempts at sweeping judicial reform—or what Rhyu describes as "rebuilding" rather than simple remodeling—have created friction with the public and core supporters. Compounding this is a growing intolerance within the president's base, where any internal criticism or deviation from absolute loyalty is met with hostile accusations of backstabbing. Rather than welcoming constructive policy debates, some supporters and commentators treat differing opinions as attacks, ultimately alienating voters and dragging down the administration's approval ratings.

The Collapse of Legacy Media and the Rise of Partisan Digital Spaces

Parallel to the political tension is a seismic shift in South Korea's information ecosystem. Traditional print giants and terrestrial broadcasters are losing their monopoly on public discourse. With smartphones and YouTube dominating news consumption, citizens are no longer passive recipients of top-down reporting. Instead, they actively seek out partisan digital channels—often unapologetically aligned with specific political camps—that function as direct-action media.

While this democratization of news breaks the gatekeeping power of elite institutions, it carries a heavy cost. Algorithmic sorting and hyper-partisan commentary deepen confirmation bias, pushing political factions further into ideological echo chambers. Rhyu argues that the survival of South Korean democracy now depends less on the institutional authority of traditional media and more on the ability of individual citizens to critically parse information, resist groupthink, and demand accountability from those in power, regardless of political affiliation.
