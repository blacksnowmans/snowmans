The October 7 AI Brief: From Anthropic’s Surge to $54 Billion Chip Bets

October 08, 2026

The AI landscape is shifting from a blind race for performance to a focus on cost-efficiency, security, and real-world utility as South Korea deepens its integration with generative models.

  • Anthropic’s Rise: Claude has become the second most popular AI app in South Korea, with usage surging 13-fold over the past year, as companies like Google integrate the model directly into workplace tools.
  • Capital Intensity: SpaceX is reportedly seeking $54 billion (about 75 trillion won) for AI chip procurement, signaling a new era of "debt-fueled" hardware acquisition that mirrors the massive capital expenditures seen across the industry.
  • Security Paradox: As convenience increases, so does risk; reports indicate that ChatGPT accounts are being traded on the dark web for as little as $0.10 (140 won), while major systems continue to face vulnerabilities.
  • Local Policy: South Korean local governments are stepping in, with places like Jincheon County subsidizing AI subscription fees for youth to bridge the digital divide.

The Shift Toward Utility and Efficiency

The era of "bigger is better" for Large Language Models (LLMs) is hitting a wall. Microsoft and other tech giants are reportedly pivotting away from raw performance specs toward cost-efficiency. This mirrors the global trend where businesses are moving from experimental AI use to finding tangible ROI. In Korea, this is visible in finance, where BC Card has launched a ChatGPT-powered "personal shopper," and Toss Bank has utilized AI agents to slash card-related customer service wait times from five minutes to 30 seconds.

However, this rapid integration brings significant risks. The misuse of AI, such as relying on chatbots for investment advice—which recently led to high-profile financial losses—highlights a growing "security amnesia." As AI becomes an automated decision-maker in education, public administration, and even personal finance, the burden of verifying these outputs remains firmly with the human user.

Infrastructure and Sovereignty

The battle for dominance is moving from software to physical infrastructure. AMD CEO Lisa Su’s recent visit to Seoul, where she met with 14 local AI semiconductor firms, underscores the industry's attempt to crack Nvidia’s near-monopoly. Simultaneously, the focus on "open-weight" models like France’s Mistral Large 4 suggests a move toward sovereignty—allowing companies to run high-performance AI on their own servers rather than relying on US-based cloud giants.

In South Korea, the government and municipal bodies are racing to build "Physical AI" infrastructure, such as the new testing center in Incheon. Yet, the rapid adoption of AI in industrial robotics and logistics raises a critical alarm: if these systems are not rigorously protected, a software vulnerability could result in a physical disaster. As we move further into an AI-integrated society, the challenge is no longer just about building the smartest tool, but ensuring it doesn't become the biggest liability.
