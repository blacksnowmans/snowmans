Cash is King: Why Seoul is Weathering the AI Investment Storm

November 02, 2026

South Korea’s financial markets are proving resilient against global AI "bubble" fears by shifting focus from growth narratives to tangible cash flow and aggressive shareholder return policies.

  • Market Resilience: The KOSPI, South Korea’s benchmark stock index, successfully defended the 2,870 level despite a tech rout in the U.S., supported by institutional buying and a rotation into undervalued domestic sectors.
  • Big Tech’s Cash Crunch: Free cash flow (FCF) among global tech giants has plummeted, with the top five firms seeing shareholder return ratios hit a 15-year low of 9.3% as AI infrastructure costs soar.
  • Altman’s Quiet Visit: OpenAI CEO Sam Altman’s low-profile visit to Seoul suggests a strategic pivot toward securing capital from cash-rich Korean giants like Samsung and SK Hynix as AI startups face a tightening credit market.
  • Fiscal Warnings: While the administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party propose a 162 trillion KRW (about $118 billion) "Future Response Fund," experts warn of a "tax cliff" looming by 2029.

The End of AI Romanticism

The global market is undergoing a cold reality check. The era where investors cheered for any mention of data centers and GPU clusters is being replaced by a "show me the money" mandate. According to recent data, the combined free cash flow of the five largest tech firms—including Alphabet and Amazon—swung from a $143.5 billion surplus last year to a $14 billion deficit this year. As capital expenditure (CapEx) doubled, these firms are no longer the cash machines they once were, forcing them to scale back the stock buybacks that previously propped up their share prices.

This liquidity squeeze is even more acute for startups. Anthropic, a major rival to OpenAI, recently received a "Junk" (BB) credit rating during its latest funding round. This isn't just a blow to prestige; it effectively bars major pension funds and institutional investors from participating, as their internal mandates usually prohibit investing in sub-investment grade assets. In this high-interest-rate environment, the "burn rate" of AI development is finally meeting the wall of fiscal discipline.

Seoul’s Hardware Giants as the New Creditors

This shift has unexpectedly flipped the script on the relationship between Silicon Valley and Seoul. Previously, Big Tech "hyperscalers" dictated terms to hardware suppliers. However, with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (the world’s leading memory chip makers) sitting on massive cash reserves, the power dynamic is shifting. Sam Altman’s recent trip to Korea—where he bypassed the typical media fanfare to hold private meetings with chip executives—indicates that AI leaders are now seeking more than just chips; they are looking for stable, cash-rich partners to help fund the next generation of infrastructure.

Domestically, the "Korea Discount"—the historical undervaluation of Korean stocks—is being challenged by a new focus on shareholder value. While Samsung Electronics was criticized for its vague stance on returning capital, companies like Samsung E&A (the group’s engineering arm) saw stock prices surge after announcing aggressive share cancellations. For the current administration, the challenge lies in balancing this corporate momentum with long-term fiscal health. While the 162 trillion KRW ($118 billion) fund aimed at AI and semiconductors is ambitious, the warning that additional tax revenue could hit zero by 2029 suggests that the current boom must be managed with extreme caution.

"In a world where even the most advanced AI startups are facing credit downgrades, a company’s most valuable asset isn't its algorithm—it’s the cash on its balance sheet."
