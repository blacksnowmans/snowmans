European Far-Right Advances Collide With Internal Splits and Extremist Threats

November 20, 2026

Far-right parties solidified their institutional reach across Europe on November 19, highlighted by Germany’s Alternative for Germany (AfD) topping a state election and reshaping political dynamics across the continent, according to major outlets including DD News, Bloomberg, and Politico Europe.

  • German and French shakeups: DD News reported that the AfD placed first in a German state vote, delivering what Christian Democratic Union leader Friedrich Merz’s camp called its worst post-war state result, while Mezzanet noted Marine Le Pen of the far-right National Rally and far-left rival Jean-Luc Mélenchon lead polling for France’s presidential runoff.
  • UK anti-immigration push: Reform UK released figures claiming foreign recipients of Britain’s Universal Credit welfare program hit a record 1.26 million, while party leader Nigel Farage returned to Brussels to oppose closer UK-EU alignment, according to Politico Europe and Mezzanet.
  • Extremism and party friction: German authorities detained a far-right suspect over an alleged mosque attack plot (Anadolu Agency), the Dutch Forum for Democracy expelled members tied to a neo-Nazi fight event (NL Times), and AfD politicians publicly criticized Le Pen for diluting her party’s identity (Yahoo).
  • Global political and diplomatic strain: Middle East Eye and Deccan Chronicle reported a mosque arson in Israel that opposition figures blamed on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s rhetoric, while in East Asia, South Korean outlets SBS News, Hankook Ilbo, and Choibosik’s Media covered Chinese diplomatic friction with Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, a paid ministerial lobbying controversy in Tokyo, and Japan’s entry denial of South Korean politician and former party leader Cho Kuk.

Electoral Momentum vs. Fragmented Alliances

The latest polling and election results suggest that populist platforms centered on migration and economic grievances are steadily displacing traditional centrist coalitions. South Korean daily Chosun Ilbo noted that leading French presidential contenders have pledged to lower the retirement age to 60, illustrating how economic pressures and voter dissatisfaction are sharpening competition at both ends of the political spectrum. These shifts are already forcing strategic adjustments beyond national borders, with Defense24 reporting that Berlin has moved to safeguard Ukraine aid in case Le Pen wins the French presidency.

Yet electoral gains have not translated into a cohesive nationalist movement. Public disputes between German AfD figures and Le Pen’s camp, alongside British Conservatives distancing themselves from the AfD in The Telegraph and domestic ridicule over Reform UK’s AI-generated video stunts, underscore the difficulty these parties face in building cross-border alliances or managing internal discipline. Simultaneously, ongoing security alerts in Norway, a neo-Nazi leader’s death during raids in Spain reported by Searchlight Magazine, and far-right criminal probes in Bulgaria show that fringe violence continues to complicate efforts by institutional far-right parties to normalize their image.

Viewed from East Asia, Europe’s turbulence reflects a wider pattern of strained diplomatic and political norms, even where the underlying disputes differ. Whether it is tech billionaire Elon Musk questioning his $442 million in US midterm spending (Harici) or Tokyo managing chilly ties with Beijing alongside domestic scandals and cross-border friction with Seoul, established institutions across regions are facing heightened volatility.
