AI and the End of Work: Why South Korea is Looking Back 300 Years for Answers

October 27, 2026

South Korea is re-evaluating the AI revolution not as a unique technological threat, but as the latest chapter in a 300-year history of labor displacement that requires a total overhaul of social and political institutions.

  • Historical Recurrence: Intellectuals are drawing parallels to the 19th-century Luddite movement, arguing that human suffering during transitions is caused by a lack of institutional safeguards, not the machines themselves.
  • Economic Vulnerability: The Bank of Korea, the nation's central bank, estimates that 3.41 million jobs—roughly 12% of the domestic workforce—are at high risk of being replaced by AI automation.
  • The Rise of Tech Power: There is growing alarm over "unelected tech powers" in Silicon Valley who dictate global AI development without democratic oversight or social accountability.
  • Political Mismatch: Critics argue that the traditional "nation-state" model is too small and outdated to handle global challenges like AI displacement and climate change.

The Machine vs. The Social Contract

The current anxiety surrounding AI in South Korea is being framed through the lens of historical struggle. Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal intellectual and former government minister, argues that the fear of AI is a echoes the cries of 19th-century British weavers. During the Industrial Revolution, the tragedy wasn't the steam engine; it was a system that forced seven-year-olds into factories and ignored the plight of the displaced. The lesson for the current era is that technology is inherently designed by capital to reduce labor costs. Without new laws—similar to the historical bans on child labor or the implementation of the eight-hour workday—AI will inevitably lead to a "cruelty of work."

This shift is already visible in the professional sectors. While previous automation targeted manual labor, AI is now moving for high-skilled, high-paying roles. Globally, firms like Goldman Sachs estimate that 300 million full-time jobs could be exposed to automation. In Korea, where roughly 2,000 workers die annually from industrial accidents, there is a moral argument for using humanoid robots to replace dangerous labor. However, the market reality is different: companies are more interested in using AI to cut entry-level white-collar recruitment than in saving workers from hazardous shipyards or construction sites.

The Crisis of "Small" Politics

The most profound challenge identified in recent Korean discourse is the obsolescence of the nation-state. Just as the ancient Greek city-states collapsed because their politics couldn't keep up with their economic integration during the Peloponnesian War, modern countries are struggling to regulate global tech giants. We are facing "Earth-sized" problems—AI sovereignty and climate collapse—while using "neighborhood-sized" political tools. This creates a dangerous vacuum where unelected tech elites, driven by subjective ideologies like "Longtermism," make decisions that affect the entire human species.

In this landscape, the Democratic Party, South Korea's current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, faces the difficult task of balancing domestic stability with these borderless shifts. While the government remains focused on localized issues like the falling birth rate, the broader warning is that "national" survival is a narrow view. If the global climate or the global labor market collapses, the survival of any single nation becomes moot. For the individual, the only survival strategy is to maintain "intellectual agency"—using AI as a tool without outsourcing the actual process of thinking and learning, which remains the core of human growth.
