South Korea’s "Sovereign Citizens" Redefine Grassroots Political Participation

November 02, 2026

A recent two-day political retreat hosted by a prominent pro-government media outlet has highlighted a significant shift in South Korean political culture, where supporters are moving beyond passive "fandom" to assert themselves as active, self-directed "sovereign" participants.

  • High-Engagement Activism: Supporters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s ruling party, gathered for an overnight workshop to engage in deep policy debates and strategy sessions.
  • Rejecting Fandom: Participants explicitly rejected being "dependent variables" of party leadership, instead declaring themselves "sovereign citizens" who hold the mandate to direct the government.
  • Horizontal Communication: High-ranking officials, including Representative Jung Chung-rae, a senior member of the DPK leadership, attended the event to participate in horizontal, unscripted Q&A sessions with the public.
  • Psychological Solidarity: The gathering acted as a remedy for "political depression," a term used in Korea to describe the burnout from hyper-polarized news cycles, by reviving the grassroots community spirit of the early 2000s.

From Fandom to Sovereignty

The retreat, organized by Park Si-young TV (a popular political YouTube channel run by strategist Park Si-young), signals an evolution in how the Korean public interacts with power. Historically, political supporters in Korea were often viewed as "fans" to be mobilized for rallies or elections. However, the discourse at this event suggests that the base of the Lee Jae-myung administration now views itself as the "employer" of the politicians they support. One participant’s declaration—"We are not dependent variables; we are the sovereigns"—resonated deeply, framing the party leadership not as idols, but as agents of the people's will.

This shift is a direct legacy of the massive candlelight vigils that eventually led to the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Having successfully removed a leader through collective action, these citizens are no longer content with being "managed" by party elites. For the DPK, this presents both an opportunity and a challenge: they possess a highly motivated "human shield" against the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, but they are also under constant, intense scrutiny from their own most dedicated supporters to deliver on reform promises.

The Return of Offline Solidarity

The event also highlighted a growing demand for face-to-face political community in an increasingly digital world. Participants, many of whom are in their 40s and 50s, reported that meeting in person helped them overcome the helplessness often felt when consuming news alone. This "offline power" is reminiscent of the Rohsamo movement (supporters of the late President Roh Moo-hyun), which was the first to harness the internet for political change in the early 2000s. By moving from the comment section to the seminar room, this group is institutionalizing grassroots power.

As the Lee Jae-myung government pushes its legislative agenda, this "sovereign citizen" model is likely to expand. The success of this retreat has already led to calls for similar regional and international events. For observers of Korean politics, the takeaway is clear: the DPK’s base has become more educated, more demanding, and more organized than ever before. They are no longer just voters; they are a permanent, active component of the governing process.
