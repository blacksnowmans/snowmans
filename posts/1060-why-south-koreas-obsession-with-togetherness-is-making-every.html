Why South Korea's Obsession with Togetherness is Making Everyone Miserable

October 13, 2026

  • What happened: South Korean writer and public intellectual Rhyu Si-min appeared on a local talk show to discuss the psychological weight of loneliness, arguing that society's demand for constant socialization is an unnatural burden.
  • What it changes: It challenges the deep-seated cultural pressure in South Korea to conform, marry, and maintain large social circles, reframing voluntary solitude as a form of personal liberation.
  • Who is affected: Young South Koreans grappling with intense societal expectations, career burnout, and the world's lowest birth rates.

South Korea's relentless cultural script insists that a happy life is a crowded one. From the moment children enter school until they retire, social conformity is treated as a baseline survival skill. But as Rhyu Si-min pointed out in a recent interview, this frantic push for constant connection ignores a fundamental truth: human beings are built to crave quiet, and forced socialization is often just a recipe for exhaustion. Society treats loneliness like a disease to be cured, but the real affliction is the paralyzing anxiety of trying to live up to everyone else's expectations.

This anxiety is deeply rooted in human history. For hundreds of thousands of years, early humans relied entirely on tight-knit tribes to stay alive; being cast out meant certain death. Modern South Koreans still carry that biological alarm system in their DNA, which partly explains why someone who chooses to live or spend time alone is often viewed with immediate suspicion—as a social failure rather than someone making a free choice. When a society treats solitude as exile, it turns normal psychological needs into sources of deep shame.

Ultimately, breaking free from this tribal programming is the only way to survive the crushing demands of modern life. Rhyu rightly dismissed the panic over South Korea's plunging birth rates, noting that the obsession with national survival completely ignores the misery of the individuals expected to sacrifice their lives for the collective. Instead of lecturing young people about patriotism and family duty while ignoring structural poverty and burnout, society should let individuals figure out their own balance of work, play, love, and quiet.
