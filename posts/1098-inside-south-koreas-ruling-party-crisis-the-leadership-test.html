Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Crisis: The Leadership Test Facing Kim Min-seok

October 14, 2026

  • South Korea's ruling Democratic Party, led under President Lee Jae-myung, has seen its approval rating slide to 35%—its lowest point since the current government took power, according to Gallup Korea.
  • Party Chairman Kim Min-seok faces mounting internal dissent and accusations that his combative rhetoric and closed-door factional politics are deepening party divisions rather than fostering unity.
  • Veteran lawmaker Park Jie-won has been embroiled in an illegal fish farm licensing scandal in the Jeolla region, threatening to trigger a broader power struggle within the party's traditional stronghold.
  • Critics argue that the leadership's reliance on echo-chamber media broadcasts and private meetings with lawmakers is alienating the public and eroding trust in the party's reform agenda.

Echo Chambers and Closed-Door Politics

South Korea's political landscape often produces bizarre spectacles, but few match the current strategy of the ruling Democratic Party's leadership. Chairman Kim Min-seok—a prominent politician boasting a Seoul National University background and a U.S. law license—has increasingly retreated to a party-run broadcast channel rather than facing tough questions from independent media. Critics compare this to someone watching a few online cooking videos and attempting a high-end steak, only to burn it repeatedly. By broadcasting to a tiny base of loyalists while dodging critical inquiries, the leadership risks insulating itself entirely from mainstream public sentiment.

This insular approach extends to internal party management. While publicly preaching unity under a sweeping banner honoring past progressive presidents, the leadership has reportedly relied on closed-door lunches with first-term lawmakers to exert pressure over future candidate nominations. Observers note that attempting to manage internal dissent through behind-the-scenes leverage, rather than transparent dialogue, harkens back to older, transactional styles of Korean politics that sit uneasily with voters demanding reform.

The Jeolla Stronghold and Factional Turmoil

As the party's broader approval numbers drop, the leadership has turned its focus toward shoring up its traditional political base in the Jeolla provinces (Honam). However, this consolidation effort has collided with existing local power structures and regional old-guard networks. Tensions have intensified following a high-profile scandal involving Park Jie-won and allegations that an illegal fish-farming operation in the southwestern coastal region traded on ties with Park's former staff. With investigators probing bribery ledgers and meeting schedules, the fallout threatens to become a major liability for the ruling bloc as different factions vie for regional hegemony.

The core challenge for Chairman Kim and his inner circle is whether they can transition from insular messaging to genuine accountability. If the party continues to dismiss plunging approval ratings as merely a PR problem or the fault of hostile media, the looming internal fractures could transform a temporary dip in the polls into a serious threat to the government's stability.
