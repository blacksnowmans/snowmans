South Korea’s $595 Billion Budget War Begins as Parliamentary Audits Close

October 31, 2026

Following the conclusion of the annual parliamentary audits, South Korea’s political parties have immediately pivoted to a high-stakes battle over a record 821 trillion won (approximately $595 billion) national budget for the coming year.

  • The Budget Clash: The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has labeled President Lee Jae-myung’s expansionary fiscal policy as "pure populism" and pledged to cut spending by at least 134 trillion won ($97 billion).
  • Conservative Realignment: PPP leader Han Dong-hoon is signaling a "principled alliance" with influential figures like Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon to consolidate the opposition against the Democratic Party’s dominance.
  • Local Autonomy: Regional leaders within the PPP are demanding the passage of a "Local Council Act" to grant local legislatures financial and administrative independence from the executive branch.
  • Ideological Friction: Ahn Chang-ho, head of the National Human Rights Commission, has reignited a fierce historical debate by suggesting North Korean involvement in the Jeju April 3 Incident, a state-recognized tragedy.

A Clash of Fiscal Philosophies

The transition from parliamentary audits to budget season has been seamless and hostile. The PPP is framing the 821 trillion won budget as a reckless burden on future generations, specifically targeting the administration's "Future Response Fund" and other large-scale social spending projects. For the ruling Democratic Party, this spending is the primary engine for economic recovery and social welfare under President Lee’s "expansionary finance" doctrine. With the opposition threatening to veto nearly 15% of the total budget, the National Assembly is bracing for a deadlock that could paralyze government operations.

This budget war is not merely about accounting; it is an ideological proxy battle. The PPP is leveraging fiscal conservatism to appeal to its base, accusing the Lee Jae-myung administration of breaking "total budget principles" to fund populist promises. Meanwhile, the ruling party views these cuts as an attempt to sabotage the government’s core agenda. Given that the audits ended with shouting matches over witness attendance—specifically regarding figures close to the President—the budget deliberations are unlikely to see much bipartisan cooperation.

Leadership Shifts and Historical Revisionism

On the opposition side, Han Dong-hoon is attempting to redefine the PPP’s identity. By meeting with Mayor Oh Se-hoon and hinting at a broader coalition that includes moderate conservatives, Han is positioning himself as a leader who prioritizes "shared principles" over the factional infighting that characterized the party during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. This realignment aims to build a "Big Tent" capable of challenging the Democratic Party’s legislative supermajority, though internal skeptics remain wary of political horse-trading with splinter groups.

Finally, the comments by Ahn Chang-ho regarding the Jeju April 3 Incident—an uprising and subsequent massacre that occurred between 1948 and 1954—have caused a significant political backlash. By echoing far-right claims of North Korean instigation, Ahn has alienated victims' families and progressive groups. In an era where the Lee Jae-myung administration has prioritized "correcting history," having the head of a major human rights body challenge the established historical narrative is a major liability that the opposition may use to highlight the government's controversial appointments.
