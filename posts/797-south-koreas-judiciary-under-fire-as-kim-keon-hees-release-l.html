South Korea’s Judiciary Under Fire as Kim Keon-hee’s Release Looms

October 07, 2026

  • Imminent Release:
    •  Kim Keon-hee, facing charges including stock manipulation and capital markets violations, faces the expiration of her detention period on October 31, raising alarms about accountability.

  • Political Distraction:

  • Public Frustration:
    •  The spectacle underscores growing public cynicism over whether South Korea’s legal system applies equally to powerful figures.

The Supreme Court's Silence on Kim Keon-hee

South Korea’s parliamentary audit sessions have descended into chaotic political theater, but the gravest concern lies with the judiciary's handling of high-profile cases. At the center of the storm is Kim Keon-hee, whose detention period expires on October 31. Major charges—including violations of the Capital Markets Act and stock manipulation linked to Deutsch Motors—sit pending before the Supreme Court's full bench. Yet, when opposition lawmakers pressed officials on whether a ruling would be handed down before her release, the Supreme Court's National Court Administration offered evasive, procedural non-answers.

This calculated passivity has fueled public outrage. Critics argue that the judiciary is dragging its feet to avoid a politically explosive decision, effectively using procedural delays to shield a major political figure from accountability. When the state's highest court hides behind technicalities while a high-profile defendant walks free on a technical expiration of detention, the foundational principle of equal justice under the law fractures.

Ideological Warfare Over Culture

Rather than addressing these pressing questions of judicial delay, conservative lawmakers used the audit as an ideological battleground. Joo Jin-woo of the People Power Party—the main conservative opposition—targeted the Supreme Court Chief Justice over state-backed investments totaling about $30 million (40.4 billion won) into a historical film touching on the assassination of former authoritarian leader Park Chung-hee. Labeling the funding a subversive distortion of history, the opposition attempted to paint the judiciary and state financial institutions as complicit in threatening the constitutional order.

The distraction peaked when it was revealed that lawmakers pushing these conspiracy theories had not even watched the film in question. This episode encapsulates the broader fatigue felt by citizens navigating economic hardship while politicians manufacture cultural grievances. By trading substantive oversight for partisan noise, lawmakers are not just wasting public time—they are actively eroding trust in the democratic institutions meant to serve the public interest.
