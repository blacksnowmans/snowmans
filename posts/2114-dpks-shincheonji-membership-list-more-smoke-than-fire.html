DPK’s Shincheonji Membership List: More Smoke Than Fire?

November 08, 2026

An internal investigation by the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation’s governing party, into alleged infiltration by a controversial religious group has failed to produce concrete evidence of organized political interference.

  • Low Validity: While rumors suggested up to 50,000 members of Shincheonji—a secretive religious group often labeled a cult—had joined the DPK, internal audits found only about 3,000 "meaningful" entries.
  • Subjective Evidence: Much of the "evidence" provided by party whistleblowers relied on subjective impressions, such as individuals "having strange eyes" or wearing specific colors, rather than documented activity.
  • Legal Risks: The DPK faces potential blowback over the source of the list; if it was leaked from confidential prosecution files gathered during the COVID-19 pandemic, the party could face charges for violating personal information laws.
  • Strategic Retreat: DPK leadership, including Representative Kim Min-seok, has opted to keep the list private and has apologized for the lack of concrete proof, seeking to prevent a "witch hunt" within the party.

The Gap Between Rumor and Reality

The controversy centered on fears that Shincheonji was systematically planting members within the DPK to influence party primaries. However, the numbers do not support a "coup" narrative. In a party where over 700,000 "dues-paying members" (qualified voters) participated in the last convention, 3,000 individuals represent a statistical drop in the bucket. The investigation itself was also criticized for being "security theater"; lawmakers were reportedly given only 30 minutes to scan hundreds of pages, making a thorough audit impossible.

The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main opposition party, has seized on the confusion to attack the government's transparency. By allowing the list to become a public talking point before verifying the facts, the DPK created a vacuum filled by conspiracy theories. This included fringe claims linking graphic design similarities in the Mapo-gu district logo to the religious group—a claim that even targeted prominent party figures like former lawmaker Son Hye-won.

A Poisoned Chalice for the Lee Administration

For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, this list is a "poisoned chalice." Using it to purge members carries immense legal risk. Under South Korean law, using investigative data for non-investigative purposes is a serious crime. During the COVID-19 pandemic, when Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (currently imprisoned following his impeachment) was in power, the prosecution gathered extensive data on Shincheonji. If the DPK obtained this list through unofficial channels, it could lead to charges of leaking official secrets.

Ultimately, the incident reveals a lack of sophisticated data management within the governing party. Instead of relying on rigorous internal vetting, the party was swayed by subjective "vibe checks" and unverified leaks. Moving forward, the DPK must balance the need for party integrity with the legal rights of its members, or risk handing the opposition a potent weapon for the next election cycle. For now, the leadership's decision to bury the list is a pragmatic, if embarrassing, admission that the "infiltration" was largely a phantom threat.
