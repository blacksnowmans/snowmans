South Korea’s Ruling Party Pushes to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae as Political War Escalates

November 24, 2026

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea is accelerating plans to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae by the end of the year, triggering an intense constitutional standoff with opposition parties.

  • What happened: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP) announced it will finalize an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae within the week, aiming to pass it in the National Assembly before year-end.
  • The opposition reaction: The main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) and the minor opposition Reform Party condemned the motion as a "bulletproof impeachment" designed to shield President Lee Jae-myung from his stalled court trials, demanding those judicial proceedings resume immediately.
  • Unprecedented pressure: PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok called on judges nationwide to actively resist DP pressure or resign en masse, while threatening to file charges against individual judges for "law distortion"—a tactic criticized as "bizarre" even by conservative Daegu Mayor Hong Joon-pyo.
  • Who is affected: The judiciary’s independence is hanging by a thread as both sides weaponize legal procedures, deepening political polarization and distracting from urgent economic issues.

A High-Stakes Battle Over Judicial Neutrality

The Democratic Party frames its impeachment drive against Chief Justice Cho as a "historical calling" to restore fairness and purge alleged political bias from the judicial branch. However, attempting to unseat the head of the Supreme Court is an extraordinary step with heavy constitutional consequences. By framing Chief Justice Cho's tenure as an existential threat to democratic values, the ruling party is using its legislative majority to signal that the judiciary is not beyond parliamentary control.

Politics Consumes the Rule of Law

The opposition’s response is equally aggressive. By encouraging sitting judges to engage in collective resistance and threatening criminal complaints against individual judges like Judge Lee Jin-gwan, the People Power Party is dragging the bench directly into party politics. When judges are publicly pressured to align with political factions, the boundary between law and partisan combat dissolves entirely.

This escalation leaves little room for governance or legislative progress on South Korea's pressing economic challenges. As both sides exchange rhetoric accusing each other of subverting the constitution, South Korea’s legal system is increasingly being treated not as an impartial referee, but as a weapon for political survival.
