Grassroots Rebellion: South Korea’s Ruling Party Sued Over Disputed Leadership Election

November 06, 2026

Dues-paying members of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) have taken their own party leadership to court, filing an injunction to suspend Chairman Kim Min-seok and nullify his election victory.

  • The legal challenge: A collective lawsuit led by attorney Lee Jae-il seeks to strip Chairman Kim Min-seok of his post, alleging party officials hushed up evidence that members of Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious sect—infiltrated the leadership primary.
  • The core clash: Dissident members accuse Kim of weaponizing the scandal to stall internal reforms, specifically the "one person, one vote" system favored by 85.3% of dues-paying party members to equalize voting power between ordinary members and party delegates.
  • The escalation: Organizers are mobilizing between 1,000 and 5,000 party co-plaintiffs—each chipping in 33,000 won (about $24) to fund litigation all the way to the Supreme Court—alongside planned street rallies in Seoul’s political hub of Yeouido.
  • The fallout: The courtroom feud reflects a wider breach between the DPK establishment and core progressives, who also voice mounting frustration over President Lee Jae-myung’s policy shifts and communication strategy.

Sect Scandals and Voting Rights

The immediate spark for the litigation is the leadership’s refusal to release findings from an internal task force investigating allegations that Shincheonji followers joined the party en masse to swing the leadership race. Instead of releasing the data, Chairman Kim dismissed the allegations as baseless and argued that public disclosure would trigger greater harm. Party dissidents, backed by several reform-minded lawmakers, argue the secrecy masks either shoddy vetting or outright complicity with party insiders.

More alarming to the party base is Kim’s signal that the party requires "institutional improvements" to prevent external infiltration. Rank-and-file activists view this as code for reversing direct democracy reforms. Under the traditional system, votes from handpicked delegates carried up to twenty times the weight of regular dues-paying members. Activists fear the leadership is cynically exploiting the Shincheonji scare to preserve entrenched establishment control, prompting additional legal action against figures like lawmaker Kim Nam-hee over alleged unauthorized leaks of party voter registries.

Growing Friction with the Lee Administration

This internal revolt coincides with an increasingly uneasy relationship between traditional progressive loyalists and President Lee Jae-myung. Facing approval ratings that dipped into the low 30s, President Lee launched a dedicated YouTube program titled "The President's Y"—hosted by former MBC anchor Sohn Jeong-eun—to bypass mainstream media algorithms and directly explain state affairs. Yet core loyalists argue the broadcast reflects an administration insulated inside an echo chamber, quick to dismiss constructive internal criticism as factional hostility.

Compounding this alienation is the administration's broader strategic pivot. Rather than prioritizing core progressive demands such as aggressive prosecutorial reform, the presidential office has courted younger voters, celebrating a recent 12.9-point rebound among voters in their twenties. When combined with contentious security moves—such as the cabinet-approved operational expansion of the anti-piracy Cheonghae naval unit to the tense Strait of Hormuz—the base increasingly feels sidelined. The lawsuit against Kim Min-seok is more than a procedural skirmish; it is an open revolt against an establishment taking its most devoted foot soldiers for granted.
