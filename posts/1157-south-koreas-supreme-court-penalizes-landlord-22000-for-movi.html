South Korea’s Supreme Court Penalizes Landlord $22,000 for Moving Out Early

October 14, 2026

South Korea's Supreme Court has ordered a landlord to pay 30 million won (about $22,000) in damages to a former tenant after vacating the property before fulfilling a statutory two-year personal occupancy requirement, establishing a strict precedent that could hasten the demise of the country's traditional rental system.

  • The Ruling: Landlords who refuse a tenant’s contract renewal by claiming personal occupancy must live in the home for at least 24 months; leaving after 14 months—even due to a severe financial crisis—does not constitute a "justifiable cause."
  • The Core Conflict: The owner evicted the tenant after failed negotiations to raise the jeonse deposit (lump-sum lease deposit) from 660 million won (about $490,000) to 1 billion won (about $740,000), moved in, but then attempted to re-lease the unit to resolve liquidity problems elsewhere.
  • High Stakes for Small Landlords: The court ruled that financial distress stemming from other properties does not excuse stripping a tenant of their renewal rights.
  • Market Shift: The decision is pushing private landlords away from jeonse contracts and toward monthly rent or corporate institutional leasing to avoid litigation.

Why Financial Distress Failed in Court

Under South Korea's tenant protection laws, tenants can claim a one-time two-year renewal unless the homeowner moves in personally. In this case, the landlord moved into the apartment in February 2023 but relocated 14 months later to re-lease the apartment. The landlord argued this was an unavoidable maneuver: a six-unit multi-household building they owned was hit by the national jeonse deposit crisis, where panicking tenants demanded immediate refunds of 690 million won (about $510,000), leaving a massive cash deficit.

While a lower court initially accepted this liquidity crunch as an unpredictable emergency, the appellate bench and the Supreme Court firmly rejected that defense. The higher courts ruled that managing debt across multiple rental holdings is a normal investment risk. The owner's inability to secure bank loans or unwillingness to sell due to hefty capital gains taxes was deemed a personal business failure, not a legal justification to breach tenant rights.

The Fall of Jeonse and the Era of Constant Lawsuits

The ruling highlights the deepening structural flaws in Korea's housing legislation. While the tenant protection framework aimed to protect vulnerable renters, ambiguous terms like "justifiable cause" have shifted the burden onto the courts, effectively turning former tenants into private investigators monitoring their previous homes for early vacancies. Meanwhile, default rates on multi-unit properties continue to set records, with state guarantor HUG (Korea Housing and Urban Guarantee Corporation) logging hundreds of millions of dollars in unpaid deposits.

The policy landscape remains fractured. Following regulatory stagnation during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term in office, the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung faces the difficult task of stabilizing the rental sector while preventing an outright freeze in private housing supply. If individual owners decide that jeonse leases carry too much legal liability, the market will inevitably pivot to monthly leases managed by large corporate landlords—permanently altering Korea's unique housing ladder.
