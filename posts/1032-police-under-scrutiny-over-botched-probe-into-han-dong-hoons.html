Police Under Scrutiny Over Botched Probe into Han Dong-hoon’s Daughter

October 12, 2026

The South Korean police are facing intense backlash for a flawed 20-month investigation into allegations of "false credentials" involving the daughter of Han Dong-hoon, the former Minister of Justice.
  • The Investigation: Despite the accusations being filed in May 2022, police closed the case in December 2023 with no charges, having never executed a single search and seizure warrant.
  • Double Standards: Critics point to a stark contrast in investigative vigor: while the former minister’s family was only subjected to a single written inquiry, other high-profile political investigations have seen dozens of raids and intense in-person questioning.
  • Credibility Gap: The National Office of Investigation (NOI) is embroiled in scandal after its chief, Woo Jong-soo, falsely claimed in a press briefing that numerous witnesses had been interviewed, when in fact, zero were questioned.

A Culture of "Protective Investigation"

The handling of the case against Han Dong-hoon’s family—who were central figures during the administration of the now-imprisoned former leader, Yoon Suk-yeol—is being decried as a textbook example of "protective" police work. While investigators claimed to have sent 14 requests for cooperation to various agencies, these were largely bureaucratic motions—mere paperwork exchanges that functioned as a legal shield to claim "due diligence" while avoiding actual discovery. The discrepancy in treatment is difficult to ignore. In a separate case involving Kim Hye-kyung, wife of current President Lee Jae-myung, police executed over 130 search warrants in just six months. Conversely, the case involving Han’s daughter remained dormant for nearly two years without a single physical evidence collection effort. This behavior suggests that under the previous regime, the police operated with a clear bias, wielding the law as a sword against political opponents while keeping it sheathed for those in power.

Systemic Failures and the Path Forward

The fallout from this investigation has sparked urgent calls for reform, especially as the nation prepares for the launch of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA). The current lack of infrastructure and clear jurisdictional guidelines between the MCIA and the National Office of Investigation is raising alarm, with fears that institutional incompetence will continue to favor the powerful at the expense of public trust. The acting head of the National Police Agency has suggested that a full reinvestigation is "not off the table," but for many, this is too little, too late. The public is demanding accountability not just for the failure to investigate, but for the deliberate deception—the false briefings and the systematic avoidance of evidence—that defined the original probe. To restore faith in the rule of law under the current administration, the police must move beyond mere promises of "review." They must conduct a transparent audit of the officers who oversaw this case and ensure that the "selective justice" of the Yoon era is fully dismantled. Anything less will only confirm the public’s suspicion that the police have yet to break free from their history as a tool for political expediency.
