Why Entropy Rules the Universe: A Brief Guide to the Second Law of Thermodynamics

October 05, 2026

The universe operates on a simple, irreversible rule: things fall apart. While the First Law of Thermodynamics tells us that the total amount of energy in the universe remains constant—like a fixed sum of money changing hands—it is the Second Law, centered on entropy, that dictates the direction of time and why we can never go back.

  • What happened: Leading South Korean statistical physicist Professor Kim Beom-jun recently explained the core concepts of entropy and thermodynamics, tracing them from historical figures like Clausius to the tragic genius Ludwig Boltzmann.
  • What it changes: It shifts our perspective on "disorder" from a moral failure or a random accident to a predictable, probabilistic outcome of how the universe is wired.
  • Who is affected: Everyone—from scientists studying the "heat death" of the universe to anyone grappling with the unpredictable nature of daily life.

The Direction of Time: From Heat to Entropy

If you have ever wondered why coffee cools down but never spontaneously heats up using energy from the surrounding air, you have encountered the "arrow of time." Rudolf Clausius, who coined the term entropy, defined it as the ratio of energy transferred to absolute temperature (ΔS = Q / T). His realization was that in any natural process, the total entropy of the universe must increase. This constant growth is why time only moves forward; the past is a state of lower entropy that, once lost, cannot be recovered.

However, Clausius only described the what, not the why. It took the Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann to explain that entropy is a matter of probability. Using his famous formula, S = k_B log W, Boltzmann proved that what we perceive as "disorder" is simply the state with the highest number of possible configurations. Systems naturally move toward states that are more probable, which, in a chaotic universe, means moving toward maximum entropy.

The Tragic Price of Scientific Insight

Boltzmann’s work was initially met with fierce resistance from 19th-century peers who demanded that science only deal with what could be directly measured. The rejection of his atomic theory led him to deep depression and, ultimately, suicide in 1906. Tragically, just months after his death, Albert Einstein’s work on Brownian motion provided the empirical evidence that finally vindicated Boltzmann’s theories. His tombstone today bears the formula that cost him his life: S = k_B log W.

Modern physics has since expanded on these ideas, addressing famous thought experiments like "Maxwell’s Demon"—a hypothetical entity that tries to reverse entropy by sorting particles. We now know that even processing information requires energy, meaning the demon itself creates more entropy than it saves. This confirms that information is a physical quantity and that the increase of entropy is an inescapable law of our reality.

For those feeling overwhelmed by life's setbacks: remember that misfortune is often not a personal moral judgment, but a result of the same probabilistic laws that govern the stars. We are, ourselves, rare islands of low entropy in a vast, cooling universe—a miraculous, temporary defiance of the cosmic drift.
