President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Rating Hits Record Low

September 07, 2026

  • South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dropped to 40%, with disapproval reaching 51%, according to the latest Gallup Korea poll.
  • Support among voters aged 18–29 has plummeted to 25%, while those in their 30s sit at 31%.
  • The administration’s strategy of "structural majority"—an attempt to engineer political support by alienating perceived "hardline" elements—appears to have backfired.
  • Analysts suggest the government’s focus on political engineering over tangible economic results is driving away both core supporters and moderates.

The latest polling data marks a turning point for the Lee Jae-myung administration. By treating voters like cells in an Excel spreadsheet, the government assumed it could manufacture a new coalition by sidelining traditional party figures, such as lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae, who were framed as obstacles to "moderate expansion." Instead, the administration has found itself in a state of political isolation, losing its core base without successfully capturing the moderate center.

The collapse of support among the 2030 demographic is a brutal indictment of the administration’s approach. Voters are not interested in the government’s internal "moderate expansion" strategies; they are concerned with the cost of living, employment, and housing. When a government prioritizes political maneuvering over bread-and-butter issues, it is perceived as an elitist detachment from reality. The "structural majority" that the administration touted as its hallmark is proving to be a mirage.

The Fallacy of Political Engineering

The current crisis within the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition) mirrors the isolation seen in previous administrations. By defining internal critics and "hardline" party members as scapegoats for declining poll numbers, the leadership has triggered a vicious cycle of internal friction. As the party moves to purge or silence those who disagree, it narrows its own political space rather than expanding it.

This "siege mentality" is a dangerous path. History shows that when a government treats its own supporters as captive assets—assuming they have nowhere else to go—it risks "quiet defection." Voters do not simply switch parties; they become disillusioned, stay home on election day, or withdraw from political discourse entirely. For President Lee, the path forward is clear but difficult: stop searching for internal enemies to blame for strategic failures and start delivering the tangible policy results that voters actually demand. Politics is not a game of drag-and-drop data, and unless the administration pivots toward genuine public needs, the current slide in popularity may be just the beginning.
