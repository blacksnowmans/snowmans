South Korea’s Democratic Party Moves to Impeach Chief Justice Over "Pro-Insurrection" Rulings

November 20, 2026

The ruling Democratic Party of Korea has initiated impeachment proceedings against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, accusing the Supreme Court of legitimizing an attempted "self-coup" carried out during the administration of the now-imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

  • Supreme Court Reversal: The court recently sent the case of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo back to a lower court, ruling that his participation in a Cabinet meeting to declare martial law followed "constitutional procedures," effectively shielding him from insurrection charges.
  • Selective Justice: While the court upheld convictions against former First Lady Kim Keon-hee for stock manipulation, it simultaneously acquitted political broker Myung Tae-kyun of related election law violations, a move critics say was designed to protect the core of the previous power structure.
  • Constitutional Backlash: The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's current ruling party, argues that Chief Justice Cho has provided a "legal mask" for anti-constitutional acts, creating a dangerous precedent for future authoritarianism.
  • Judicial Dissent: Justice Oh Kyung-mi issued a sharp dissenting opinion, arguing that high-ranking officials have a primary duty to protect the Constitution and that following a president’s illegal orders is a crime regardless of administrative "formalities."

The Paradox of Procedural Justice

The Supreme Court’s current trajectory under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae suggests a shift toward a rigid, formalistic interpretation of the law that ignores the substantive intent of criminal acts. In the case of Han Duck-soo, the court argued that because the Cabinet meeting was convened according to the book, the participants could not be held liable for the resulting insurrection. This logic is akin to arguing that a robbery is legal as long as the thieves drafted a formally perfect itinerary beforehand. By prioritizing the "outer shell" of administrative procedure, the court has effectively signaled to civil servants that they can assist in the destruction of democracy so long as they check the right boxes.

The rulings regarding Kim Keon-hee and Myung Tae-kyun further deepen the public’s cynicism. By confirming a conviction for Kim on lesser charges while acquitting Myung—the man allegedly at the center of her political and financial dealings—the court appears to be performing a balancing act. It offers a "sacrificial" conviction to quiet public anger while ensuring that the actual mechanisms of influence-peddling remain legally untouched. This "selective justice" suggests the judiciary is acting more as a political mediator than a neutral arbiter of the law.

Impeachment as a Final Constitutional Check

The DPK’s decision to pursue impeachment is a high-stakes gamble intended to halt what they describe as a "counter-historical" judicial trend. Alongside other progressive parties, the DPK has formed an "Impeachment Task Force" to formalize the charges. The party leadership, including representative Kim Min-seok, has stated that they cannot tolerate a judiciary that "forces the public to accept the behavior of Han Duck-soo as a behavioral norm during an insurrection." By making the impeachment a formal party platform, the DPK is ensuring total legislative unity in their attempt to remove Cho.

"The Chief Justice’s rulings will be remembered as an act of murdering justice in the name of the law. Impeachment is the final desperate measure to resuscitate that dying justice."

For the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, this battle represents the final stage of dismantling the remnants of the Yoon Suk-yeol era. While the executive and legislative branches have already shifted, the judiciary remains a stronghold of the old guard. If the impeachment succeeds, it will signal a fundamental restructuring of the Korean legal system; if it fails, it may embolden a conservative judiciary to continue acting as a shield for officials of the previous regime. For now, the "conscience of the court" rests with dissenters like Justice Oh, who remains the lone voice reminding the bench that the law must serve the Constitution, not the commander.
