President Lee Jae-myung’s Legal Troubles Highlight Constitutional Ambiguities and Ruling Party Rift

November 04, 2026

Ongoing court trials involving President Lee Jae-myung are testing South Korea’s constitutional limits on presidential immunity, driving down administration approval ratings and triggering severe internal division within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

  • Constitutional friction: While South Korea’s Constitution shields sitting presidents from new criminal indictments, courts retain the independent authority to continue pre-existing trials.
  • Stalled agenda: Falling public support threatens to paralyze the Lee administration’s key policy goals as national attention remains consumed by judicial proceedings.
  • Party infighting: Hardline loyalists and online fandoms within the Democratic Party of Korea are attacking internal critics and journalists, alienating moderate voters.
  • Systemic strain: The clash highlights how South Korea's political landscape remains tied to judicial battles, distracting from governance and public welfare.

Constitutional Privilege Meets Judicial Independence

Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution grants sitting presidents immunity from criminal prosecution except in cases of treason or insurrection. However, legal experts remain sharply divided on whether this protection automatically pauses criminal trials that were already underway before the president took office. Because individual trial judges hold the authority to schedule court dates, the presidency lacks a direct mechanism to unilaterally suspend ongoing proceedings without triggering severe political blowback, such as attempting legislative overhauls or judicial impeachments.

This legal ambiguity creates immediate operational risk for the administration. As legal headlines dominate the news cycle, public perception has shifted from viewing constitutional immunity as an absolute shield to seeing it as a source of ongoing instability. Consequently, President Lee’s policy momentum is being bogged down by persistent judicial pressure, accelerating a decline in approval ratings and signaling deep uncertainty to the electorate.

A Ruling Party at War with Itself

The legal pressure on the presidency has triggered an aggressive reaction among hardline factions within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. In the name of shielding President Lee, militant online fandoms and party loyalists have launched hostile campaigns against journalists and moderate party figures who voice dissent. Slurs and conspiracy-laden accusations against critics have become common among extreme supporters, creating a hostile culture that damages the party's broader public appeal.

This toxic environment threatens the Democratic Party's ability to retain middle-ground voters required for sustained political strength. Following the removal of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence—South Koreans expected political stability and institutional recovery. Instead, persistent judicial battles and ruling-party infighting threaten to lock the nation in a continuous state of confrontation. To regain public trust, the ruling party must restore internal democracy, restrain fanatical loyalists, and re-anchor its platform on economic delivery rather than partisan defense.
