Political Tensions Spike as Justice Minister Hearing and Judicial Rulings Converge

November 19, 2026

A wave of judicial rulings and high-stakes confirmation hearings is pushing South Korea’s political tension to a breaking point this week as the ruling and opposition parties clash over the legacy of the previous administration.

  • Justice Minister nominee Lee Yong-cheol will face his confirmation hearing on November 24, a pivotal event for the legal reform agenda of the President Lee Jae-myung administration.
  • Choo Kyung-ho, the floor leader of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, was acquitted in his first trial on charges of obstructing the lifting of martial law during the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era.
  • The Supreme Court, South Korea’s highest judicial body, will live-stream the final verdict for former first lady Kim Keon-hee and former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo on November 19.
  • The PPP has threatened a full-scale filibuster to block the Democratic Martyrs Act, a bill proposed by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP) to honor and compensate pro-democracy activists.

A Legal Reprieve for the Opposition

The acquittal of Choo Kyung-ho has sent shockwaves through the capital. The court ruled that there was no objective evidence proving that Choo acted under specific instructions from Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol to interfere with the lifting of martial law. While the PPP hailed the decision as a victory for the rule of law, the ruling DP described the verdict as a "historical betrayal," arguing that the judiciary is shielding those who threatened the nation's democratic foundations. This ruling has effectively recharged the opposition's resistance against the current government's efforts to prosecute figures from the previous administration.

Meanwhile, the upcoming hearing for Lee Yong-cheol is being framed as a referendum on the Lee Jae-myung government’s "prosecution reform" roadmap. Lee, the nominee to lead the Ministry of Justice, is expected to face intense scrutiny over his views on the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers. For the ruling party, his appointment is essential to "normalizing" a legal system they claim was weaponized under Mr. Yoon; for the opposition, it is an attempt to install a loyalist to protect the sitting president.

Transparency and Legislative Deadlock

In a rare move, the Supreme Court’s decision to live-stream the final rulings for Kim Keon-hee and Han Duck-soo marks a significant shift toward judicial transparency. By broadcasting the proceedings, the court aims to mitigate public distrust and explain its legal logic directly to a polarized citizenry. Although neither defendant is expected to appear in person—a common practice in final appeals—the outcome will be a major bellwether for other ongoing investigations involving the former first lady, including allegations of stock manipulation.

The battleground now shifts back to the National Assembly, where the legislative process remains paralyzed. The DP is determined to pass the Democratic Martyrs Act to provide state support for those who suffered during past pro-democracy movements. However, the PPP views this as a "special favors" bill designed to benefit the ruling party's political base. With the opposition preparing a filibuster and the ruling party ready to force a vote, the legislature is bracing for another round of physical and procedural gridlock that threatens to stall unrelated fiscal and welfare bills.

"The judiciary is currently the most volatile arena in Korean politics. Whether through live-streamed verdicts or acquittal rulings, the courts are now dictating the pace and tone of the rivalry between President Lee Jae-myung and the conservative opposition."
