President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Rating Drops to 35.2% Amid Internal Party Strains

October 16, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has fallen for a second consecutive week to 35.2%, triggering heightened anxiety and calls for strategy overhauls within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK).

  • What happened: President Lee’s public support slid to 35.2% following backlash over unrefined social media messages, prompting a faction of 55 DPK lawmakers to publicly align behind party figure Kim Yong to consolidate the party's core base.
  • What it changes: The ruling party is shifting toward a more aggressive strategy, positioning combative lawmakers in parliamentary committees to clear out remaining prosecution networks built under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment.
  • Who is affected: The DPK leadership faces dual pressure from moderate voters defecting to third parties and grassroots party loyalists demanding strict discipline and hardline candidate vetting for upcoming elections.

Unforced Errors and a Fragmenting Base

The drop to 35.2% serves as a sharp warning for the Lee administration. A primary driver behind the decline is messaging consistency: critics point out that official presidential apologies have been repeatedly undercut by President Lee's own follow-up posts on social media. This unscripted communication style has alienated swing voters and exhausted key supporters. Minor political forces are capitalizing on the slump; the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party reached 6.3% support, while non-aligned voters grew by 2 percentage points.

Adding to the friction, President Lee’s past combative rhetoric from his time as Seongnam mayor—such as his comment that "mad dogs need a stick"—has resurfaced in public debate. While core loyalists view this fighting spirit as necessary to dismantle deep-rooted corruption, critics argue that aggressive messaging without polished execution risks driving away moderate voters who favor presidential stability.

Doubling Down on Party Discipline

In response to the slump, the DPK core is opting for front-line combativeness rather than centrist moderation. The public declaration by 55 lawmakers to support Kim Yong is a deliberate show of strength meant to signal unity and warn internal dissenters. Simultaneously, the party is deploying sharp-tongued lawmakers to the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee to lead aggressive parliamentary audits targeting the legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s prosecution apparatus.

For the ruling party, the immediate focus has shifted to internal discipline. Grassroots members are demanding ideological clarity and calling for the removal of lawmakers who fail to align with the party line. How the DPK manages this internal push for purity while refining President Lee’s public messaging will determine whether the administration can regain its political footing.
