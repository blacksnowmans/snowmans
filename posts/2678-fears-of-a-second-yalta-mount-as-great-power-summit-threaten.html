Fears of a 'Second Yalta' Mount as Great-Power Summit Threatens to Sidelong South Korea

November 24, 2026

A historic meeting between Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin—paired with reports that Trump considered shelving North Korean denuclearization demands—has raised alarm in Seoul that South Korea is being excluded from critical decisions about its own national security.

  • What happened: Former US President Donald Trump reportedly considered setting aside strict nuclear disarmament demands in favor of a formal peace declaration and liaison office to lure Kim Jong Un back to the negotiating table.
  • North Korea's strategy: Kim Jong Un declared North Korea holds no "hegemonic or expansionist" ambitions during a rare audience with foreign ambassadors, leveraging diplomatic diversification to legitimize Pyongyang's status as a nuclear-armed state.
  • What changes: Russian President Vladimir Putin proposed hosting a second trilateral summit with Kim invited as an observer, threatening to entrench a bloc-based alignment in Northeast Asia.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung’s administration in South Korea faces mounting pressure to prevent a scenario where Washington and Moscow dictate the security architecture of the Korean Peninsula over Seoul's head.

The Spectre of 'Korea Passing'

The prospect of Washington bargaining directly with Pyongyang without Seoul's involvement revives deep historical anxieties in South Korea, drawing comparisons to the 1945 Yalta Conference where superpower bargaining partitioned the peninsula. Na Kyung-won, a lawmaker from the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, warned that waiving denuclearization requirements for quick diplomatic trophies—such as a peace declaration ending the 1950–53 Korean War—threatens to shatter South Korea's security baseline. Accepting North Korea's nuclear arsenal as a given in exchange for a frozen conflict would leave Seoul living permanently under a nuclear shadow while Pyongyang secures international legitimacy.

Pyongyang is capitalizing on this moment by executing a clear plan B. Rather than relying solely on high-stakes talks with Washington, Kim Jong Un hosted envoys from 10 nations for the first time in three decades and reaffirmed historic ties with Beijing. By framing its nuclear arsenal as purely defensive, North Korea aims to break out of diplomatic isolation and get international sanctions eased without giving up a single warhead.

Diplomatic Realignment and Seoul's Challenge

The emerging dynamics between Moscow, Beijing, and Washington leave South Korea in a precarious position. Putin's push to bring North Korea into future multi-power summits as an observer would further integrate Pyongyang into a counter-Western coalition, rewarding its military cooperation with Russia and weakening the leverage of international sanctions.

In response, President Lee Jae-myung has attempted soft-power overtures—including proposing a casual game of Go to Chinese President Xi Jinping—to keep diplomatic channels open and avoid being boxed into an isolated corner. However, as great powers maneuver for influence, Seoul must ensure that its core requirement—the complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula—remains non-negotiable on the global stage.
