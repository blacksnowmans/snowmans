South Korea's Rival Parties Propose "3+3" Body to Break Housing Gridlock

October 31, 2026

South Korea’s main opposition party has proposed a bipartisan "3+3" consultative body to tackle skyrocketing rental prices and unblock urban housing supply, putting pressure on the ruling Democratic Party to negotiate long-stalled real estate reforms.

  • What happened: The conservative People Power Party proposed a six-member bipartisan panel—three senior lawmakers from each side—to negotiate property regulations and housing supply.
  • What is on the table: The agenda includes easing the reconstruction excess-profit tax, loosening household loan restrictions for first-time buyers, and stabilizing the rental market.
  • Who is affected: Young adults, tenants facing soaring deposits and forced transitions to monthly rent, and prospective homebuyers caught between tight credit controls and high prices.

Politics Meets the Housing Squeeze

Seoul and the surrounding capital region have watched lease deposits climb month after month, with a severe shortage of jeonse (lump-sum deposit) properties pushing desperate tenants into semi-jeonse or monthly rent arrangements. By aggressively offering a negotiation table first, the People Power Party is attempting to seize the political initiative, shifting the public's frustration over stagnant housing policies directly to the doorstep of the ruling Democratic Party, led by President Lee Jae-myung.

While the proposal is framed as a gesture of legislative cooperation, it is hard to separate it from partisan calculus. The real test is whether both sides can move past political posturing to address deep structural issues: streamlining urban redevelopment approvals and reforming the contentious reconstruction tax without triggering a new wave of speculative buying in wealthy Seoul districts.

Loans, Rentals, and the Risks of Quick Fixes

Another major flashpoint is household debt. With household debt approaching roughly $1.4 trillion (1,900조 원), the government is wary of easing mortgage rules. Yet, stringent loan caps have hit ordinary buyers saving for a small home just as hard as speculative investors. At the same time, the rental market is straining under the aftershocks of a widespread villa (low-rise multi-family housing) fraud crisis, which has funneled panicked tenants exclusively into apartments and driven rents even higher.

For buyers and renters watching from the sidelines, this political maneuvering offers little immediate relief. Easing regulations on paper will not instantly build new apartments, and rushing into the market based on vague promises of reform carries heavy financial risks. Until the National Assembly turns headlines into concrete, well-calibrated support for tenants and genuine supply-side fixes, prospective homeowners would be wise to rely on conservative financial planning rather than political optimism.
