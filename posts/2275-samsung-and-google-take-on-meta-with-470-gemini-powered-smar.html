Samsung and Google Take on Meta with $470 Gemini-Powered Smart Glasses

November 13, 2026

Samsung Electronics and Google will launch their first multimodal AI smart glasses on December 10 across South Korea and the United States, pricing the device aggressively to challenge Meta’s early grip on the wearable eyewear market.

  • Aggressive pricing and fashion-first design: Starting at 650,000 won (about $470) and topping out at 750,000 won (about $540), the "Intelligent Eyewear" will offer 30 frame styles co-developed with South Korea's luxury eyewear designer Gentle Monster and US retailer Warby Parker.
  • Gemini under the hood: Google’s Gemini multimodal engine drives real-time spoken translation, visual search, and contextual assistance through a micro-display, supported by power optimization claiming up to 72 hours of mixed battery life.
  • Built-in anti-voyeurism safeguards: Addressing widespread privacy anxieties, the glasses automatically shut down if the camera or sensor is physically covered, while onboard software blurs the faces of bystanders in real time.
  • Cheating panic in South Korea: The release lands amid acute domestic controversy following allegations that high-tech glasses were used to cheat on a national tax accountant exam, exposing a total absence of test-center regulations for discreet wearables.

A Fashion-First Play for the Post-Smartphone Era

Samsung’s strategy shows the industry has finally learned why the original Google Glass failed a decade ago: nobody wants to wear a computer on their face unless it looks like ordinary designer eyewear. By partnering with Gentle Monster—a brand that commands a cult following across Asia and beyond for its high-fashion frames—and Warby Parker, Samsung is burying the circuitry inside styles consumers would buy anyway. Priced well below premium smartphones, the alliance is staging a direct assault on Meta's Ray-Ban monopoly.

The real shift is how Google's Gemini functions when fused to human vision. Bypassing the friction of pulling out a smartphone, opening a camera, and typing a query, the glasses turn whatever the wearer looks at into immediate AI prompts. If the promised power management holds up beyond standby mode, Samsung and Google may present the first wearable ecosystem capable of genuinely peeling users away from handheld screens.

South Korea as the Proving Ground for Wearable Ethics

The device is arriving at a cultural flashpoint in South Korea, where high-stakes exams determine careers and social mobility. Recent suspicions that an examinee scored a perfect 100 on an infamously difficult certified tax accountant exam using connected eyewear have panicked educators. Because glasses are treated as medical necessities rather than mobile devices, proctors cannot simply ban them at the door, leaving test authorities with an acute regulatory blind spot.

Samsung and Google deserve credit for designing hardware-level kill-switches rather than relying solely on easily bypassed LED lights to signal recording. Yet social resistance will be harder to engineer away than hardware constraints. If public anxiety over cheating and surreptitious filming pushes Korean institutions to ban smart eyewear in classrooms and offices, Samsung’s domestic test bed may quickly signal the legal limits facing ambient AI worldwide.
