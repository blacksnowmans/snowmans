South Korea’s New Property Tax Overhaul Risks Shocking Retirees by 2028

September 24, 2026

  • South Korea's finalized property tax revisions mask hidden traps, notably a strict 6 million won (about $4,400) cap on tax credits starting in 2028 that will hit asset-rich, cash-poor retirees hard.
  • Harsh multi-homeowner penalties—with acquisition taxes reaching up to 13.4% and transfer taxes up to 82.5%—have all but killed speculative buying, leaving a market where people only sell to shuffle assets.
  • An estimated 40,000 registered rental apartments in Seoul must be sold off by 2027 to avoid heavy penalties, injecting massive inventory pressure into the capital's housing market.
  • While the government frames these rules to curb speculation, cash-strapped retirees face mounting pressure to dump homes, and renters risk absorbing higher costs passed down by landlords.

South Korea’s latest property tax changes are sparking panic among homeowners, but the real danger isn't in today's tax bills — it's the delayed time bombs buried in the legislation. Property experts Lee Jang-won and Kim Hak-ryul warn that looking only at headline tax rates or minor adjustments to deduction limits misses the structural danger threatening ordinary asset owners and retirees in the coming years.

The core issue is a looming shift in tax credits. While current rules offer heavy deductions for seniors and long-term homeowners—sometimes slashing multi-million-won tax bills by up to 80%—a strict 6 million-won cap on tax credits kicks in by 2028. For retirees living on modest pensions whose home values have climbed, the removal of this safety net means sudden, unaffordable tax bills. Unable to rely on adult children who face their own financial strains, many will be forced to put their lifelong homes on the market.

The Death of Speculation and the 2027 Rental Supply Shock

Heavy government crackdowns since 2020 have fundamentally broken South Korea's multi-home investment market. With acquisition taxes as high as 13.4% and transfer taxes hitting 82.5% for owners of three or more properties, the math simply no longer works for speculators. Today’s transactions are strictly defensive: people selling off underperforming properties or executing forced relocations.

Adding to the pressure, roughly 40,000 registered rental apartments in Seoul alone must be sold off by 2027 to avoid punishing tax penalties. This incoming wave of inventory, combined with strict lending caps that paralyze everyday buyers, means the market is undergoing a painful structural purge rather than a natural cycle. Ultimately, these punitive tax policies don't just hurt wealthy owners; the financial burden inevitably trickles down to tenants through higher rents, proving that the deepest risks in Korea's housing market are born from regulatory uncertainty rather than market forces alone.
