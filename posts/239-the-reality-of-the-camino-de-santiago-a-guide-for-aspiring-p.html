The Reality of the Camino de Santiago: A Guide for Aspiring Pilgrims

September 07, 2026

The Camino de Santiago—the ancient network of pilgrimage routes leading to the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela in Spain—has transformed from a niche religious journey into a global “coming-of-age” ritual, with over 400,000 people completing the trek annually as of 2026.

  • The Surge: The route, particularly the popular "French Way" (Camino Francés), is seeing unprecedented foot traffic, turning once-quiet paths into crowded corridors.
  • New Challenges: Overtourism has led to issues with trash and strain on local communities, forcing travelers to move beyond the "romantic" ideal of the walk.
  • Logistical Hurdles: Success requires careful planning regarding the credencial (pilgrim passport), mandatory lodging reservations, and managing health risks like bedbugs.
  • The Takeaway: The journey is less about a grand epiphany and more about the discipline of daily, humble progress.

Beyond the Romance: Navigating the Trail

The Instagram-worthy images of the Camino mask a grueling physical and environmental reality. With thousands walking the same path daily, the cumulative impact of litter and overcrowding is becoming a significant burden for local residents. For the modern pilgrim, sustainability is no longer optional; it is a responsibility. Travelers who view the route solely as a path to personal healing often ignore the irony of contributing to the environmental degradation of the very land they visit.

Preparation is the only defense against the harsh realities of the trail. The credencial—a document costing about $2 to $3 (2,000–3,000 won)—is your essential proof of pilgrimage, used to collect stamps at stops along the way. Without it, you are effectively a ghost on the trail. Moreover, the "war" for lodging at albergues (pilgrim hostels) is real. Especially as you approach the 100km mark, failing to book in advance can leave you stranded without a bed, forced to walk until exhaustion in the next town.

Survival Skills and Mental Discipline

The biggest threats on the Camino are not grand challenges, but small, persistent ones. Bedbugs are a constant risk in communal hostels; experts advise keeping bags off beds and sealing belongings in plastic. Furthermore, the 780km journey is essentially a month-long marathon. Physical training prior to departure is the only insurance policy for your knees.

Embracing the rhythm of local life—such as the siesta (midday rest) and the menu del día (the affordable, set-price "menu of the day" for pilgrims)—is key to survival. Ultimately, the Camino is not a race for a certificate, but a lesson in pacing. Whether you are battling blisters or the monotony of the path, the most important thing is to move at your own speed rather than comparing yourself to others. Success is found not in the completion of the trek, but in the decision to keep moving forward, one step at a time.
