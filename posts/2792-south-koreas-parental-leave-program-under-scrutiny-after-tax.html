South Korea’s Parental Leave Program Under Scrutiny After Tax Exam Controversy

November 27, 2026

A South Korean worker who passed a professional tax accountant exam while on paid parental leave—after studying 80 hours a week and providing just two hours of childcare—has been reported to the Ministry of Employment and Labor for benefit fraud, sparking nationwide controversy.

  • What happened: An online post revealed a mother extended her state-subsidized parental leave to study from 6:30 AM to 10:30 PM daily while collecting up to 2.5 million won (about $1,800) per month in government allowances, triggering formal complaints of fraudulent benefit collection.
  • The debate: Critics argue that state benefits funded by taxpayers and employment insurance are strictly meant for hands-on parenting, while defenders contend that self-improvement during off-hours is a personal choice if extended family handles childcare.
  • What it changes: The Ministry of Employment and Labor's upcoming review will set a key precedent on whether full-time exam prep during parental leave constitutes illegal misuse requiring benefit repayment.
  • Who is affected: Working parents, corporate colleagues taking on temporary workloads, and government agencies managing South Korea's expanding family welfare budget.

Exploitation vs. Upskilling: A Test for Welfare Integrity

The controversy highlights a growing friction point in South Korea’s labor and welfare systems. To combat its severe demographic decline, South Korea has progressively expanded paid parental leave subsidies. However, when an employee uses extended leave primarily for intensive exam preparation while delegating childcare to family members, it creates resentment among co-workers who absorb the absent employee's workload under the belief that they are actively parenting.

If labor authorities rule that full-time studying violates the statutory purpose of parental leave, it will draw a clear boundary between acceptable personal development and systemic abuse. The decision will also test how strictly the government intends to audit employment insurance funds as social safety net spending expands.

Broadening Subsidies Amid Structural Demographic Decline

The controversy comes as South Korea continues to roll out aggressive incentives to support young families. Recent government measures include specialized low-interest mortgage loans (starting around 1%) for new parents, reduced work-hour options for parents of children up to the sixth grade, and the addition of 56,000 public housing units in the Greater Seoul area next year.

Yet behind these expanding perks lies a deepening crisis in local infrastructure. With national births remaining below 300,000 annually, regional daycare centers in industrial cities like Ulsan are facing severe financial distress from declining enrollment, while provincial education offices report structural budget deficits reaching 9.5 trillion won (about $6.8 billion). As the state invests heavily in child-rearing incentives, maintaining public trust in welfare integrity will be crucial for sustaining these long-term demographic policies.
