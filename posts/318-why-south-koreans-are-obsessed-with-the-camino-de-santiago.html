Why South Koreans Are Obsessed with the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

South Korean interest in the Camino de Santiago is surging, with a growing number of travelers documenting their grueling preparations for the 800-kilometer trek across Spain.

  • The Trend: South Korean participation in the Camino de Santiago has grown steadily, placing the country among the top Asian visitors to the pilgrimage route.
  • The Reality: Far from a casual vacation, the journey is treated as a severe physical test, requiring meticulous packing strategies focused strictly on minimizing weight.
  • The Double Trip: Many Korean travelers combine the pilgrimage with a standard European vacation, packing separate gear for rugged trail walking and city sightseeing.
  • The Warning: Experienced travelers emphasize that safety and pain management take precedence over stubborn attempts to complete the route at all costs.

Packing for Pain: The Logistics of an 800-Kilometer Walk

For South Korean travelers preparing for the Camino, the golden rule is that weight equals pain. Videos detailing pre-trip checklists reveal a rigorous selection process where heavy gear—such as a 1.5-kilogram sleeping bag—is instantly rejected and returned. To survive the trek, every gram matters. Pilgrims opt for lightweight, multi-purpose items, choosing 35-liter backpacks with built-in rain covers and compact quick-drying layers rather than excessive changes of clothes.

At the same time, preparation goes beyond simple minimalism. Travelers must balance shedding weight with essential survival tools: medical kits for blisters, headlamps for pre-dawn starts, and personal sleeping bags to guard against bedbugs in shared pilgrim hostels, known as *albergues*. The financial investment is substantial—often totaling over 1 million won (about $750) for gear alone—reflecting how seriously these journeys are planned.

More Than a Hike: The Quest for Mental Simplicity

Beyond the blisters and sore knees, the appeal of the Camino for South Koreans lies in its radical simplicity. In a hyper-connected, high-pressure society where individuals face constant choices, the pilgrimage reduces life to two fundamental questions: How far to walk, and what to eat today. This stripping away of modern complexity is what draws so many to endure the physical torment.

However, commentators and veterans of the trail offer an important caveat: stubbornness can be dangerous. Over-reliance on a romanticized notion of "finishing at all costs" frequently leads to injuries, exhaustion, and rescue operations in treacherous areas like the Pyrenees. True pilgrims know that respecting one's physical limits and prioritizing safety over a completion certificate is the only way to find genuine value on the road.
