Why Nvidia's 400,000 GPU Frenzy is Supercharging Samsung and SK Hynix

September 08, 2026

  • What happened: OpenAI unveiled its new "GPT-6 Astra" model, prompting Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to declare the arrival of the "AGI era" backed by hundreds of thousands of GPUs.
  • The supply crunch: Surging demand for advanced AI chips is causing a severe memory shortage, with next-gen HBM4 production eating into standard DRAM capacity.
  • Korean market impact: South Korean tech giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are seeing soaring profit estimates and target price upgrades despite currency headwinds.
  • The broader risk: Massive tech infrastructure spending faces a widening "revenue gap," alongside secondary bottlenecks in U.S. power grids and transformers.

South Korean stock market investors watching memory giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix experienced a major jolt after OpenAI dropped its new "GPT-6 Astra" AI model. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang quickly followed by declaring the dawn of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), confirming that Nvidia systems use over 100,000 processors and will soon deploy 40万 additional Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) for training. As data center projects expand to handle these workloads, demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and specialized semiconductor chips has officially entered a hyper-growth phase.

This unprecedented demand has triggered severe warnings of a memory supply shortage for next year. According to KB Securities, manufacturing advanced HBM4 memory requires triple the wafer capacity of standard DRAM, meaning that ramping up high-end production naturally chokes off basic chip supply. With global AI infrastructure investments swelling toward $1.3 trillion, market demand is projected to outstrip supply by over 10 percentage points. Consequently, operating profit estimates for Samsung and SK Hynix have surged into record territory, with SK Chairman Chey Tae-won warning that the memory supply crunch will peak next year.

Beyond the Hype: Supply Crunches, Valuation Gaps, and Power Grids

The Double-Edged Sword of Chronic Shortages

While skyrocketing memory prices are driving immediate profits for South Korean chipmakers, chronic shortages carry a hidden sting. Industry experts warn that if memory prices climb too high, big tech companies will aggressively develop workarounds and memory-efficient tech to cut costs—essentially cannibalizing their own hardware demand. Furthermore, the strong South Korean won has put temporary pressure on export-heavy earnings. Yet, brokerages continue to raise target prices because the profit scale from HBM sales vastly outweighs short-term currency fluctuations. Crucially, long-term supply agreements (LTAs) are transforming memory chips from a notoriously cyclical commodity into an essential industrial staple.

The $600 Billion Revenue Gap and Power Grid Bottlenecks

Beneath the surface of this trillion-dollar AI boom lie deeper structural risks. Sequoia Capital's "revenue gap" model highlights a troubling mismatch: while hyperscalers pour hundreds of billions into capital expenditures, the end-user software market falls far short of generating the revenue needed to justify those costs. OpenAI and Anthropic continue to burn through billions annually, drawing uneasy parallels to the dot-com bubble. At the same time, physical infrastructure is hitting a wall. With individual Nvidia GPUs pulling up to 700 watts, data centers face severe power densities, while aging U.S. electrical grids and multi-year wait times for gas turbines create a severe bottleneck. This exact pinch has turned into an unexpected windfall for South Korean transformer and power equipment makers, whose exports to the U.S. are surging.

Ultimately, the current market turbulence reflects the birth pains of a structural transformation rather than a fleeting trend. While short-term noise around valuations and currency rates will persist, South Korea's memory heavyweights sit firmly at the neck of an irreplaceable supply chain. For investors playing the long game, tuning out the day-to-day volatility and focusing on who controls the physical bottlenecks remains the only strategy that matters.
