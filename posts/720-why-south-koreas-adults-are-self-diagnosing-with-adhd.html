Why South Korea's Adults Are Self-Diagnosing With ADHD

September 11, 2026

South Korea is experiencing a massive boom in adults seeking psychiatric care for Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, but experts warn that many people are masking burnout, anxiety, and the pressure of a hyper-competitive society behind a medical label.

  • The numbers: The number of South Korean adults treated for ADHD jumped more than 4.8 times between 2020 and 2024, crossing 120,000 patients, with 30-something women driving the sharpest surge.
  • The self-diagnosis trap: Viral social media content has popularized checklists that turn everyday procrastination and deadline stress into "ADHD traits," offering instant relief from self-blame.
  • The hidden cost: Attributing systemic exhaustion entirely to a brain disorder can prevent people from treating actual underlying issues like depression, sleep deprivation, or physical ailments.
  • The medical reality: Real ADHD is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition with symptoms present before age 12 across multiple areas of life—not a sudden reaction to adult workplace stress.

Procrastination as Emotional Defense

When office workers stare at a blank document for hours instead of writing a report, society loves to blame poor time management or a lack of willpower. Psychologists, however, point out that procrastination is rarely about the task itself. Instead, it is an emotional defense mechanism—an attempt to escape the dread, anxiety, and fear of failure associated with the work.

By putting off a task until the absolute last minute, people build a ready-made excuse: "I didn't fail because I'm incompetent; I failed because I ran out of time." For perfectionists trapped in South Korea's grueling corporate culture, grabbing onto an ADHD diagnosis offers a comforting shield against harsh self-criticism. It provides a momentary sense of peace, but it stops people from examining the deeper roots of their exhaustion.

When the Diagnosis Masks Deeper Illness

Treating every symptom of burnout as a neurological defect carries real risks. Psychiatrists emphasize that clinical depression in modern adults rarely looks like cinematic weeping; instead, it looks like a system shutdown. Tasks as simple as replying to a text message or doing the dishes suddenly feel like climbing Mount Everest, triggering a vicious cycle of guilt and paralysis.

Physical health can play a massive role as well. Chronic sleep deprivation—sleeping fewer than five hours a night as a badge of honor—produces cognitive symptoms that are neurologically indistinguishable from ADHD. Thyroid disorders, severe vitamin deficiencies, and anemia can similarly strip away executive function, leaving people mentally sluggish and desperate for answers.

Ultimately, labeling every human flaw with a medical term provides a quick release from modern pressure, but it risks trapping patients in the wrong treatment. Before blaming the brain for a drop in focus, experts suggest looking closer at sleep, stress, and the emotional toll of living in an unrelenting culture of competition.
