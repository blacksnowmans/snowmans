South Korea's Ruling Party Hit by Record Low Ratings Amid Bitter Parliament Clashes

October 10, 2026

  • What happened: Fresh Gallup polling shows President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating holding steady at 37%, but support for his ruling Democratic Party has plummeted to 35%—its lowest point since the current administration took office—while parliament's annual audit kicked off with fierce partisan battles.
  • What it changes: The widening gap between the president's stable numbers and his party's declining support signals growing voter fatigue with both major parties, forcing the ruling camp to reevaluate its legislative strategy.
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party, the conservative main opposition People Power Party, and vulnerable groups like farmers and fishermen, who scored a temporary win after the president delayed a major trade pact.

Slump in Ruling Party Support as Opposition Pressures Government

South Korea's political landscape is flashing warning signs for the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party. While President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating held at 37%, a new Gallup poll revealed that support for his party sank to 35%, a record low for the current administration. Meanwhile, the conservative main opposition People Power Party polled at 26%. Analysts view the drop as a sign that core supporters are growing restless or moving to the sidelines amid mounting fatigue with the country's two dominant political blocs.

Capitalizing on this vulnerability, minor opposition groups like the Rebuilding Korea Party ratcheted up pressure on the liberals. At the same time, the People Power Party launched aggressive attacks over judicial and legal controversies. Rather than uniting behind a coherent reform agenda, the progressive camp has found itself bogged down in internal friction and defensive maneuvers.

Parliamentary Audits and Trade Friction Keep Seoul on Edge

The first week of the National Assembly's annual government audit devolved into heavy partisan bickering. Key committees, including the Political Affairs and Health and Welfare panels, clashed bitterly over state funds and judicial independence. Observers note that instead of serving as a mechanism to check government policy, the opening days of the audit turned into a display of score-settling that left little room for urgent livelihood legislation.

On the diplomatic and trade front, tensions flared following reports regarding a U.S. visa delay for prominent ruling party lawmaker Chung Chung-rae, drawing sharp rebukes from liberal legislators over perceived diplomatic disrespect. Conversely, the administration threw a temporary lifeline to local agriculture by having President Lee order a freeze on any application to join the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) within the year, prioritizing negotiations to protect domestic farmers and fishermen.
