Global Far-Right Surge: From European Election Gains to International Security Threats

November 19, 2026

Far-right movements are no longer fringe elements but central political forces reshaping immigration, international relations, and national security across Europe and the Pacific.

  • Marine Le Pen, a prominent figure in France’s far-right National Rally, has emerged as a frontrunner for the 2027 presidential election, alarming European Union authorities who fear a populist takeover of the bloc's second-largest economy.
  • In Germany, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) is expanding its influence despite internal friction, leveraging "neo-Nazi" networks and targeting welfare benefits for children living abroad to stoke nationalist sentiment.
  • The Australian government has imposed targeted sanctions on three individuals and four entities linked to violent Israeli settler movements in the West Bank, signaling a proactive stance against extremist-driven human rights abuses.
  • Security agencies have linked a wave of bomb threats against at least 27 European passenger flights to Russian far-right organizations, highlighting a shift toward hybrid warfare and international terror tactics.

The Normalization of Extremism in Europe

The rise of these movements represents a systemic failure of mainstream political establishments to address grassroots economic anxiety and identity concerns. In France and Germany, the "normalization" of once-taboo rhetoric regarding immigrants and social welfare has allowed groups like the AfD and National Rally to move from the periphery into the political center. This is not merely a shift in voter preference; it is a fundamental reconfiguration of the European political landscape, where hate-based populism is increasingly viewed as a legitimate alternative to traditional governance.

In countries like Spain and Italy, right-wing parties are successfully using "fear marketing" to dominate the news cycle. By targeting specific demographics—such as Moroccan immigrants or social welfare recipients—these parties cultivate a sense of crisis that justifies radical policy shifts. The recent commemoration of former fascist leaders in the Italian parliament serves as a stark reminder that the historical safeguards meant to prevent the resurgence of extremism are rapidly eroding.

Global Security and the Limits of Populism

The international dimension of this trend is equally troubling. Far-right extremism is increasingly manifesting as a direct threat to global stability, whether through state-sponsored harassment or localized violence. The Australian government’s decision to sanction violent settlers in the West Bank reflects a growing international consensus that far-right violence is a security concern that transcends national borders. Similarly, the use of bomb threats against commercial aviation by Russian-linked groups shows how extremist ideology is being weaponized to create widespread social paralysis.

However, there are signs that this momentum is not invincible. In the United Kingdom, Nigel Farage and his Reform UK party have seen their approval ratings hit a two-year low, suggesting that populist rhetoric without concrete policy solutions eventually yields diminishing returns. For observers in South Korea—where President Lee Jae-myung’s Democratic Party government faces a sharply divided domestic landscape against the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition)—these global shifts serve as a reminder that political stability depends on the ability to address public grievances before they are captured by extremist actors.
