Seoul’s Housing Divide Widens as Luxury Hits Records Despite Looming Rental Squeeze

October 25, 2026

While tighter lending restrictions have cooled headline price growth across Seoul, the capital faces an intensifying housing divide marked by record luxury sales and an imminent rental crunch driven by a severe supply drought.

  • Diverging prices: Weekly apartment price growth hit a yearly low due to strict debt-service caps, yet ultra-luxury units in the affluent Banpo district sold for record highs of 12 billion won (about $8.9 million), completely decoupled from credit curbs.
  • A looming rental cliff: New apartment move-ins in Seoul are projected to drop to historic lows—an echo of soaring construction costs and delayed permits during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure—coinciding with roughly 50,000 households forced to relocate due to urban redevelopment projects.
  • Pressure on outer boroughs: Working-class northeastern districts, including Nowon, Dobong, and Gangbuk, logged cumulative 10% gains in sale and jeonse (two-year lump-sum lease) prices, shutting out younger, first-time buyers who cannot access major credit.
  • Political friction over supply: Freshly cleared of election law charges on appeal, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has demanded deregulation for urban renewal, directly challenging the macroeconomic tightening and anti-speculation focus of President Lee Jae-myung's administration.

A Market Split by Cash and Credit

The statistical slowdown reported by KB Real Estate masks a fractured reality. Government tightening measures—notably the stricter "Stress DSR" mortgage caps—have paralyzed standard transactions and forced average salaried workers into a wait-and-see stance. However, super-prime real estate operates in a separate orbit. Wealthy buyers paying entirely in cash continue to bid up flagship riverfront complexes, leaving middle-class aspirants with a sense of relative deprivation as the wealth gap hardens into real estate tiering.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, the burden has fallen squarely on entry-level neighborhoods. Frustrated buyers who panicked during earlier price surges pushed up prices in peripheral districts by 10%, exhausting the last affordable rungs of Seoul's housing ladder. For young Koreans in their twenties and thirties, the prospect of building equity is slipping further out of reach.

The Approaching Rental Bottleneck

The most immediate systemic risk is not home purchases, but leasing. Next year's drop in new completions leaves the rental market without a buffer just as 50,000 households are displaced from demolished redevelopment sites. Inevitably, this will funnel displaced tenants into neighboring villas and older apartments, triggering cascading rent increases across the metropolitan area.

This dynamic sets up a high-stakes standoff between the central government and the Seoul municipal government. Mayor Oh advocates fast-tracking private reconstruction to unleash supply, but such moves risk fueling short-term speculation. Meanwhile, President Lee's government, recently buoyed by an approval rebound to the 40% mark, faces mounting public anxiety over domestic lending curbs while foreign purchases—such as Chinese investors owning over 60,000 properties—draw conservative scrutiny. Without a coordinated plan to stagger rehousing and stabilize rental supply, Seoul's low- and middle-income tenants face a bruising year ahead.
