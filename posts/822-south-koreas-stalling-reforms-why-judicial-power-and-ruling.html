South Korea's Stalling Reforms: Why Judicial Power and Ruling Party Infighting are Damaging Public Trust

October 08, 2026

South Korea's reform agenda under President Lee Jae-myung is stalling amid sagging approval ratings, widening public distrust in the judiciary, and a growing rift within the ruling party over how to handle dissent.

  • What happened: President Lee’s approval ratings have slipped again despite a brief post-press-conference bump, while a South Korean court handed down a controversial not-guilty verdict on appeal for imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol in a trial involving a political broker.
  • Judicial backlash: Public frustration has intensified following revelations that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and top court officials allegedly misused public funds for expensive meals and wine, fueling anger over a self-policing judiciary.
  • Ruling party friction: President Lee and senior ruling party figures have publicly targeted internal critics and online commentators as "extremists," drawing sharp rebukes from within the party for attempting to muzzle grassroots supporters.
  • Who is affected: The progressive ruling Democratic Party risks alienating its core base just as public pressure mounts for deep structural changes to the legal system.

The Crisis of Judicial Impunity and Stalled Reform

The core of South Korea's current political malaise lies in an unreformed, imperial judicial system that operates above public accountability. Civil society groups have long pointed out that the concentration of administrative power under the Chief Justice—epitomized by the powerful National Court Administration—has repeatedly shielded judges from self-correction and enabled past judicial scandals. Yet, lawmakers in Yeouido, Seoul's political district, have shown little appetite to dismantle these entrenched privileges. Calls to abolish the National Court Administration, establish an independent judicial commission to handle judge appointments, and expand citizen-participation trials modeled after Western jury systems have gone largely ignored.

This institutional insulation was laid bare when disclosures from parliamentary audits revealed top court leadership spending six-figure sums in won on discretionary activities and wine, even as ordinary citizens struggle economically. When the judiciary's top officials casually dodge accountability, it reinforces the public perception of the courts as an untouchable "sacred family." That frustration spilled over into the courts themselves, where an appellate court overturned a lower court's guilty verdict against Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment—sparking widespread outcry over inconsistent legal standards for powerful figures.

Top-Down Control and the Backlash Against Internal Dissent

Compounding these systemic legal issues is a self-inflicted communications crisis within the ruling camp. Rather than leaning into structural reforms to win back public confidence, President Lee and senior Democratic Party officials have resorted to aggressive rhetoric against critics. President Lee recently used social media to blast internal dissenters and online critics as "irresponsible extremists," while party leaders have openly suggested reining in grassroots voices that challenge leadership lines.

Labeling legitimate internal criticism as extremism is a heavy-handed strategy that alienates the very voters who brought the government to power. Pushing back against progressive reformers while treating the privileged legal establishment with kid-gloves signals a dangerous drift from the administration's founding mandate. Without a shift toward genuine public engagement and uncompromising institutional reform, the current administration risks squandering its political capital and turning popular fatigue into a systemic liability.
