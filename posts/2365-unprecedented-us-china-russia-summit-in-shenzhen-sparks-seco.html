Unprecedented US-China-Russia Summit in Shenzhen Sparks 'Second Yalta' Fears

November 16, 2026

US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin will hold a historic trilateral summit in Shenzhen on November 19, igniting intense global diplomatic anxiety over potential great-power dealmaking behind closed doors.

  • The Event: Moscow officially confirmed the first-ever three-way meeting among the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia, set to take place on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen.
  • The Agenda: Global security, nuclear arms control, military AI safeguards, and terms for ending the war in Ukraine will dominate discussions, with Trump reportedly agreeing to set aside corporate semiconductor friction over Nvidia for the meeting.
  • The Stakes: Ukraine and Taiwan fear their territorial integrity could be traded off in a great-power bargain, while South Korea faces the threat of "Korea Passing"—being sidelined on decisions regarding North Korea's nuclear program.
  • The Reality: Despite the historic setting, conflicting national interests mean a binding global pact remains unlikely, as each leader brings incompatible strategic objectives to the table.

Three Leaders, Three Divergent Calculations

Despite the grand backdrop in China's technology hub of Shenzhen, the three powers arrive with starkly different motivations. President Trump aims to drag Beijing into a trilateral nuclear arms control framework, seeking to cap China's rapidly expanding nuclear arsenal. President Putin views the summit as a crucial opportunity to break out of Western isolation, demonstrate Russia's superpower status, and secure favorable terms on Ukraine in exchange for strategic cooperation. Meanwhile, President Xi Jinping finds himself in a delicate position: while hosting the event, Beijing fears that joining multi-lateral arms reduction talks will lock in US and Russian strategic advantages while restricting China's military modernization.

The Shadow of Yalta and South Korea's High-Stakes Test

The summit has raised fears of a "Second Yalta"—a reference to the 1945 conference where WWII Allies carved up spheres of influence without consulting smaller nations. For South Korea, the primary risk is that the three powers might compromise on North Korea's nuclear program by accepting a freeze on arms development rather than enforcing complete denuclearization, effectively recognizing Pyongyang's nuclear state status to keep regional stability intact.

This places South Korean President Lee Jae-myung under significant diplomatic pressure ahead of APEC. To protect the nation's security interests, the Lee administration must actively engage in multilateral diplomacy during the summit week to ensure that South Korea is not excluded from crucial regional decisions and to maintain strategic autonomy amid shifting global power lines.
