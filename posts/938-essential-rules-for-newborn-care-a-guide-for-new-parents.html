Essential Rules for Newborn Care: A Guide for New Parents

October 10, 2026

  • Protecting immunity: Newborns have underdeveloped immune systems; avoid kissing them to prevent the transmission of the herpes simplex virus (HSV-1), which can lead to severe complications like encephalitis.
  • Hydration myths: Breast milk and formula provide sufficient hydration; giving newborns plain water is unnecessary and poses hygiene risks, such as gastroenteritis from contaminated water.
  • Safe sleep and development: To prevent SIDS (Sudden Infant Death Syndrome), keep cribs bare—no pillows or heavy blankets—and ensure mattresses are firm. Utilize "tummy time" under supervision to aid muscle development.
  • Digital assistance: Modern parents increasingly rely on specialized apps like BabyTime to track routines and Dokdak to manage pediatric hospital visits, streamlining the complexities of early childcare.

The Delicate Balance of Newborn Health

Newborn care in South Korea often feels like a steep learning curve, filled with conflicting advice from previous generations and modern medical guidelines. Among the most critical, yet often ignored, warnings is the danger of "affectionate" contact. While it may seem harmless, kissing a newborn can transmit the herpes virus, even if the adult shows no visible symptoms. This is why medical experts insist that parents set firm boundaries with visitors, regardless of how awkward it may feel.

Similarly, hydration habits are a frequent point of contention. Older generations often suggest giving water to newborns to stop hiccups, but pediatricians strongly advise against this. Because a newborn’s kidneys are still developing and their primary nutrition—breast milk or formula—is already 90% water, extra fluid is not only unnecessary but potentially dangerous due to the risk of bacterial contamination.

Infrastructure for a Healthy Start

Physical development requires active management. Tummy time is essential for strengthening the neck and shoulder muscles, which are necessary to support a baby's proportionally heavy head. However, this must be done safely on firm surfaces and under constant supervision to prevent suffocation or injury. Proper "head shaping" is equally vital; alternating the baby's sleeping and holding positions is the best way to prevent flat-head syndrome (plagiocephaly or brachycephaly) before it requires expensive medical intervention like corrective helmets.

Beyond physical care, the modern South Korean parent is increasingly turning to digital solutions to manage the exhaustion of the first few months. From apps that synchronize care schedules between parents to platforms that handle the "hospital war"—the scramble to book pediatric appointments online before they fill up—technology is becoming an indispensable parenting tool. Ultimately, while the transition to parenthood is a rigorous journey, adhering to these scientific safety standards ensures that the focus remains where it belongs: on the healthy development of the child.
