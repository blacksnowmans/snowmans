How to Cull Fake Friends and Survive South Korea’s Age of Outrage

October 10, 2026

As South Koreans age, a familiar cultural exhaustion sets in: the realization that maintaining a sprawling social circle is mostly an exercise in hollow emotional labor, leading many to drop acquaintances in favor of a tightly edited few.

  • The core test: According to prominent South Korean public intellectual and writer Rhyu Si-min, appearing polite to your face means nothing if a person treats service workers or strangers poorly.
  • The workplace toll: Workplace stress data shows that internal office relationships drive up employee burnout—especially during economic downturns, where interpersonal friction accounts for up to 27.7% of workplace fatigue.
  • The digital mirror: Online hate comments and toxic "cancel culture" reflect only the deep-seated insecurities and lack of self-esteem of the trolls, not the worth of the targets.
  • The takeaway: Stop wasting energy trying to fix toxic people or winning over bad-faith critics; instead, invest your limited time in reciprocal, respectful relationships.

Rhyu’s recent reflections on a popular South Korean YouTube talk show strike a nerve because they diagnose a wider cultural malaise. In a hyper-connected society driven by fierce competition and online echo chambers, everyday interactions often feel transactional. The Korean workplace, notoriously hierarchical and demanding, compounds this exhaustion, forcing employees to wear social masks that spill over into chronic burnout. When the pressure mounts, the superficial networks we built to "get ahead" suddenly look less like lifelines and more like anchors.

What makes Rhyu’s perspective sharp is his insistence that true character is revealed at the bottom, not the top. How someone treats a waiter—someone who holds no immediate leverage over their career or social standing—is a far more accurate barometer of their empathy than how nicely they smile at their boss. The pervasive toxicity found in Korean internet comments and polarized political debates stems from the same root cause: individuals suffering from deep-seated inferiority complexes who try to inflate their own fragile egos by tearing others down.

Ultimately, navigating modern life requires shedding the exhausting delusion that we must be liked by everyone. As Rhyu suggests, true self-awareness isn't found by hunting for some hidden identity; it is forged by consciously choosing how we act, who we keep close, and what we read. In a culture obsessed with status and public approval, the most radical act is simply walking away from the noise to share a quiet meal with someone who actually accepts you as you are.
