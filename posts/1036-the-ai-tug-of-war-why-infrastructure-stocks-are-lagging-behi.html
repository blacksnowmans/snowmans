The AI Tug-of-War: Why Infrastructure Stocks Are Lagging Behind Big Tech

October 12, 2026

The current divergence between soaring US "hyperscalers" and struggling South Korean semiconductor giants is not a sign of a structural breakdown, but rather a temporary market disconnect in an inseparable ecosystem.

  • What happened: While tech giants like Microsoft, Meta, Google, and Amazon hit new highs, infrastructure-heavy firms—specifically Nvidia, Micron, and South Korea’s chip titans Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—have faced significant sell-offs.
  • The catalyst: Recent volatility was triggered by "noise" regarding OpenAI’s revenue accounting, which caused temporary panic, coupled with ongoing foreign divestment from the Korean stock market.
  • The reality: The market is currently separating "platforms" (the flowers) from "infrastructure" (the soil), but the former cannot exist without the latter.

Infrastructure vs. Hyperscalers: A Bound Destiny

The market is currently fixated on a narrative that favors "hyperscalers"—the platforms providing AI services—over the infrastructure providers building the hardware. While software-focused firms are seeing 2–4% daily gains, hardware leaders are being sidelined. This is a classic market cycle of rotation, often exacerbated by extreme volatility that feeds on investor fear. However, it is a mistake to view these as competing interests; they are a "bound destiny." Without the physical compute power provided by companies like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, there is no AI-driven revenue growth for the software giants.

Recent confusion regarding OpenAI’s revenue reporting—initially reported as $5 billion but later clarified as $70 billion—highlighted how jittery the market is regarding AI-related numbers. This was a accounting technicality, not a fundamental shift in business prospects. Yet, this "noise" prompted an immediate flight from infrastructure stocks, revealing a market that is currently hypersensitive to headline data rather than long-term productivity gains.

The China Factor and the Path Forward

There is growing anxiety regarding China’s AI model development and potential chip self-sufficiency. However, even if China achieves a lead in token generation, the demand for physical AI factories—the data centers, high-bandwidth memory (HBM), cooling systems, and power grids—remains universal. Whether a model is built in the US or China, the physical infrastructure requirements are non-negotiable. US export controls are designed to buy time for the US and its allies to solidify their competitive edge, not to halt global infrastructure expansion.

For Korean investors, the recent foreign sell-off has been painful, yet the fundamentals remain strong. Samsung Electronics, despite achieving massive annual operating profits of about $77 billion (107 trillion KRW), is currently facing an unfairly skeptical market. As we approach the Q3 earnings reports for Samsung and SK Hynix, the focus should shift toward concrete guidance and shareholder return policies. Once the current obsession with high-valuation tech platforms cools, the massive valuation gap between those trading at 30x P/E and infrastructure firms at 4–7x P/E will inevitably narrow. Patience, rather than panic, is the only logical strategy in this climate.
