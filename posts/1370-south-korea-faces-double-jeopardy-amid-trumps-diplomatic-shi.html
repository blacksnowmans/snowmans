South Korea Faces Double Jeopardy Amid Trump’s Diplomatic Shifts and Global Trade Wars

October 21, 2026

As Donald Trump pivots toward a "Great Power" deal with Russia and China, South Korea’s Lee Jae-myung administration is struggling to navigate a fragmenting global order and a resurgent protectionist Europe.

  • Trump’s "Yalta 2.0" Vision: The former U.S. President is signaling a preference for a trilateral summit with Russia and China over a bilateral meeting with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, potentially sidelining Seoul’s security interests.
  • European Protectionism: The EU, led by France and Germany, is shifting toward "market blocking" strategies and strict environmental regulations to counter Chinese exports, threatening to squeeze South Korean manufacturers.
  • Middle East Volatility: Israel’s expansion of the "Gaza model" into Lebanon is drawing the U.S. into a deeper regional conflict just days before the American election, complicates global energy markets.
  • Domestic Diplomatic Repair: President Lee Jae-myung is attempting to "normalize" foreign policy following the damage caused during the administration of the now-imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol.

The Great Power Realignment and the "America First" Shadow

Donald Trump’s recent ambivalence regarding a potential meeting with Kim Jong Un marks a significant departure from his previous diplomatic showmanship. Instead, his focus has shifted to a "Yalta 2.0" concept—a trilateral negotiation between the U.S., Russia, and China to carve out spheres of influence. This "Great Power Club" approach is inherently dangerous for South Korea; it risks a scenario where the peninsula’s fate is decided by global titans without Seoul at the table. Trump’s increasingly cold shoulder toward Ukrainian President Zelenskyy further underscores his willingness to sacrifice ally interests for a quick deal with Moscow, a precedent that keeps Korean strategists awake at night.

This shift comes at a time when the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative party) is struggling to maintain its hawkish relevance while the current Lee Jae-myung government attempts to restore a "balancer" role in Northeast Asia. The challenge is compounded by Japan’s continued rightward lurch under Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, whose recent ritual offering to the Yasukuni Shrine—seen as a symbol of past militarism—reminds Seoul that historical tensions remain a barrier to a unified regional front against North Korean provocations.

Trade Barriers and the Cost of Regressive Policies

On the economic front, South Korea is being hammered by a new wave of "Green Protectionism." The European Union is deploying the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) and carbon neutrality projects—backed by about $3.7 billion (3.4 billion euros) in subsidies—as sophisticated trade barriers. While Korean companies like KGM (formerly SsangYong Motor) are racing to manage supply chain emissions, many small and medium-sized enterprises are ill-equipped for this transition.

The Lee Jae-myung administration is currently paying the "bill" for the era of Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol. During his tenure, Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol’s decision to retreat from renewable energy targets left Korean industry vulnerable to the very carbon regulations now being enforced by the EU. With Japan securing massive investments—such as a $38 billion (53 trillion won) deal from Blackstone—and China focusing on manufacturing exports to offset domestic stagnation, South Korea must pivot rapidly toward technological leadership to avoid being "sandwiched" in a world where the old rules of free trade no longer apply.

The Stakes for Seoul

"In an era where alliances are being recalculated as business transactions, South Korea cannot afford to rely on the diplomatic scripts of the past. The cost of the previous administration's failures is now a matter of national economic survival."
