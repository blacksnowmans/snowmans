South Korea’s Top Scorer Confesses to Cheating with Generative AI on Professional Licensing Exam

November 15, 2026

A top-scoring candidate for South Korea’s national tax accountant exam has surrendered to police after admitting to using micro-cameras and generative AI to cheat his way to a near-perfect score.

  • The suspect, a man in his 30s identified as Han, recorded an unprecedented 100% on the Tax Law section—a subject where 74% of applicants failed and the average score was just 30.18.
  • Han utilized a hidden micro-camera to transmit exam questions to an external AI system, receiving answers in real-time through a mobile device.
  • The confession included an admission of similar cheating during the first round of the Certified Public Accountant (CPA) exam.
  • The Ministry of Economy and Finance (South Korea's top fiscal agency) has suspended Han’s passing status while police investigate charges of obstruction of justice and business interference.
  • The case has exposed massive vulnerabilities in national exam security, as Han successfully cheated while seated in the very front row, directly under the eyes of proctors.

The "impossible" score was what ultimately brought the scheme down. In a subject as complex as tax law, which requires nuanced legal interpretation and multi-step calculations, a perfect score is statistically an anomaly. When Han’s results were published, veteran tax accountants and instructors immediately flagged the performance as "inhuman." The resulting pressure from the testing community and an official inquiry by the Korean Association of Certified Public Tax Accountants (the professional body representing the industry) eventually forced Han to confess.

This incident is a wake-up call for the Lee Jae-myung administration and testing authorities. For decades, South Korea’s high-stakes exams have relied on analog proctoring and the "honor system" for electronic devices. This case proves that human supervision is no longer a match for miniaturized AI technology. With the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT)—the country’s most critical university entrance exam—approaching, the government is now scrambling to deploy signal detectors and specialized security personnel to prevent "AI-assisted" academic fraud.

The Tech Behind the Fraud

Contrary to initial rumors of "smart glasses," Han employed a more discreet combination of a micro-camera, a smartphone, and a remote generative AI setup. By capturing images of the exam paper and feeding them to an AI model capable of solving complex tax problems in seconds, Han was able to simply transcribe the output. He admitted to the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency that he turned to cheating due to the economic pressure of being a long-term student and a desperate desire to "become his parents' pride."

The fallout has also reached the CPA exams. Han confessed that his previous success in the first round of the accounting certification was also fraudulent. This suggests that the vulnerability is not isolated to a single agency but is a systemic weakness across South Korea’s professional licensing framework. Investigators are now performing digital forensics on Han’s equipment to determine if he had external accomplices or if he utilized a pre-programmed AI "bot" designed specifically for Korean law.

A Crisis of Trust for Professional Licenses

The scandal has had a secondary victim: the actual second-place finisher, identified as Yoon. Because Han’s score was so high, Yoon—who achieved a legitimate high score through years of study—found himself under a cloud of suspicion from the online testing community. Yoon was forced to upload photos of his tattered textbooks and videos of himself solving problems in real-time to prove his innocence. Following Han's confession, the public has begun calling Yoon the "rightful top scorer."

The Human Resources Development Service of Korea, which administers the exam, is now under fire for allowing Han to operate electronic devices while sitting in the front row during both the first and second rounds of the test. While Han faces a five-year ban from state exams and potential prison time for obstruction of justice, the broader challenge remains: how to maintain the "fairness" that South Korean society prizes when the tools of deception are becoming invisible. If the government cannot guarantee the integrity of these licenses, the professional authority of the nation's accountants, lawyers, and doctors could be permanently diminished.
