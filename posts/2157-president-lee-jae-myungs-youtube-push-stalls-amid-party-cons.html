President Lee Jae-myung’s YouTube Push Stalls Amid Party Conspiracy Scandal

November 10, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s attempt to bypass traditional media through a new YouTube channel is backfiring as his own Democratic Party becomes mired in a self-inflicted conspiracy scandal involving a controversial religious sect.

  • One-Way Communication: President Lee launched "The President’s Y," a YouTube talk show intended to explain the "why" behind his policies, but critics argue the pre-recorded format avoids the "uncomfortable questions" necessary for true democratic accountability.
  • The Shincheonji Ghost: The ruling Democratic Party (DP) is struggling with the fallout of claims that Shincheonji—a religious group often described as a cult—massively infiltrated the party to rig the recent leadership election.
  • Exaggerated Threats: Internal investigations debunked the "secret alliance" narrative; while rumors suggested up to 50,000 infiltrators, the actual number of suspicious voters was found to be just 3,300, or 0.4% of the total.
  • Leadership Crisis: DP Chairman Kim Min-seok faces intense pressure from within his own party to provide a sincere apology for using these baseless allegations to attack rivals during his campaign for the chairmanship.

The Illusion of Direct Communication

President Lee has long preferred direct digital engagement, frequently posting on X (formerly Twitter). However, the launch of a dedicated YouTube studio—funded by the state—marks a shift toward a more controlled, "broadcaster-in-chief" style of governance. By retreating into a studio with pre-selected questions, the President risks creating an echo chamber that replaces genuine public discourse with polished propaganda. This strategy ignores the reality that the public’s dissatisfaction often stems from policy outcomes—such as stagnant housing issues—rather than a lack of "explanation."

This digital-first approach also undermines the role of the traditional press and official government spokespeople. When the head of state treats policy debate as a curated talk show, it signals to the bureaucracy that appearances matter more than the grit of field-based governance. As the opposition People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) pointed out, the President was elected to lead a nation, not to become a professional YouTuber.

A Party Paralyzed by Internal Politics

While the President focuses on his digital brand, the ruling Democratic Party is eating itself from the inside. The "Shincheonji infiltration" narrative was a potent weapon during the leadership race, used to brand opponents as cult collaborators. Now that the data proves the threat was negligible, the party leadership is in a defensive crouch. The decision to allow lawmakers only a 30-minute, "no-notes" private viewing of the investigation report has only heightened suspicions of a cover-up.

The danger for the Lee administration is that this internal friction is wasting the "golden time" for judicial and economic reforms. If the ruling party remains obsessed with clearing its name from its own conspiracy theories, it loses the moral authority to lead the country. True communication requires more than a high-definition camera; it requires the courage to face critics and the integrity to admit when political rhetoric has crossed the line into misinformation.
