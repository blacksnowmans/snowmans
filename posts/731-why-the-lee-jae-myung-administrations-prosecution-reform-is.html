Why the Lee Jae-myung Administration’s Prosecution Reform is Backfiring

September 13, 2026

The Lee Jae-myung administration, which rose to power on promises of curbing the influence of South Korea’s powerful prosecution service, now faces accusations that its reform efforts are not dismantling the system but merely consolidating it through compromise.

  • The Policy Gap: Despite campaigning on the total separation of investigation and prosecution powers, the administration has increasingly filled key Ministry of Justice positions with career prosecutors.
  • The "New Prosecution": Plans for a new "Major Crimes Investigation Agency" are being criticized as a rebranding exercise that retains the core structure and personnel of the existing prosecution, rather than a true reform.
  • The "Legal Risk" Factor: Observers argue that the administration’s conciliatory approach stems from a fear of judicial retaliation against its leaders after they leave office, leading to a "truce" that ultimately weakens the government's hand.

The Paradox of "Reformers" Cozying Up to Prosecutors

The Lee Jae-myung government, representing the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, promised to "de-prosecutorialize" the Ministry of Justice. However, the reality has been the opposite. By appointing seasoned prosecutors to high-ranking posts, the administration has arguably invited the logic of the prosecution back into the heart of national administration. Rather than breaking the "prosecutorial cartel," the government appears to be attempting to appease it to ensure political survival.

This strategy is widely viewed as a "self-defeating trap." Investigative journalist Jang In-soo recently highlighted the dangers of the government’s behind-the-scenes maneuvering—specifically regarding the dropping of certain charges—which critics warn provides the prosecution with the perfect grounds to accuse the administration of "abuse of power." By treating judicial reform as a matter of personal negotiation rather than a structural overhaul, the administration is inadvertently giving the prosecution the very evidence it needs to strike back.

Structural Reform or Political Theater?

The proposed creation of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency is the centerpiece of the administration’s reform agenda, but it faces harsh criticism for being a "clone" of the existing system. The selection committee for the agency’s leadership is reportedly packed with figures who played key roles during the previous administration of Yoon Suk-yeol, a former Prosecutor General turned President. As long as these core structures—such as the power to maintain charges and command investigations—remain intact, critics argue that changing the agency’s name is meaningless.

The underlying issue is a fear of the "post-retirement trap." Former South Korean presidents have frequently faced aggressive investigations after leaving office, and the current administration seems paralyzed by this historical cycle. By prioritizing short-term stability over necessary institutional changes, the government is missing its window to weaken the prosecution’s absolute grip on power. True reform requires the political courage to dismantle the prosecution’s institutional monopoly, but as long as the administration views the prosecution as a partner to be managed rather than a system to be reformed, the status quo will remain unchanged.
