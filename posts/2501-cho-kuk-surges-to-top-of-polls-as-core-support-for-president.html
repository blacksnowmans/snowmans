Cho Kuk Surges to Top of Polls as Core Support for President Lee Jae-myung Erodes

November 19, 2026

Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, has claimed the top spot in a recent presidential preference poll among liberal voters, signaling a significant fracture within South Korea’s ruling progressive bloc.

  • A New Frontrunner: Cho Kuk reached 19.1% in a Jowon C&I poll conducted November 16–17, 2026, surpassing senior figures in the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) including party leader Kim Min-seok (16.1%).
  • Core Collapse: Support for the DPK has plummeted to the 20% range, with its bedrock demographic—voters in their 40s—seeing a massive 17-point drop in support in just one week.
  • Administrative Hardball: Prime Minister Han Seong-sook has emerged as a key administrative fixer, mobilizing 8,400 personnel to police the upcoming Suneung, South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam, amid fears of high-tech cheating.
  • Practical Backlash: Despite the government’s "total war" on exam fraud, teachers and students have criticized the measures—such as inspecting clothing buttons and glasses—as "desk-bound administration" that is impossible to implement.

The Rise of an Alternative and the Progressive Split

The "Cho Kuk Phenomenon" has evolved from a niche movement into a major political threat to the incumbent administration of President Lee Jae-myung. The poll results from Jowon C&I are being described as a "nuclear bomb" for the ruling camp, showing that the core progressive base is no longer satisfied with the DPK’s leadership. This shift is driven by a perception that the government has failed to deliver on bold reforms, instead becoming bogged down in internal factional infighting and controversial appointments, such as the nomination of Kim Ji-yong to lead the Central Investigation Agency.

The Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor liberal party founded by Cho, is successfully absorbing "disappointed loyalists" who feel President Lee has lost his reformist edge. This is a dangerous development for the DPK; historically, the liberal bloc wins only when its core remains unified. With the main opposition People Power Party (the conservative party) currently leading in general polls at 41.3%, the fragmentation of the liberal vote between President Lee’s camp and Cho Kuk’s supporters could lead to a crushing defeat for the DPK in upcoming elections.

Administrative Execution vs. Public Mockery

Amidst this political volatility, Prime Minister Han Seong-sook is carving out a unique profile. A former executive at Naver, South Korea’s dominant search engine and tech giant, Han is leaning on her reputation for "execution" to handle the security of the Suneung exam. She has threatened cheaters with up to ten years of disqualification and criminal charges for complicit officials. In a vacuum of clear successors within the DPK, Han’s decisive administrative style is positioning her as a potential alternative, though she remains a polarizing figure.

However, the government's heavy-handedness is meeting stiff resistance on the ground. The plan to use 8,400 staff to inspect thousands of students’ sleeves and buttons for hidden cameras in a 30-minute window has been labeled a "fantasy" by teachers' unions. Educators fear that physical inspections of students could lead to sexual harassment lawsuits, while parents are mocking the government for focusing on "button stickers" rather than high-tech signal jamming. This gap between the Prime Minister’s "perfect" administrative plan and the reality of the classroom highlights a broader criticism of the Lee Jae-myung administration: that it is increasingly isolated and out of touch with the very people it claims to represent.
