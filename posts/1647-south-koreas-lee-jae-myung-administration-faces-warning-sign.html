South Korea’s Lee Jae-myung Administration Faces Warning Signs as Approval Ratings Slide

October 29, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has fallen into the mid-40s—and down to 35% in some polling—triggering alarm bells for the ruling liberal administration’s reform momentum and exposing growing fractures within its political base.

  • What happened: Recent polling shows President Lee’s approval dropping to 46% in Gallup and 35% in Realmeter, with disapproval ratings surging past 60% in automated tracking.
  • What it changes: Falling support threatens to stall key reform initiatives, as high disapproval erodes the public mandate needed to overcome opposition resistance in the National Assembly.
  • How it compares: At the same point in office, former liberal President Moon Jae-in enjoyed a 75% approval rating, while Lee's current trajectory uncomfortably mirrors the persistent 35% lows seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term prior to his impeachment and imprisonment.
  • Who is affected: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea and its network of political commentators, who are struggling with internal factional divisions and declining voter confidence.

The Anatomy of the Polling Slump

While an approval rating of 46% might appear manageable, for the Democratic Party of Korea—South Korea’s ruling liberal party—it represents a fragile foundation. When former President Moon Jae-in reached this same milestone in his presidency, he commanded a 75% approval rating that provided immense momentum for major policy overhauls. By contrast, President Lee's numbers reflect a troubling downward trend. Telephone interview polls that logged approval in the mid-70s earlier in 2026 have given way to a persistent "dead cross," where disapproval consistently outpaces approval.

The situation appears even starker in tracking by pollster Realmeter, which places President Lee’s positive evaluation at 35% and disapproval at 62%. This structure closely resembles the persistent public distrust recorded during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term before his eventual impeachment. When disapproval solidifies above 60%, the public begins to question the motives behind government initiatives, drastically reducing policy effectiveness regardless of their intent.

Attitude Problems and Factional Fractures

Political analysts and liberal commentators point to President Lee’s personal leadership style as a primary factor driving the decline. In South Korea’s strong presidential system, executive governance inevitably reflects the individual leader's personal demeanor. Critics note that Lee’s drive for rapid execution has often been perceived as top-down and uncompromising rather than communicative, alienating voters who look for democratic consultation and empathy.

Equally dangerous for the administration is the internal dissolution of its supporting coalition. Prominent liberal commentators—including Kim Ou-joon, Rhyu Si-min, and Park Si-young—have voiced alarm over core supporters walking away. Infighting among party figures and competing media voices have created fatigue among voters, leading some supporters to argue that Democratic Party figures should strategically stay silent to avoid further damaging the president's numbers. If these internal divisions deepen, President Lee risks political isolation just as his legislative agenda faces its toughest tests.
