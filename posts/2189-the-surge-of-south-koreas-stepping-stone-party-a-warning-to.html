The Surge of South Korea’s "Stepping Stone" Party: A Warning to President Lee Jae-myung

November 10, 2026

The Cho Kuk Innovation Party’s rapid rise in polling signals a deep fracture within South Korea’s progressive base, as core supporters of President Lee Jae-myung seek a more aggressive alternative to the ruling Democratic Party’s perceived inertia.

  • President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has hit a record low of 34.7%, indicating that dissatisfaction has spread beyond the opposition to include the administration's own base.
  • The Cho Kuk Innovation Party (CKIP), led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, has surged to over 20% support among self-identified progressives, positioning itself as a "militant" alternative.
  • Public sentiment toward the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the current ruling party, has soured, with over 50% of voters labeling it the party they are most reluctant to support.
  • Commentators describe the CKIP as a "stepping stone" party—a temporary refuge for frustrated liberals who want to pressure the President to pursue more radical reforms.

A Crisis of Confidence in the Ruling Party

The recent polling data from Research View is a wake-up call for the Blue House. President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating dropping to 34.7% suggests that the "concrete" support base is beginning to crack. Particularly concerning for the administration is the flight of voters in their 50s—the demographic that typically forms the backbone of political stability in Korea. This demographic appears increasingly disillusioned by a perceived lack of progress in key policy areas, viewing the government’s approach as overly cautious or ineffective.

The rise of the CKIP is not a sign of voters abandoning the progressive camp, but rather a tactical migration. Progressive voters are seeking "efficacy." While the DPK holds a majority, its focus on parliamentary procedure and moderate consensus has frustrated "militant liberals" who desire a swifter "cleanup" of the prosecution and media. The CKIP has filled this void by recruiting high-profile figures like former prosecutor Park Eun-jung and lawyer Shin Jang-sik, who project a more combative stance against the establishment.

Lessons from the Past and the "Stepping Stone" Strategy

This internal shift mirrors the collapse of the conservative coalition during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Before his impeachment and subsequent imprisonment, Mr. Yoon dismantled his own support base by alienating key allies and narrowing his circle of influence. Analysts warn that President Lee faces a similar risk if the DPK continues to prioritize internal loyalty over broader public appeal. The "stepping stone" theory suggests that voters are currently using the CKIP to send a message: "Fix the party, or we will find a new home."

"The relationship between the CKIP and the DPK is like a shallow stream with stepping stones; voters can hop back and forth easily," notes progressive commentator Rhyu Si-min.

For the Lee administration, the path forward requires more than just new policy announcements. When trust in leadership is compromised, policies are often dismissed as mere populist theater. To win back the "stepping stone" voters, the DPK must demonstrate a return to its core values and show tangible results in judicial and social reforms. If the ruling party fails to consolidate its base before the next major electoral cycle, it risks losing the "icebreaker" momentum that the CKIP currently commands, potentially threatening the administration's ability to maintain a legislative majority.
