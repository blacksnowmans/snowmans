South Korea’s Entry-Level Job Freeze Is an AI Shock, Not Just an Economic Slump

October 26, 2026

South Korea's vanishing entry-level white-collar job market is being driven primarily by corporate adoption of generative AI rather than a standard macroeconomic downturn, according to an exhaustive analysis of 10 million job listings over the past seven years.

  • Plummeting junior tech openings: Entry-level software developer postings fell from 37.1% of all coding jobs in 2022 to 26.8% in 2024, with IT infrastructure junior roles dropping from 50.7% to 36.9% over the same period.
  • A white-collar anomaly: While youth under 29 enrolled in employment insurance plunged sharply across information technology, youth hiring in manufacturing and college-educated blue-collar roles held steady at around 75%.
  • Kicking away the ladder: Employers are substituting entry-level onboarding with advanced AI tools, steadily raising the minimum years of experience required in postings proportionally to a job's exposure to AI.
  • The perception divide: A survey of 1,700 adults revealed that while 46% use AI tools, only 8% pay for cutting-edge frontier models—meaning the general public and casual users severely underestimate the disruption already visible to high-end users and executives.

Data Exposes a Structural Shift, Not a Normal Recession

Corporate managers and older generations have largely blamed South Korea’s hiring freeze on post-pandemic inflation, high borrowing costs, and weak domestic demand. Yet the numbers tell a very different story. If broad macroeconomic headwinds were the real culprit, hiring reductions would be distributed evenly across all industries. Instead, entry-level openings in marketing, digital design, accounting, and software development cratered immediately after ChatGPT’s launch in late 2022, while blue-collar and factory roles remained unscathed.

Companies are no longer willing to shoulder the heavy cost and time required to train fresh university graduates. Routine white-collar tasks once assigned to juniors—such as writing boilerplate code, drafting baseline marketing copy, and cleaning data—are now generated by AI in seconds. Consequently, firms are shifting their headcounts almost exclusively toward mid-career professionals with three to five years of experience who can audit AI output and make executive decisions.

The Broken Career Ladder and Rising Inequality

This dynamic creates an immediate paradox for young job seekers: companies demand years of experience, but no one is offering the entry-level positions where that experience is gained. Research by the Korea Development Institute (KDI), a state-run economic think tank, indicates that nearly 39% of domestic jobs have the technical potential to automate more than 70% of their core tasks. Generative AI does not eliminate work outright, but it concentrates output into fewer, more experienced hands while vaporizing the entry points of professional careers.

The gap is compounded by unequal access to the technology itself. Wealthier students and elite professionals who can easily afford premium AI subscriptions develop advanced workflows that multiply their productivity, leaving ordinary graduates relying on free, low-performing models that mislead them about workplace realities. If South Korean policymakers treat this transformation as a temporary hiring slump rather than a fundamental rewiring of the knowledge economy, the country risks permanently dismantling the social mobility pipeline for its next generation.
