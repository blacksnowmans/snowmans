Why Living for Other People’s Approval is Ruining Your Relationships

September 11, 2026

  • What happened: Prominent South Korean Buddhist monk Pomnyun Sunim addressed the modern anxiety of constant self-editing and living for the approval of others.
  • The core trap: Trying to project a false, inflated image of oneself—whether in dating, school, or daily life—creates an exhausting cycle of anxiety and self-deception.
  • The takeaway: Authentic relationships require dropping the "false advertising," lowering expectations, and accepting oneself without the burden of constant performance.

We spend an exhausting amount of energy worrying about what other people think. From obsessing over an outfit to monitoring every word to avoid sounding foolish, modern life forces many of us to lock up our personalities like rigid robots. According to Pomnyun Sunim, a well-known Korean monk and spiritual teacher, this anxiety stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of why we care about the opinions of others in the first place.

The monk makes a sharp distinction between healthy consideration—mindfully wondering if your actions are genuinely inconveniencing someone else—and toxic self-presentation. The former flows from inner peace and mutual respect. The latter, however, is driven by a desire to be perceived as smarter, kinder, or more capable than you actually are. In essence, trying too hard to look good is a form of lying to the world, which inevitably leads to lying to yourself. When you chase praise you haven't earned, you become your own 24-hour guard, terrified that someone will finally see through the facade.

Stop Over-Promising in Relationships

This trap is most visible in romance and social media. People enter dating markets like corporate entities issuing overhyped prospectuses—hiding flaws, inflating achievements, and playing a character. But starting a relationship on inflated pretenses guarantees future resentment. When you present yourself as a 150 while operating at a 100, the other person will eventually feel cheated. Sunim compares this to a monk's training: novices are deliberately kept plain and humble for years so they are not crushed or distorted by premature fantasies and external validation.

Lowering your public profile on purpose isn't giving up; it's a strategic advantage. Going on a first date in flat shoes and a bare face, or accepting a modest rank rather than pretending to be at the top, leaves room for pleasant surprises and genuine growth. When people hold low expectations, you have the safety and space to build actual substance.

The Verdict

Ultimately, the desperate craving for external validation is a symptom of a weak internal foundation. When self-esteem falters, people try to fill the void with applause, turning human connection into a fragile house of cards. The solution is not to become recklessly inconsiderate of others, but to stop treating life like an endless theatrical performance. Letting go of the urge to look flawless is terrifying at first, but it is the only way to let the real self breathe.
