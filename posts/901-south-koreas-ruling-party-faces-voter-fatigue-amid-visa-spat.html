South Korea’s Ruling Party Faces Voter Fatigue Amid Visa Spat and Audit Clashes

October 10, 2026

South Korea’s main political parties remain locked in fierce partisan conflict over a delayed U.S. visa for a senior ruling party lawmaker, even as the annual parliamentary audit highlights pressing livelihood issues and sliding approval ratings for the government.

  • What happened: The U.S. visa delay for Jung Chung-rae, a prominent lawmaker with the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party), triggered intense political crossfire. While the conservative opposition People Power Party framed it as a sign of fraying U.S.-South Korea alliances under President Lee Jae-myung, the Foreign Ministry and the Democratic Party dismissed it as a routine, isolated administrative matter.
  • What it changes: The uproar underscores how quickly administrative red tape can be weaponized into high-stakes partisan warfare, overshadowing concurrent parliamentary audits into corporate safety and small business support.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung's administration and the Democratic Party, which both hit record-low approval ratings in recent polling, face mounting pressure to pivot away from petty political bickering and deliver tangible policy results for an increasingly exhausted public.

Partisan Battles Overthadow Livelihood Audits

The political friction over routine diplomatic paperwork reveals a troubling symptom of South Korea's current political landscape. Rather than treating the visa delay as a standard consular review—a view shared even by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs—the conservative opposition has seized on the issue to attack the Lee Jae-myung government's foreign policy competence. This aggressive posturing, critics note, risks self-inflicted damage to national diplomatic assets purely for short-term political point-scoring.

Yet away from the televised shouting matches, the ongoing parliamentary audits are unearthing critical social issues. Lawmakers from the Democratic Party have used the sessions to grill corporations over workplace safety, such as delivery driver overwork risks linked to Coupang, and to highlight steep cancellation rates for small business employment insurance following subsidy cuts. These are the substantive issues voters care about, contrasting sharply with the empty rhetoric dominating the headlines.

A Wake-Up Call on Falling Approval Ratings

Recent polling from Gallup Korea illustrates the political cost of this endless cycle of confrontation, placing the Democratic Party's approval rating at 35% and President Lee Jae-myung's support at 37%—both lows for the current administration. With a historic share of voters identifying as politically unaffiliated, public fatigue with partisan warfare has reached a breaking point.

The path forward for the ruling party is clear, though difficult. To reverse sliding support and rebuild trust, the Democratic Party must resist the trap of getting bogged down in superficial controversies. Instead, it must double down on policy competence and structural reforms that directly improve everyday lives. In an era where voters are rapidly tuning out of politics entirely, political survival depends less on winning shouting matches and much more on delivering real results.
