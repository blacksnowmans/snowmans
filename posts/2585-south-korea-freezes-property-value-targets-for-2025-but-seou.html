South Korea Freezes Property Value Targets for 2025, but Seoul Tax Bills Still Set to Soar

November 21, 2026

South Korea’s Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport has announced it will maintain the "realization rate"—the percentage of market value used to calculate property taxes—at 69% for the fifth year in a row.

  • The move by the administration of President Lee Jae-myung aims to prevent a sudden spike in the tax burden for homeowners amid ongoing discussions regarding broader tax reforms.
  • Despite the frozen rate, owners of expensive apartments in Seoul face tax increases of up to 50% because the underlying market prices in those districts have surged over the past year.
  • A significant equity gap persists between housing types, with detached houses appraised at only 53.6% of their market value compared to 69% for apartments.
  • The decision highlights a deepening divide between the booming luxury market in the capital and struggling regional housing markets in areas like Daegu.

The Illusion of a Frozen Tax Bill

For many homeowners, "frozen rates" might sound like "frozen taxes," but in the Korean system, the tax bill is a product of the market price multiplied by the realization rate. Because luxury complexes in Seoul’s "Gangnam 3-gu" (the wealthy districts of Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa) have seen record-breaking sales prices this year, their 2025 tax appraisals will jump regardless of the rate freeze. For example, a standard 84㎡ unit in the Banpo Xi complex in Seocho is expected to see a tax increase of about $6,000 (8.31 million won). In the ultra-luxury Hannam the Hill complex, the annual holding tax could reach a staggering $79,000 (110 million won)—an amount comparable to the annual salary of a high-earning corporate professional.

This creates a paradoxical situation where the government claims to be easing the tax burden while high-end property owners feel a "tax bomb" is dropping. The Lee Jae-myung administration is prioritizing "public receptivity," likely seeking to avoid the intense backlash seen during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, when rapid adjustments to realization rates combined with rising prices led to explosive growth in tax bills. By holding the rate steady, the government effectively shifts the responsibility for tax hikes onto market fluctuations rather than policy changes.

A Deepening Market Polarization

The freeze also exposes the widening chasm between Seoul and the rest of the country. While Seoul’s riverside districts are hitting new price ceilings, regional cities like Daegu and those in North Gyeongsang Province are struggling with a surplus of unsold homes and stagnant prices. In these regions, the 69% freeze means taxes will stay flat or even decrease, as market values have not moved. This reinforces the "one smart home" strategy, where investors sell off provincial holdings to focus on a single, high-value property in Seoul, further driving up prices in the capital while draining liquidity from the provinces.

The Ministry’s decision is also a tactical pause while the Democratic Party of Korea, the current ruling party, navigates complex legislative negotiations. With major reforms to the tax system still pending in the National Assembly, the government is hesitant to move the appraisal needle and risk further market volatility. For prospective buyers, the takeaway is clear: the headline "freeze" is a distraction. The real variable is the specific neighborhood’s market heat, which will dictate whether next year’s tax bill is a minor adjustment or a major financial blow.
