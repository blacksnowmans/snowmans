South Korea's Appeals Court Overturns Acquittal, Sentences Former First Lady to Four Years in Prison

September 30, 2026

    Kim Keon Hee, the wife of former South Korean President Yoon Suk Yeol, was sentenced to four years in prison and a fine of 50 million won (about $36,000) by an appeals court on multiple charges including stock manipulation and accepting illegal favors.
  • The appeals court overturned a previous lower court ruling, finding Kim guilty of violating the Capital Markets Act through her involvement in a 2010–2012 Deutsch Motors stock-manipulation scheme.
  • The court also convicted her of influence peddling (알선수재) regarding gifts and favors tied to the Unification Church during the presidential election campaign and afterward.
  • The ruling sends a strong judicial signal against white-collar crime and the abuse of proximity to presidential power, closing loopholes previously exploited by political figures' family members.

The appeals court's decision marks a watershed moment in South Korean jurisprudence regarding accountability at the highest levels of power. By combining the capital markets violations and the influence-peddling charges into a single comprehensive framework, the court rejected the piecemeal approach of the lower trial and evaluated the full scope of the offences.

The ruling directly addresses a long-standing point of public contention: whether the partners of powerful politicians are insulated by informal influence and legal loopholes. The court forcefully argued that utilizing presidential proximity for personal and organizational favors irreparably damages public trust in government and fuels deep national division. It also exposes the glaring inadequacies of existing anti-corruption statutes, such as the Graft Prohibition Act, which have historically struggled to penalize family members for accepting luxury items.

Market Integrity and the Myth of Impunity

Beyond the political scandal, the court took an unyielding stance on financial crimes. The judges detailed how thousands of manipulative orders and wash trades severely distorted fair pricing on the stock exchange, inflicting direct harm on ordinary retail investors. For years, critics have decried light sentences for white-collar criminals who turn public markets into speculative gambling dens. This verdict signals that the judiciary is no longer willing to treat market manipulation with leniency.

While the defense highlighted mitigating factors—such as Kim's lack of a major prior criminal record and her secondary, non-leadership role in the stock scheme—the court ultimately decided that these factors could not erase the gravity of the systemic harm caused. The mandatory confiscation of high-end jewelry and monetary forfeiture orders underscore that illicit gains tied to presidential proximity will be stripped away.
