South Korea's Selective Justice Under Fire as Police Open Door to Reinvestigating Han Dong-hoon's Daughter

October 12, 2026

South Korean police have signaled they may reopen an investigation into academic fraud allegations surrounding the daughter of Han Dong-hoon, a high-profile conservative politician, following intense questioning during a recent parliamentary audit.

  • What happened: The acting National Police Commissioner told lawmakers that police could reinvestigate allegations that Han's daughter used ghostwritten papers and fabricated credentials for college applications if new evidence emerges.
  • The core controversy: The initial 20-month police probe resulted in zero search warrants, zero witness interviews, and a single written questionnaire for the suspect, closing with no charges.
  • The double standard: Critics point out a stark contrast with aggressive past investigations targeting political rivals—such as a former justice minister's daughter, who faced over 70 search warrants.
  • What changes: The admission revives a politically sensitive scandal from the administration of impeached former leader Yoon Suk Yeol, putting pressure on law enforcement to prove they apply the law equally regardless of political power.

The handling of the allegations against Han's daughter has long been cited by critics as a textbook example of political shielding. While ordinary citizens and opposition figures face exhaustive raids and witness summonses, the initial investigation into Han—who served as a powerful justice minister during Yoon Suk Yeol's presidency—relied on a solitary written response from the accused. Compounding the public backlash, the National Office of Investigation previously claimed it had conducted numerous witness interviews, a statement flatly contradicted by records showing zero witnesses were ever called.

This glaring disparity damages public trust in South Korea's legal system. The scandal centers on high school-era academic entries, including an English-language paper attributed to a Kenyan ghostwriter named Benson, which critics argue required resources and connections far beyond a normal student's reach. For a public accustomed to hyper-competitive college admissions where ordinary students face rigorous scrutiny, the apparent lenitism granted to political elites exposes a profound fracture in the principle of equality before the law.
