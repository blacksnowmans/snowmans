South Korea's Prosecutors Are Losing Their Investigative Powers—Or So the Public Was Promised

September 09, 2026

  • The flashpoint: South Korea's Ministry of Justice has released a restructuring plan for the new Public Prosecution Service following the total separation of police investigation and prosecution rights.
  • What it changes: Critics argue the plan preserves the old investigative apparatus by retaining nearly 7,000 investigators and adding a new "Judicial Control Division" to oversee other agencies.
  • Who is affected: The move has sparked fierce pushback from reformists, led by figures like Rep. Kim Yong-min of the Democratic Party, who warn it creates an unchecked super-agency through bureaucratic fine print.
  • The bottom line: Rather than dismantling Korea's powerful prosecutorial cartel, critics say the new decree repackages the old guard under a new name.

A Bureaucratic Shell Game: Same Prosecutors, New Signage

South Korea’s long-sought overhaul of its criminal justice system was supposed to mark a historic shift. By splitting investigative and indicting powers, the reform aimed to strip the traditional prosecution service of its unchecked authority and return power to the public. But the Ministry of Justice’s newly proposed organizational decree threatens to undo those gains through administrative loopholes, proving that institutional muscle memory dies hard.

On paper, the government touts a 21.4% personnel reduction for the incoming Public Prosecution Service. Look closer, however, and the math tells a different story. Once investigators slated for transfer to the newly formed Major Crime Investigation Agency are subtracted, the actual staff reduction shrinks to a mere 238 positions. Roughly 7,000 criminal investigators remain firmly in place, alongside a request to increase active prosecutor positions. For an agency supposedly restricted strictly to indictments and trials, keeping an army of investigators makes little sense unless the goal is to maintain the infrastructure for back-channel probes.

The Return of the Cartel and the Danger of Executive Overreach

Even more alarming to critics is the creation of the "Judicial Control Division," a newly minted unit within local prosecution offices designed to review non-indictment cases. Granting the very institution that requires strict oversight the power to police other investigative bodies is a breathtaking reversal of logic. Paired with the appointment of former prosecutors to lead the new major crime and prosecution agencies, the restructuring risks cementing a "two roofs, one family" arrangement that shields the old legal establishment from genuine democratic accountability.

This is where executive decrees threaten to override legislative intent. By relying on internal restructuring rules rather than structural dismantling, the administration is bypassing the spirit of the law to keep old privileges intact. If the government refuses to hit pause on its rapid legislative timeline and fundamentally redesign these decrees, South Korea's hard-won prosecutorial reform risks becoming nothing more than a cosmetic rebranding.
