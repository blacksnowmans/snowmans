Internal Strife Paralyzes South Korea's Main Opposition Party

November 14, 2026

The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, is currently spiraling into a deep internal crisis fueled by allegations that disciplinary actions against former party leader Han Dong-hoon were rigged.

  • Allegations of Manipulation: Reports suggest that unauthorized "external figures" may have influenced the severity of disciplinary measures against former leader Han Dong-hoon.
  • Leadership Clash: Current PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok is facing a direct confrontation with the pro-Han faction, which is demanding a formal inquiry and threatening potential criminal charges.
  • Fractured Unity: The infighting has effectively paralyzed the party's ability to act as a check on the government of President Lee Jae-myung (of the ruling Democratic Party).
  • Political Realignment: In a defensive maneuver, Jang has sought an alliance with senior lawmaker Ahn Cheol-soo, a vocal critic of Han’s potential return to leadership, deepening the factional divide.

A Credibility Crisis

At the heart of the scandal is the suspicion that the PPP’s disciplinary system—the very mechanism meant to uphold internal standards—has been weaponized for factional warfare. For a party that identifies as the primary democratic alternative to the Lee Jae-myung government, the accusation that these processes were manipulated by shadowy external actors is devastating. If proven true, this constitutes not just a political scandal but a potential legal violation, including charges of obstruction of business.

Instead of addressing these concerns with transparency, the party leadership is attempting to dismiss them as "attacks on the leadership." By labeling internal dissent as "immoral" or "political maneuvering," leader Jang Dong-hyeok is failing to act as a unifying force. His recent, performative meetings with figures like Ahn Cheol-soo appear less like strategic governing and more like a desperate attempt to consolidate power against the pro-Han wing. This strategy has backfired, fueling public perception—with disapproval ratings for the leadership already exceeding 60%—that the party is more interested in its own survival than in serving the public.

The Cost of Infighting

The PPP currently finds itself in a state of "psychological fracture." While the party is busy squabbling over internal disciplinary records and trading accusations of "immoral politics," the crucial role of the opposition in holding the current administration accountable has all but vanished.

The ongoing power struggle suggests a bleak path ahead. As the party awaits court rulings on several of its key members, the leadership’s reliance on aggressive, accusatory rhetoric rather than policy alternatives is alienating the very voters they need to recover. Without a swift, transparent investigation into the alleged manipulation, the People Power Party risks sliding further into irrelevance, leaving a vacuum in South Korean politics that a functioning democracy cannot afford.
