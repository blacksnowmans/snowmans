Why Splitting Your Home Title with Your Spouse Could Cost You a Fortune in South Korea

September 02, 2026

  • The default assumption: Putting a South Korean home in both spouses' names is widely treated as a no-brainer for cutting taxes, but the math is rarely that simple.
  • The property value trap: While co-ownership shines for homes valued between about $1 million and $1.4 million (₩1.4 billion to ₩1.9 billion), single-ownership often wins out for properties worth $1.4 million to $2.3 million (₩1.9 billion to ₩3.2 billion) thanks to hefty long-term holding deductions.
  • Hidden costs: Focusing solely on future capital gains tax while ignoring annual property taxes, potential gift taxes on asset transfers, and the risk of losing dependent health insurance status can wipe out any expected savings.

Among South Korean couples, putting a new home in both names is practically a reflex. The logic sounds bulletproof: split the asset, lower the tax bracket, and split capital gains allowances when it's time to sell. But as property tax bills land and complex tax brackets kick in, thousands of homeowners realize too late that blindly following the crowd leads straight to a nasty tax surprise. The reality of South Korea's real estate tax code is that small print matters far more than social trends.

The core issue lies in how different thresholds interact. For instance, South Korea's comprehensive real estate holding tax treats homes differently depending on official appraised values. While co-ownership expands tax-free deductions up to about $1.3 million (₩1.8 billion) for married couples, properties valued past $1.4 million (₩1.9 billion) benefit heavily from single-ownership senior and long-term holding discounts—reductions that can slash tax bills by up to 80%. When you add in secondary hits, like a stay-at-home spouse losing dependent health insurance status because they suddenly own a slice of the property, the "tax-saving" move suddenly looks like an expensive mistake.

Real estate strategy in South Korea cannot be solved with a one-size-fits-all rule of thumb. Every homeowner needs to weigh their property's specific appraised value against their personal timeline—balancing the fear of annual holding taxes against future sales taxes. If you are copying a neighbor's setup just because it is popular, you are likely paying for a mistake you didn't even know you were making.
