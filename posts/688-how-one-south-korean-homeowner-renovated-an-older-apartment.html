How One South Korean Homeowner Renovated an Older Apartment for Under $10,000

October 04, 2026

    By ditching traditional general contractors and hiring individual trade specialists directly, a South Korean homeowner slashed the cost of a 1,100-square-foot (34-pyeong) apartment remodel to roughly 13 million won (about $9,500).
  • What happened: The owner acted as their own general site manager, sourcing individual contractors for demolition, tiling, flooring, and lighting.
  • What it changes: It demonstrates how to bypass the hefty 40% markup typically charged by Korean interior design firms, transforming a dated apartment on a tight budget.
  • Who is affected: Young buyers and renters across South Korea facing soaring renovation quotes—often exceeding $35,000 for a standard 30-pyeong home—in a sluggish property market.

Cutting Out the Middleman in South Korea's Remodeling Market

Traditional interior design firms in South Korea often market themselves as all-inclusive service providers, but they rarely do the physical labor themselves. Instead, they hire the same independent tilers, carpenters, and painters that homeowners can source independently. By managing these workers directly, homeowners can pocket the 40% management fee usually paid to an agency. However, this savings comes with a cost: personal time, meticulous scheduling, and the legal gray areas of South Korea's construction laws, which generally require licensed operators for projects exceeding 15 million won.

Strategic budgeting makes all the difference in older apartments, known locally as guchuk. Rather than tearing down entire ceilings—a costly endeavor that can add thousands of dollars to the bill—the project focused on flattening bulky, outdated drop-ceilings and removing obsolete built-in storage. Paired with minimalist 3-inch recessed lighting instead of expensive, ornate fixtures, the living space instantly feels wider and more modern. Simple cosmetic updates, such as applying white film to yellowed window frames and swapping out kitchen faucets, deliver dramatic visual changes without requiring a full structural overhaul.

The Hidden Costs of DIY Project Management

While direct management saves money, it requires strict attention to detail and realistic expectations about maintenance. For instance, trendy tile-textured laminate flooring offers a sleek, modern aesthetic but can trap dirt in its grooves—a frustrating reality for families with young children. Similarly, minimalist 2-centimeter baseboards look clean and contemporary, but they expose uneven walls, forcing wallpaper crews to spend extra time and money on patching and putty work.

Execution timeline is another critical factor. A standard three-week schedule leaves zero room for error, and failing to build buffer days into the calendar can ruin the entire project. In South Korea's densely populated apartment complexes, weekend work is strictly prohibited to avoid neighbor disputes. Homeowners acting as their own contractors must be on-site during critical milestones—especially the first and last days—to catch mistakes early and avoid costly legal disputes with independent laborers.
