What Makes Us Human? Reading Tolstoy in Modern Times

September 27, 2026

Leo Tolstoy’s classic short story What Men Live By offers a sharp, timely challenge to the relentless materialism of modern capitalism.

    What happened: A reflection on Tolstoy’s 19th-century masterpiece uses the struggles of a poor Russian shoemaker to interrogate modern society's obsession with wealth and survival. What it changes: It reframes charity not as a luxury of the privileged, but as a fundamental act of survival and shared human dignity among those on society's margins. Who is affected: Modern readers caught in cycles of debt, consumption, and isolation, looking for deeper meaning beyond economic survival.

The Trap of Materialism and the Weight of Survival

Set against the grim backdrop of 19th-century Russia—where the 1861 emancipation of the serfs left peasants economically shackled and holding only 20% of the land while elites retained the rest—the story follows Semyon, a cobbler so poor that his entire daily earnings barely cover bread. Desperate for a winter coat, Semyon instead encounters a naked stranger behind a chapel. Overcoming his initial fear and selfishness, he takes the man home.

This dynamic mirrors our own economic anxieties today. Just as Semyon’s wife Matryona initially rages against sharing their meager food with a stranger, we are conditioned to believe that generosity is only possible when we have a surplus. Yet Tolstoy argues that true human connection begins precisely when we share our scarcity with those who have even less.

Three Truths for a Commercialized World

Tolstoy’s narrative rests on three core questions: What dwells in human beings? What is not given to men? And what do men live by? The answers dismantle the foundational myth of modern economics—that humans are purely selfish, utility-maximizing actors. Instead, Tolstoy suggests that humans are sustained not by the accumulation of wealth or the illusion of total control over the future, but entirely by love and mutual aid.

In an era dominated by social media comparison and economic precarity, Tolstoy’s 19th-century parable acts as an antidote. It reminds us that our institutions and economic systems matter, but without basic human empathy and the willingness to bear the pain of others, survival is reduced to a purely mechanical exercise.
