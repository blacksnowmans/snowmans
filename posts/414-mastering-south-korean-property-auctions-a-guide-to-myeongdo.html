Mastering South Korean Property Auctions: A Guide to 'Myeongdo' (Eviction)

September 08, 2026

  • Property auction winners in South Korea often panic upon realizing someone still occupies their newly purchased home, but the legal framework heavily favors the buyer.
  • The eviction process—known locally as myeongdo—requires strict adherence to legal timelines, starting with visiting the management office and posting a notice on the door on day one.
  • Crucial legal steps include filing for a provisional injunction to prevent occupants from transferring possession to a third party, and filing an eviction order within six months of paying the final balance.
  • Successful eviction relies on psychological control and structured negotiation—such as offering tiered moving subsidies—rather than emotional confrontations or illegal self-help measures.

The Golden Rules of Early Action: From Winning the Bid to Final Payment

For first-time property auction buyers in South Korea, winning the bid brings only momentary joy before the daunting reality of eviction sets in. Popular culture often depicts aggressive occupants throwing salt or threats, but reality is usually far more mundane: most occupants are simply debtors or tenants who are financially cornered and deeply confused. To handle this without losing sleep, buyers must strip away emotion and rely entirely on a cold, legal timeline.

The process begins on the day of the auction win. The buyer must visit the building's management office to leave contact details and drop a polite written note at the front door of the property. For the next 45 days leading up to the final balance payment, patience is vital. Avoid shouting matches or aggressive demands. Instead, secure your legal standing immediately by filing a provisional injunction (점유이전금지가처분) to stop the occupant from secretly handing over possession to a spouse, friend, or fake third party. Failing to do this can instantly render all subsequent legal victories useless.

Executing Eviction: Orders, Pressures, and Negotiation Tactics

The moment the final balance is paid, the property legally belongs to the buyer, unlocking the most powerful tool available: a court-ordered eviction directive (인도명령). Because this is an expedited review process handled primarily through paperwork, a decision typically arrives within two to four weeks. However, missing the strict six-month legal deadline to file this request means forfeiting the fast track and forcing the buyer into a slow, expensive civil eviction lawsuit.

Even with legal orders in hand, occupants often dig in their heels. This is where psychological pressure becomes essential. Issuing a formal certified mail notice (내용증명) and proceeding with a court-supervised forced eviction warning (강제집행 계고) usually breaks the occupant's resolve. Over 95% of stubborn occupants wave the white flag and come to the negotiation table once court bailiffs officially pin eviction notices to the door.

When negotiating moving expenses (이사비), never surrender control by asking the occupant how much they want. Instead, deploy a "double-binding" strategy: offer tiered financial support tied strictly to speed, such as higher payouts for vacating within a month and nothing if forced enforcement becomes necessary. By combining clinical legal precision with the calm demeanor of a helpful relocation assistant, South Korean auction buyers can reclaim their property efficiently and protect their investments.
