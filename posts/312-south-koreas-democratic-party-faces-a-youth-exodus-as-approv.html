South Korea’s Democratic Party faces a youth exodus as approval ratings plummet

September 07, 2026

  • The crisis: The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the main liberal opposition party, has seen its approval ratings among voters in their 20s and 30s collapse for eight consecutive weeks.
  • The numbers: Support for the DPK among 20-somethings has fallen to roughly 25%, significantly trailing the conservative People Power Party (PPP).
  • The cause: Young voters increasingly view the DPK as an out-of-touch, hypocritical "establishment" force rather than a progressive alternative.
  • The fallout: Internal infighting and a perceived lack of accountability are fueling a "veto sentiment," threatening the party’s long-term viability.

The latest data from pollster Realmeter paints a grim picture: President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating sits at 37.4%, while his disapproval has reached nearly 60%. More concerning for the DPK leadership is that their core base is fragmenting. Among men in their 20s, support for the DPK has cratered to just 13.2%. The party, which once prided itself on being the voice of "fairness and justice," is now being rejected by the very youth demographic it sought to court.

A party disconnected from reality

The DPK’s leadership currently appears more focused on internal factional battles—specifically targeting dissenters—than on addressing the policy failures that have alienated voters. This perceived arrogance has transformed the DPK into a "crony establishment" in the eyes of the youth. Instead of engaging with younger generations, the party is often criticized for treating them as subjects to be "taught" rather than political partners to be heard.

Furthermore, the party’s struggle to control the narrative online has left a vacuum. Critics argue that young voters are being pushed toward conservative viewpoints due to a distorted information environment, exacerbated by organized disinformation campaigns. However, rather than fostering a healthier media ecosystem through transparent, systemic reforms—such as proposed "anti-fake news" legislation—the DPK leadership has been accused of prioritizing internal power consolidation and silencing criticism.

Leadership failure and the path ahead

The current leadership, including the inner circle around Kim Min-seok, is being criticized for a defensive, litigious approach to public discourse rather than offering a compelling vision for the future. By fixating on "housecleaning" internal opposition, the party has effectively stalled its own reform agenda. Observers note that the DPK’s refusal to admit fault or pivot on key issues—such as housing and tax policy—has drained its remaining political capital.

If the DPK expects to remain a viable force, it must stop blaming external factors for its decline. The "youth exodus" is a direct reaction to a party that has stopped listening. Unless the leadership shifts from a strategy of suppression to one of genuine reflection and inclusive governance, the current polling slide is unlikely to reverse. For the DPK, the choice is stark: evolve into a party that practices the values it preaches, or continue its slide into electoral irrelevance.
