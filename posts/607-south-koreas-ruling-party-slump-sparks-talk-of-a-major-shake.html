South Korea's Ruling Party Slump Sparks Talk of a Major Shakeup

October 02, 2026

  • Plunging approval: South Korea's ruling Democratic Party is facing severe voter backlash, with core supporters alienated and approval ratings hitting new lows amid internal bickering.
  • The "Notebook" scandal: Party Chairman Kim Min-seok was caught on camera with a notepad referencing an "Han Dong-hoon file"—sparking accusations of dirty politics and deep-seated party paranoia.
  • The leadership crisis: High-profile gaffes by party figures, including aggressive rhetoric against internal critics and the media, have deepened the divide between the party and moderate voters.
  • The last resort: Frustrated supporters are increasingly looking to party heavyweight Jung Cheong-rae as a potential high-profile appointment—such as Justice Minister—to rescue the administration's reform agenda.

South Korea's political landscape is fracturing as the ruling Democratic Party grapples with a crisis of confidence. President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and party leadership are struggling to stop a bleeding approval rating, driven by tone-deaf messaging, internal factionalism, and a series of self-inflicted controversies. Rather than focusing on policy, the party is bogged down by petty squabbles and defensive explanations that only alienate its base further.

The situation highlights a dangerous drift into extreme partisan tribalism. From controversial online slang adopted by leadership to heavy-handed rhetoric labeling internal dissent as acts to be "suppressed," the party's tolerance for internal debate appears to be evaporating. When local leaders resort to crude threats against the press and public officials prioritize factional loyalty over competence, moderate voters quickly head for the exits. The party's core problem isn't just low numbers—it's a fundamental loss of democratic self-correction.

Can a High-Stakes Personnel Overhaul Save the Agenda?

With key investigative and justice posts sitting vacant, the administration is running out of time to deliver on its promised reforms. Speculation is mounting that President Lee may turn to firebrand lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae to take on a high-profile role, such as Justice Minister, following a four-hour dinner meeting between the two. For a frustrated base desperate for strong leadership that can push past conservative opposition, Jung is seen as a rare figure capable of enduring grueling parliamentary confirmation hearings and reviving the government's stalled momentum.

Yet, relying on polarizing figures is a double-edged sword. While it may rally the party's fading core supporters, it risks alienating the wider public if nominees carry too much political baggage. The Democratic Party stands at a definitive crossroads: without genuine self-reflection, a purge of undisciplined rhetoric, and strategic appointments that prioritize competence alongside reform, the current slump risks hardening into a permanent loss of political capital.
