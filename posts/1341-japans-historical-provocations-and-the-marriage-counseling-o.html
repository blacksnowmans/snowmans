Japan’s Historical Provocations and the "Marriage Counseling" of the Korea-US Alliance

October 20, 2026

South Korea faces a volatile diplomatic landscape as Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi renews historical provocations while influential voices in the United States signal a fundamental "redesign" of the bilateral alliance.

  • Japanese PM Takaichi sent ritual offerings to the Yasukuni Shrine, a site honoring Class-A war criminals, drawing sharp condemnation from the South Korean government and Beijing.
  • Russian President Vladimir Putin proposed a trilateral summit with Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping to discuss ending the Ukraine war, notably excluding Ukraine from the initial proposal.
  • Global gold prices hit a record high of $4,200 per ounce, reflecting extreme market anxiety over geopolitical instability and the upcoming U.S. election.
  • U.S. Senator David McCormick, a key ally of Donald Trump, described the Korea-U.S. alliance as being in "marriage counseling," signaling a shift toward a more transactional, business-like partnership.

Historical Friction and the Shift in Tokyo

The administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the current leader from the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), is navigating a sharp departure from the previous administration's approach. While the former leader Yoon Suk-yeol—who was removed from office following impeachment and is currently imprisoned—pursued a policy often criticized as submissive toward Tokyo, President Lee is under pressure to take a firmer stance. PM Takaichi’s decision to honor the Yasukuni Shrine despite Seoul’s protests highlights a recurring pattern: Japanese leaders leveraging right-wing nationalism to bolster domestic approval ratings, which have recently dipped below 50% due to economic hardship and a weak yen.

For South Korea, these provocations are more than symbolic. They represent a rejection of the peace order in Northeast Asia. As Japan attempts to pivot the domestic conversation away from economic failure toward historical revisionism, the Lee administration must balance its desire for regional cooperation with the need to defend national dignity. "Real cooperation is impossible as long as Japan continues to beautify its past wrongdoings," remains the prevailing sentiment in Seoul, necessitating a more sophisticated diplomatic response than the simple expressions of regret seen in the past.

Redefining the Alliance and Economic Security

The "marriage counseling" metaphor used by David McCormick suggests that Washington no longer views the alliance as an unconditional bond but as a relationship requiring a fundamental "reset." This involves increased pressure on South Korea regarding defense cost-sharing and trade. The Lee government’s challenge is to transition into a "pragmatic diplomacy" that treats the U.S. as a business partner rather than an oracular protector. This is particularly urgent as the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, continues to watch the administration's every move regarding the U.S. relationship.

Economic security is now inseparable from this diplomatic shift. Domestic leaders, including Representative Ahn Cheol-soo, are sounding the alarm over SK Hynix (South Korea’s second-largest chipmaker) and its subsidiary, Solidigm. There are growing calls to block Solidigm’s potential U.S. listing to prevent the leak of core semiconductor technology. Meanwhile, Lee Hyung-il, the head of Statistics Korea, has emphasized the need for a currency swap with the U.S. as a "safety net" against market volatility. With China’s trade surplus nearing $1 trillion despite Western sanctions, South Korea is caught in a pincer move between a "Made in Europe" protectionist push and a shifting U.S. security architecture that prioritizes "America First."

"The age of unconditional following is over. Only a pragmatic, national-interest-centered diplomacy can navigate the waves of a changing Korea-U.S. relationship and the 'jungle' of global trade regulation."
