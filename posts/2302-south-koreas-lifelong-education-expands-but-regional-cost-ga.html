South Korea's Lifelong Education Expands, but Regional Cost Gaps Reveal Deep Inequality

November 13, 2026

South Korean local governments and universities are ramping up adult learning programs nationwide, but glaring regional cost disparities and administrative blind spots are threatening equal access to lifelong education.

  • What happened: Universities and municipal governments reported major milestones in adult education, with Inha University in Incheon recording an 88.7% completion rate for its citizen college, while Busan’s Seo-gu district earned a top award from South Korea's Ministry of Education.
  • What was exposed: A regional audit in North Jeolla Province revealed a stark 43.9 percentage point disparity in adult learner out-of-pocket fee burdens between facilities in Jeonju and nearby Gimje, despite both operating under the same regional educational authority.
  • Who is affected: Middle-aged and senior adults pursuing second careers or degree certifications, alongside disabled residents who still face severe shortages of tailored education programs.
  • What's next: The government-run National Institute for Lifelong Education is processing final fourth-quarter registrations for adult students attempting to complete degrees through South Korea's Academic Credit Bank System by February.

A Push for Adult Learning Across the Provinces

As South Korea faces rapid demographic aging, local municipalities and universities are increasingly partnering to turn traditional campuses into lifelong learning hubs for adults. Incheon Citizen College, hosted by Inha University in Incheon, recently graduated 172 adult students with an 88.7% completion rate. Meanwhile, Busan’s Seo-gu district received the Minister of Education Award for outstanding adult learning infrastructure, and Chuncheon was designated as a regional hub city for continuing education in Gangwon Province for the second consecutive year. These initiatives reflect a growing push to help older residents gain practical skills and stay engaged in community life.

Policy Gaps and the Challenge of Equal Access

However, the rapid expansion of these programs has exposed significant structural flaws. The audit findings in North Jeolla Province—where adult learners in Gimje pay drastically higher out-of-pocket costs than those in neighboring Jeonju for identical public services—demonstrate a lack of standardized oversight. Furthermore, municipal councils in Seoul and Siheung noted that while total course offerings are growing, specialized infrastructure for vulnerable groups, particularly adults with disabilities, remains underfunded and understaffed.

Without centralized guidelines to standardize tuition costs and guarantee equitable access, South Korea risks creating a multi-tiered continuing education system where an adult's ability to upskill depends heavily on their zip code.
