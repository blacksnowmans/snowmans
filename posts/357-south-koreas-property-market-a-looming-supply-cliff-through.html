South Korea’s Property Market: A Looming "Supply Cliff" Through 2028

September 07, 2026

South Korea’s real estate market is facing a perfect storm of record-high liquidity, a critical shortage of new housing, and a political class paralyzed by the fear of voter backlash.

  • The Supply Gap: New apartment move-ins in the Seoul metropolitan area are projected to crater from roughly 176,000 units in 2024 to just 96,000 by 2029—a 10-year low.
  • Liquidity Pressure: Broad money supply (M2) has exploded to over 4,400 trillion won (about $3.2 trillion), effectively acting as a floor for asset prices regardless of minor policy shifts.
  • Market Distortion: Speculative "gap investing"—buying property with the intention of using high deposit-based rental (jeonse) income to cover the purchase price—remains a persistent threat to stability.

The Invisible Force: Liquidity vs. Interest Rates

There is a dangerous misconception that interest rate hikes are a silver bullet for cooling property prices. While the central bank may raise rates, the "real interest rate"—inflation minus the nominal rate—often remains negative. In this environment, taking out a mortgage acts as a hedge against currency devaluation, incentivizing buyers to remain in the market despite high borrowing costs.

Furthermore, the Korean economy is caught in a trap. While high rates are intended to curb borrowing, they simultaneously squeeze small business owners and households already burdened by debt. This limits how aggressive the Bank of Korea can be, leaving the housing market to be driven by the sheer weight of circulating money rather than economic fundamentals.

The 2028 Supply Cliff and the Policy Vacuum

The most immediate threat is the "supply cliff." With new apartment completions falling sharply through 2028, the rental market is expected to tighten, pushing up jeonse prices. Historically, when these rental premiums rise close to the value of the property, it triggers a surge in purchase demand from tenants who can no longer afford to wait, further inflating prices.

Currently, the market is exhibiting a "triple decline"—falling trade volume in sales, jeonse, and monthly rentals. This is not a healthy correction; it is a sign of a market reaching its breaking point. Prospective buyers are being priced out, with households earning 70 million won (about $50,000) annually finding themselves unable to service the debt required for an average Seoul apartment.

The solution is not more "patchwork" regulations, which often do more harm than good. Experts argue that the government must move toward structural reform: phasing out tax benefits for short-term, non-resident property owners and implementing more aggressive taxes on multi-home owners. Without such measures, policy will continue to prioritize short-term political optics over the long-term housing stability required by the next generation.

"Policy must be based on the housing rights of the next generation, not the voter turnout of today."

For now, the market remains a "boiling pot" accessible only to a wealthy few, while the broader public watches from the sidelines. Until the government finds the courage to treat housing as a social asset rather than a speculative engine, the volatility is likely to continue well past 2028.
