Japan’s Rate Hike Won’t Fix the Weak Yen

October 02, 2026

  • The Bank of Japan (BOJ) raised interest rates from 0.1% to 0.25%, but the move is widely seen as too small to bridge the massive gap with U.S. rates.
  • The yen remains a low-interest "funding currency" for global investors, meaning the era of the weak yen is likely far from over.
  • While the weak yen boosts Japanese corporate profits, it forces Japanese households to endure higher import costs and stagnant wages.
  • For South Korea, the weak yen threatens price competitiveness in consumer goods, though high-tech exports like semiconductors remain largely insulated.

A "Storm in a Teacup" for Global Markets

The recent hike by the Bank of Japan—the country’s central bank—has failed to excite global markets. When compared to U.S. interest rates, which sit in the 5% range, Japan’s bump to 0.25% is statistically negligible. For major global capital flows, Japan’s move is being dismissed as a "storm in a teacup." As long as the yield spread between the dollar and the yen remains this wide, global capital will continue to favor dollar-based assets, keeping the yen under persistent downward pressure.

The situation is exacerbated by Japanese investors themselves. After three decades of domestic stagnation, Japanese capital has fled to higher-yielding U.S. Treasuries and global tech stocks. This creates a vicious cycle: as Japanese investors trade yen for dollars to chase better returns, they further weaken their own currency. The BOJ’s policy is essentially trying to swim upstream against the tide of global profit-seeking behavior.

The Illusion of "Full Employment"

Japan boasts an unemployment rate of roughly 2%, technically a state of "full employment." However, this figure masks a deep, structural rot. For the past 30 years, Japanese corporations—including giants like Toyota—have prioritized preserving jobs over raising wages. This "low-wage for job security" pact has left Japanese workers with stagnant paychecks while inflation erodes their purchasing power. To the average citizen, the low unemployment rate is not a sign of prosperity; it is a desperate survival metric in a country where wages refuse to grow.

This reality forces a critical look at the "hidden costs" of Japan's economic stagnation. The weak yen has become a double-edged sword: it acts as a drug for major exporters who enjoy inflated earnings, but it serves as a poison for the average consumer facing skyrocketing prices for food and energy. Until Japan addresses its structural inability to raise wages and revitalize its domestic market, monetary policy tweaks like interest rate hikes will remain merely cosmetic. For South Korea, the lesson is clear: long-term economic resilience depends not on currency fluctuations, but on maintaining the "irreplaceability" of its own high-value industries in a shifting global market.
