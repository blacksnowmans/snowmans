Why You Should Stop Cleaning Your Ears Immediately

September 11, 2026

  • Earwax is not waste: It is a natural, acidic, and oily barrier designed to trap dust and kill bacteria.
  • The "Cotton Swab" trap: Using swabs or metal tools removes this protective layer, leading to external otitis (swimmer’s ear), a condition that spikes in summer when humidity and irritation overlap.
  • Self-cleaning mechanism: The ear naturally expels wax through jaw movement; if you feel it at the opening, only clean the outer part gently.
  • Health consequences: Pushing debris deeper can lead to chronic inflammation, pain, and even temporary hearing loss.

The Perils of "Cleaning" Your Ears

For many, the post-shower routine of clearing out earwax with a cotton swab is a source of strange satisfaction. However, ENT (ear, nose, and throat) specialists warn that this habit is essentially an act of self-sabotage. By scraping away earwax, you aren't cleaning your ears; you are stripping away your body’s primary defense system. Data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (South Korea's government agency overseeing medical costs) reveals that over 2.4 million people were treated for external otitis in 2023, with cases peaking in the summer due to moist conditions and habitual cleaning.

If you find yourself with an itchy or blocked ear, avoid the temptation to reach for a tool. The "clean" sensation is a temporary reward that often paves the way for severe infection. If you must manage buildup, use a hairdryer on a low, cool setting to dry the ear after a shower, rather than inserting anything into the ear canal. If the buildup feels excessive or painful, visiting a professional is the only safe path forward.

A Lesson from Medical History

The transition from "folk remedies" to modern medicine is littered with horrifying anecdotes that put our current health habits into perspective. Before the 20th century, surgery was often a gruesome race against time, performed without proper anesthesia—which was frequently reserved for "laughing parties" rather than clinical use. The 19th-century surgeon Robert Liston famously once performed an amputation so quickly that he accidentally cut a patient’s assistant, who later died of sepsis, while a spectator died of shock. The "300% mortality" incident stands as a chilling reminder of how quickly medical practice becomes dangerous when hygiene and ethics are ignored.

This history of "medical overreach"—from trepanation (drilling holes in the skull to let out demons) to the mid-20th century practice of lobotomies—shows that human intervention is not always "healing." The same logic applies to your daily habits: sometimes, the best medical advice is to stop "fixing" what isn't broken. Just as early surgeons caused more harm than good by failing to understand the body, we often cause our own ear and throat issues by interfering with the natural processes our bodies have evolved to handle on their own.
