South Korea's Ruling Party Urges Supporters to Drop Derogatory Nicknames Amid Toxic Political Discourse

September 30, 2026

  • Kim Min-seok, a senior figure in the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), recently urged party members and online supporters to stop using derogatory terms like "pseudo-citizens" against opponents.
  • The rare self-reflection within the progressive camp highlights growing anxiety over how extreme political language and online mockery are trivializing governance.
  • Commentators and political figures have emphasized that public figures and partisan YouTubers bear a heavier responsibility for toxic discourse than ordinary citizens venting online.

South Korean politics is notoriously aggressive, with fierce rivalries often boiling down to personal attacks and online mockery. But a recent shift has brought the issue to the fore: even political insiders are admitting that the rhetoric has crossed a line. When major players start calling out the weaponization of language within their own base, it signals that the mudslinging is no longer just a fringe problem—it is poisoning the entire political ecosystem.

The core tension lies in the hypocrisy of political figures hiding behind their most radical supporters. While politicians often deflect blame by pointing to rough language on internet message boards, commentators note that public officials and influential YouTubers wield institutional power. Their insults carry real weight and shape public norms in a way that anonymous online venting simply does not. When leaders validate mockery and dismissive nicknames, they fuel polarization while pushing actual policy debates off the table.

Moving Beyond the Mud or Business as Usual?

Calls for civility from party leadership sound good on paper, but skeptics wonder whether they will amount to anything more than a PR gesture. In South Korea's high-stakes political arena, red-meat rhetoric plays well with hardcore loyalists who demand aggressive pushback against rivals. Telling supporters to take the high road often triggers pushback from a base convinced they are locked in an existential fight.

Yet, the long-term cost of this toxic culture is mounting voter fatigue. As lawmakers spend more time generating viral insults and trading juvenile nicknames, citizens struggling with everyday economic pressures are left high and dry. If political parties want to restore public trust, token appeals to polite language will not be enough; they will have to stop treating outrage and division as their primary political assets.
