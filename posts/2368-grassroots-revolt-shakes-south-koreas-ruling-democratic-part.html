Grassroots Revolt Shakes South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

November 16, 2026

Over 1,083 dues-paying members of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) have filed a court injunction against senior lawmaker Kim Min-seok, escalating a fierce internal revolt against President Lee Jae-myung’s party leadership, according to commentary from grassroots critics within the party.

  • Leadership challenged in court: The 1,083 party members petitioned the Seoul Southern District Court to suspend Kim Min-seok’s official duties, alleging that party leaders abused their authority and manipulated nomination processes.
  • Targeted ethics probe: Critics argue the backlash intensified after Kim referred reformist lawmaker Choi Min-hee to the party’s ethics panel over leaked remarks from a private meeting, where she had recounted the DPK’s cautious strategy during the late-2024 martial law crisis and the impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol, who is now imprisoned following his removal from office.
  • Stalled reforms and 2028 nominations: Dissident members accuse the Lee administration and an emerging "New Lee Jae-myung" faction of stalling promised prosecutorial reforms—such as establishing a Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—out of concern for centrist approval ratings, while maneuvering to sideline vocal incumbents like Choi and lawmaker Jung Chung-rae ahead of the 2028 parliamentary elections.
  • Expanding legal battles: Outside internal party channels, activist Kim Han-me refiled previously dismissed complaints linking reformist media outlet Sisa Tapa TV and former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae to past allegations, prompting counter-lawsuits for false accusation and violations of the Attorney-at-Law Act.

What the Infighting Reveals

The dispute highlights a widening rift between the activist base that helped bring President Lee to power and the coalition currently steering the DPK. According to the commentary, party insiders are squandering early governing momentum by prioritizing poll-watching over sweeping institutional reforms, while using disciplinary probes to silence internal dissent. From the critics' perspective, branding loyal grassroots supporters as "hardliners" risks eroding the party's core mobilization engine even as the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, struggles to recover.

Beneath the debate over reform policy lies an early struggle for control over the 2028 general election nominations. The commentary alleges that newer factions—including the "New Lee Jae-myung" group, the first-term lawmakers' circle The Mincho, and the DPK Innovation Council—represent an opportunistic blend of regional Seongnam insiders and former conservative "New Right" figures. By marginalizing traditional supporters aligned with past liberal administrations, the leadership risks hollowing out the party's long-standing political identity.

Regardless of how the court ultimately rules on the injunction, the spectacle of more than a thousand dues-paying members taking their own ruling party leadership to court signals serious organizational strain. If President Lee and the DPK leadership fail to address the grievances of their core base, internal friction could increasingly complicate the administration’s legislative agenda.
