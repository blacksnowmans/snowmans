South Korea’s Public Sector Goes All-In on AI Transformation

November 28, 2026

South Korean local governments and public institutions are shifting from simple chatbot implementations to a wholesale "AI Transformation" (AX) of administrative systems, safety protocols, and the national education curriculum.

  • Regional Control Towers: Major hubs like Jeju Island and Gwangjin-gu (a district in eastern Seoul) have launched dedicated AI policy committees to break down bureaucratic silos and create unified roadmaps for tech adoption.
  • Safety and Infrastructure: Nonsan City and South Chungcheong Province are successfully using AI for building risk analysis and optimizing emergency 119 dispatch routes, proving the technology’s utility in life-saving scenarios.
  • Educational Overhaul: Yonsei University, one of the country's top private institutions, has announced a $93 million (130 billion KRW) "human-centered AI campus" project to be implemented over the next eight years.
  • Ethical Guardrails: The Lee Jae-myung administration and the National Information Society Agency (NIA) are drafting regulations to combat AI-assisted cheating in schools and close the digital divide between social classes.

From Efficiency to Life-Saving Infrastructure

The current push for AI in Korea is moving past mere office efficiency. In provincial areas, the focus is on physical safety. Nonsan’s use of generative AI to monitor building safety has already earned it national recognition, as the system can identify structural risks faster than human inspectors. Similarly, the "AI 119" system in South Chungcheong Province analyzes emergency calls in real-time to provide dispatchers with the fastest routes and critical medical protocols. These are not experimental pilots; they are becoming the standard infrastructure for local governance.

In the economic sphere, cities like Daegu are pushing for deregulation to turn entire urban areas into AI testing grounds. This is a strategic move to attract startups by offering a "regulatory sandbox" where new technologies can be verified in real-world conditions. For these local governments, AX is seen as a survival strategy to attract investment and compensate for shrinking administrative workforces due to demographic shifts.

Guardrails in the Era of "Reasonable Rejection"

As AI enters the classroom and the civil service, the government is grappling with the "shadow side" of the technology. The Blue House (the presidential office of the Democratic Party administration) recently began reviewing policies to "reasonably reject" AI-generated answers in academic and civil service exams. This follows growing concerns from the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative opposition) regarding the fairness of school grades as students increasingly use smart devices to bypass traditional testing methods.

There is also a significant philosophical shift occurring in how officials view their roles. Local authorities, such as those in Ganghwa County, are emphasizing "human responsibility" even as they automate tasks. The prevailing sentiment among policymakers is that while AI can handle data analysis and paperwork, the final decision—and the moral responsibility for that decision—must remain with human officials. This "human-in-the-loop" approach is intended to ensure that the drive for efficiency does not overlook the "human face" of public service, particularly for marginalized groups who may be left behind by the digital transition.

"Technology is a partner in solving problems, but the act of wiping away the tears of those in welfare blind spots remains the duty of humans."

Ultimately, the rapid adoption of AI across Korea’s public sector reflects a nation in a hurry to define the next global standard. By 2026, the government aims to have completed a full transition to an "Intelligent Platform Government," where data-driven decisions are the norm rather than the exception. The success of this transition will depend on whether the legal framework can keep pace with the technical innovation.
