South Korea Abolishes Its 78-Year-Old Prosecutors' Office

October 03, 2026

South Korea has officially dismantled its powerful Prosecutors' Office after 78 years, splitting its immense powers into two new bodies—the Serious Crimes Investigation Office and the Prosecution Office—marking a watershed moment for the country's criminal justice system.

  • The structural overhaul: The century-old prosecution system has been dissolved, dividing investigative and prosecutorial powers into separate agencies.
  • A quiet exit: While the new investigation agency held public launch ceremonies, the new prosecution body kept its debut subdued and unpublicized, reflecting internal resistance.
  • Years of political struggle: The reform is the culmination of decades of efforts championed by liberal administrations from Roh Moo-hyun to Moon Jae-in and Lee Jae-min.
  • Lingering risks: Critics warn that rushed implementation and retained special investigation units could leave loopholes for future conservative administrations to reverse the changes.

The Bitter End of an Untouchable Institution

The abolition of the South Korean Prosecutors' Office is being hailed by reformists as the ultimate check on an institution that has long wielded unchecked political and legal power. For decades, prosecutors used high-profile investigations and indictments as political weapons, building an insular culture where high-profile cases were treated like trophies. Revelations from political figures like former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook highlighted how prosecutors viewed aggressive tactics—such as high-profile raids—as badges of honor rather than tools of justice.

Yet, the transition has been plagued by friction and institutional foot-dragging. The stark contrast between the new investigative body holding loud public events and the new prosecution office hiding behind closed doors underscores how deeply resistant the old guard is to losing its privileges. Critics note that unless the state cuts excess personnel and closes loopholes that allow prosecutors to retain investigative functions under different labels, the old abusive habits will simply move under a new roof.

Lessons from the Past and the Threat of Reversal

The road to this reform was paved with deep political turmoil. Former President Moon Jae-in later expressed regret over appointing early reform-era prosecutors, admitting that his administration was too blinded by the pursuit of institutional change to anticipate the fallout. Observers warn that without robust judicial oversight—similar to Germany's intermediate court procedures that vet indictments—the newly split powers could easily be reassembled if a conservative government returns to power and promises to restore the old system under the guise of restoring public safety.
