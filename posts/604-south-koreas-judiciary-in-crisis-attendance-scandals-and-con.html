South Korea’s Judiciary in Crisis: Attendance Scandals and Constitutional Deadlock

October 02, 2026

South Korea’s judiciary is facing a deepening crisis of public trust, fueled by allegations that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae lacks basic accountability regarding his own attendance and his unprecedented refusal to participate in the constitutional appointment process for the Constitutional Court.

  • Attendance Scandal: Reports indicate that the Supreme Court lacks a formal system to track the arrival and departure times of its judges and staff, raising concerns about systemic administrative failure and "imperial" workplace culture.
  • Constitutional Deadlock: Cho has refused to formally request the reappointment of Constitutional Court justices—a duty required of his office—leading to a state of paralysis in one of the nation's key constitutional institutions.
  • Public Backlash: Critics, including legal experts and opposition politicians like Lee Yong-woo of the Democratic Party, argue that the Chief Justice is effectively acting above the law, undermining the very rule of law he is sworn to uphold.

A Judiciary Without a Clock

The judiciary, despite South Korea’s reputation as a tech-forward nation, has admitted to the National Assembly that it essentially lacks a functional system to monitor the working hours of its judges. While employees use badges to open doors, these are not recorded as official time-stamped attendance data. Critics argue that this exemption from basic public-sector accountability makes the court appear as a "sanctuary" outside the reach of the rules applied to ordinary civil servants.

The controversy surrounding Chief Justice Cho’s own attendance habits has drawn sharp comparisons to previous scandals involving political figures. Legal experts note that since Supreme Court leadership has access to dedicated vehicles and drivers—which generate digital logs—the failure to provide clear records suggests either gross administrative negligence or a deliberate effort to obscure data. This lack of transparency strikes at the core of judicial credibility; it is difficult for a court to demand public adherence to the law when its leaders operate with such institutional opacity.

Constitutional Collision

The crisis reached a breaking point when Cho unilaterally blocked the re-nomination process for Constitutional Court justices. Despite legal advice from the National Court Administration—the body responsible for judicial policy—recommending that he proceed with the request in line with the court’s own precedents, Cho allegedly reacted with hostility and refused to initiate the process. He has since justified this by claiming the government’s request lacked sufficient constitutional detail, a move critics dismiss as a "legalistic" maneuver designed to mask a personal, arbitrary decision.

This standoff is unprecedented in South Korean history. In previous eras, even under political pressure, the judiciary upheld its duty to complete appointment procedures to ensure the continuity of constitutional oversight. By framing a mandatory administrative duty as a matter of personal discretion, Cho has invited calls for his impeachment. The current situation suggests that the judiciary is no longer functioning as an independent check on power, but is instead becoming a site of political friction, further eroding the public’s faith in the impartiality of the law.
