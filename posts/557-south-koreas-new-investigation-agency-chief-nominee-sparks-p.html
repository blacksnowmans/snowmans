South Korea's New Investigation Agency Chief Nominee Sparks Prosecutor Reform Backlash

October 02, 2026

  • The Nominee: Kim Ji-yong, a veteran prosecutor, has been tapped to head the newly established Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA), South Korea's new body designed to strip investigative powers from the traditional prosecution service.
  • The Backlash: Lawmakers like Park Eun-jung of the Rebuilding Korea Party are demanding his nomination be scrapped, citing his past as a prosecutor who opposed limits on prosecutorial power and signed a 2020 collective statement backing then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol.
  • The Controversy: Critics argue that appointing a career insider to lead an agency meant to dismantle prosecutorial overreach turns the reform into a toothless rebranding exercise.
  • What's at Stake: Set to launch in October 2026, the SCIA is meant to separate investigation from indictment—a cornerstone of progressive legal reforms aimed at curbing the unchecked power of South Korea's prosecutors.

South Korea's push to overhaul its powerful criminal justice system is hitting heavy turbulence. The nomination of Kim Ji-yong to lead the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA) has ignited a fierce political row, with critics arguing that placing a loyalist of the old guard at the helm of a reform agency defeats its entire purpose. Rather than a fresh start, opponents are calling the appointment a betrayal of the public mandate to break up the prosecution's monopoly on investigative power.

A Reform Agency Led by an Old-Guard Insider?

At the heart of the outcry is Kim's history inside the prosecution service. In 2020, when the Ministry of Justice attempted to discipline then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol, Kim was among the senior prosecutors who signed a collective letter pushing back against the move. Critics point out the bitter irony of selecting someone who fought against structural reforms to now lead the very institution born out of those reforms. Past involvements in high-profile, contentious indictments that later ended in acquittals further fuel opposition claims that Kim's appointment signals a retreat rather than progress.

The nomination has also put the presidential office's vetting system under fire. Lawmakers and civic groups question how a candidate so fundamentally opposed to the founding ethos of the SCIA could pass a rigorous background and philosophy check. For international observers watching South Korea's perennial struggles over legal reform, this episode underscores a familiar pattern: whenever deep-seated institutional power is challenged, the system tends to recycle its own elites, leaving real structural change hanging in the balance.
