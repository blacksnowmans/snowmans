South Korea's High Housing Costs Push Newlyweds to Renovate Abandoned Homes

October 04, 2026

Faced with soaring apartment prices and long commutes, a South Korean newlywed couple with a tight budget of about $110,000 (150 million won) chose to buy and overhaul a dilapidated 50-year-old house in Yongin instead of purchasing a standard apartment.

  • What happened: The couple bought an abandoned, debris-filled old house—facing heavy skepticism from family and friends—and spent about $11,000 (14.5 million won) just on waste removal and months on hands-on renovation.
  • What it changes: It highlights a growing counter-trend against South Korea's obsession with uniform, high-priced apartments as the only acceptable form of housing and wealth accumulation.
  • Who is affected: Young couples priced out of metropolitan real estate markets, who are increasingly forced to choose between punishing commutes or creative, non-traditional housing solutions.

South Korea’s real estate culture is notoriously fixated on brand-name apartments as both status symbols and retirement funds. Bucking that pressure requires real grit, especially when friends mock your fixer-upper as needless self-punishment. But by turning a garbage-strewn ruin into a vintage-styled home with a party-ready basement bunker, this couple carved out an existence on their own terms rather than a developer's blueprint.

Yet the romanticizing of old-house makeovers glosses over severe structural risks. In South Korea, amateur renovations often run headfirst into unexpected repair costs, waterproofing failures, and notoriously tricky contractor disputes. For every inspiring TV segment about a charming rural or semi-urban old home, there are unsuspecting buyers walking straight into a legal and financial quagmire.
