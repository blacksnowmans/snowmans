South Korea’s President Lee Jae-myung Rules Out Re-election in Major Press Conference

September 18, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung used a televised press conference at the presidential office to rule out seeking a second term, reject any military deployment to the Strait of Hormuz, and reaffirm his commitment to prosecutorial reform.

  • No second term: President Lee explicitly stated he has "no intention" of pursuing a constitutional amendment to allow consecutive terms, aiming to shift focus entirely to livelihood issues and policy outcomes.
  • Strait of Hormuz: Rejecting foreign pressure, Lee drew a hard line against military involvement, declaring there will be no troop dispatches or deployment of military assets to the Middle East conflict zone.
  • Prosecutorial reform: The administration reaffirmed its goal to permanently separate investigative and indictment powers from the prosecution service, despite ongoing friction within the ruling party.
  • Media and communication: The broadcast highlighted growing tensions between the administration’s open-door communication style and online harassment targeting reporters covering the presidential office.

Shifting from Grand Visions to Livelihood Politics

By voluntarily taking constitutional re-election off the table, President Lee has attempted to defuse opportunistic political framing and address public wariness head-on. Recent polls showed a solid majority of citizens wanted a firm non-running commitment tied to any talk of constitutional reform. While Lee's approval ratings have seen a modest rebound, the political landscape remains sharply divided—reflecting a public that cares far less about grand political restructuring and far more about tangible economic relief.

The bigger test for the administration is execution. Lee’s refusal to send troops to the Strait of Hormuz is a welcome, risk-averse foreign policy stance that shields young South Koreans from foreign entanglements, but it will require careful diplomatic maneuvering to manage allied expectations. Domestically, the ruling Democratic Party—described by critics as behaving more like a wrench than a bumper for the administration—must stop infighting and align behind actionable reforms.

The Battle Over Reform and Media Transparency

Prosecutorial reform has been a perennial battleground in South Korean politics for decades. Lee’s insistence on separating investigative and indictment powers is intended to institutionalize checks and balances rather than allow executive capture. However, the reform agenda has repeatedly bogged down in friendly fire among the ruling party's base. If the administration wants to succeed where past governments stumbled, it must move past toxic online tribalism and focus on disciplined, systemic changes.

At the same time, the administration's push for radical transparency—such as live-streaming ministry briefings—has exposed a darker side of South Korea's media ecosystem. When journalists asking tough questions become immediate targets for coordinated online abuse and YouTube pillorying, genuine debate suffers. For transparency to work, the government and the press must establish guardrails that protect rigorous reporting from turning into performative bloodsport.
