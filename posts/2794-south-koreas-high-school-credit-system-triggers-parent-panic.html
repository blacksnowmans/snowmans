South Korea's High School Credit System Triggers Parent Panic and Teacher Backlash

November 27, 2026

As South Korea prepares for the nationwide rollout of its High School Credit System in 2025, confusion over course selection and underlying staffing shortages are sparking severe anxiety among parents and fierce pushback from teacher unions.

  • Massive Turnout: Over 800 parents flooded a single briefing in Seoul's affluent Gangnam-Seocho district, with similar crowds gathering nationwide in Daejeon, Guri-Namyangju, and Geochang to decipher how high school course selections will affect university admissions.
  • Broken Promises: Essential elective subjects are frequently cancelled due to teacher shortages—including a specialized Geometry class that was dropped despite 15 students signing up.
  • Teacher Opposition: Three major national teachers' unions jointly protested the Ministry of Education's recent showcase event, demanding extra hiring and workload reductions rather than self-congratulatory publicity.
  • Information Gap: Incoming high school freshmen currently have no transparent way to check whether a prospective school has a history of cancelling promised courses before they enroll.

The Gap Between Reform Promises and Classroom Reality

South Korea’s High School Credit System was designed to mimic university-style course selection, allowing students to tailor their studies to their career ambitions. In practice, however, the policy is colliding with severe structural roadblocks. While the Ministry of Education actively promotes the reform, local high schools lack the teaching staff required to run niche electives. This creates a frustrating dynamic where students are officially granted the right to choose their curriculum, only to find their preferred classes unstaffed and quietly scrapped.

Compounding the problem is an opaque admissions landscape. Because South Korean university admissions increasingly factor in whether applicants took subjects relevant to their intended majors, parents view these course choices as high-stakes decisions. Yet middle school students choosing which high school to attend are forced to enroll blindly, without access to data showing which high schools actually deliver on their course catalogues and which ones routinely cancel them.

Navigating an Unprepared System

In response to the growing anxiety, regional education support offices and local municipal governments—from Seoul's Mapo district to regional offices in Jeonbuk and Yechon—are scrambling to launch one-on-one counseling programs and teacher training sessions. A few institutions, such as Daejeon Hanbit High School, are showing that student-centered curricula focused on long-term life design can work. However, relying on the exceptional initiative of individual schools is not a scalable national strategy.

Until the Ministry of Education addresses fundamental teacher shortages and mandates full public transparency regarding course cancellations, the burden will remain squarely on parents. For now, families have little choice but to aggressively vet prospective high schools themselves, cross-referencing past course offerings and seeking out joint-school credit programs to safeguard their children's educational futures.
