South Korea's Former First Lady Blames Husband for Ruin at Appeal Hearing

September 10, 2026

South Korea's former First Lady Kim Keon Hee has blamed her husband's presidency for ruining her wealth and reputation, delivering a defiant closing statement at her appeal trial for influence-peddling.

  • The Appeal: Prosecutors are seeking more than 7.5 years in prison for Kim, who was sentenced to 7 years in prison by a lower court for trading political influence for luxury gifts.
  • The Defense: Kim claimed in a 10-minute court statement that she would have made far more money had she stayed in business, arguing that her husband's time in office cost her everything.
  • The Historical Context: Kim and her husband, jailed former President Yoon Suk Yeol—currently facing insurrection charges—mark the first time in South Korean history that a former presidential couple has stood trial in custody simultaneously.
  • The Evidence: The court dismissed defense attempts to contest the authenticity of high-end gifts like a $100,000-plus painting by artist Lee Ufan, citing the clear exchange of valuable items for political favors.

The spectacle of a former first lady standing in a criminal court complaining about lost business profits lays bare a profound failure of self-awareness. Throughout the investigation and trial, Kim and her legal team have relied on a predictable pattern: deny everything until confronted with physical evidence, then hastily invent alternative explanations—such as claiming luxury jewelry and designer watches were merely casual gifts or acquired through convoluted proxy purchases. The court has seen through these shifting excuses, noting that stashing luxury goods in locked bags hidden at relatives' homes is hardly normal behavior for innocent recipients.

Beyond the personal arrogance on display, this trial serves as a critical test for South Korea's judicial system and public accountability. Under the law, influence-peddling by public figures—or those leveraging close ties to power—undermines the integrity of public service regardless of whether a formal public office was held. The defense's insistence that these transactions were harmless personal exchanges collapses under the weight of testimonies from former aides and clear paper trails. As the court prepares its ruling, the proceedings stand as a stark warning that executive power and royal treatment do not grant immunity from the rule of law.
