South Korea's Ruling Party Fractures as Core Supporters Cross Their Arms

September 23, 2026

  • What happened: Prominent liberal figures and politicians in South Korea are publicly clashing over plunging approval ratings for President Lee Jae-myung and his administration, exposing deep anxieties over stalled prosecutorial reforms.
  • What it changes: The friction shifts the debate from surface-level political strategy to a fundamental identity crisis within the liberal camp, testing the bond between the leadership and its most loyal voters.
  • Who is affected: President Lee Jae-myung's administration, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), and the core grassroots supporters whose active mobilization is vital for governance.

South Korea's liberal political scene is fracturing in public view, marked by sharp, televised exchanges between high-profile commentator Rhyu Si-min and senior Democratic Party figures like lawmaker Kim Min-seok. While party leadership blames the administration's sliding approval ratings on toxic infighting and harsh rhetoric from hardline supporters, critics argue that the rot runs deeper. For Korea's progressive base, the current slump is not just a PR problem—it is a betrayal of the reformist mandate that brought the government to power.

The core dispute centers on prosecutorial reform—a holy grail for South Korea's liberal movement. Commentator Rhyu Si-min has openly accused the administration of dragging its feet on fully separating investigative and indicting powers within the prosecution service, warning that President Lee's incremental approach amounts to a half-measure rather than genuine structural change. Although the presidential office has dismissed such claims as groundless speculation, the ambiguity has left core supporters feeling alienated. Rather than actively defending the government, the grassroots have figuratively "crossed their arms," withholding the enthusiastic backing that any progressive administration needs to push through entrenched opposition.

Renovation vs. Extension: The Architectural Metaphor for Political Survival

The most biting critique of the current administration comes from Rhyu's architectural metaphor, arguing that while supporters wanted an "extension" (building upon the existing foundation of trust and shared values), President Lee's team is attempting a "rebuilding" project that bulldozes the original base to court centrist voters. In South Korean politics, abandoning your foundational base to court the middle often leaves a party stranded—demolishing the old house before a new one is built, leaving only ruins.

This strategic pivot has left the Democratic Party trapped in a narrower, more insular loop, alienating allies from previous progressive factions. When a government prioritizes political pragmatism over core promises—such as sweeping prosecutorial and judicial overhauls—it risks losing the very foot soldiers who defend it during crises. As internal dissent mounts and public trust wavers, the administration faces a stark choice: authentically reconnect with its reform-minded base or risk losing its political momentum entirely.
