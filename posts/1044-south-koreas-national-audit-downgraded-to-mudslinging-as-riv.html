South Korea's National Audit Downgraded to Mudslinging as Rival Parties Clash Over Family Controversies

October 13, 2026

    What happened: South Korea's ongoing parliamentary audit descended into bitter personal attacks on October 12, with the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party trading accusations involving family scandals, including a controversial visit to President Lee Jae-myung's parents' graves.
  • What it changes: Plunging poll numbers for the ruling party have triggered an internal scramble for message discipline, prompting leadership to aggressively pivot toward livelihood legislation like a new rural development bill.
  • Who is affected: Voters weary of toxic political gridlock, alongside core progressive blocks whose shifting support has rattled the ruling camp.

South Korea's annual national audit—meant to serve as a rigorous check on executive power—has instead turned into a toxic spectacle of tit-for-tat family investigations. While the conservative opposition People Power Party hammers the ruling liberal camp over past controversies surrounding President Lee Jae-myung and his family, the liberal Democratic Party has fired back with renewed allegations against former officials, leaving voters stuck watching a sideshow of personal grievances.

For the ruling Democratic Party, the stakes could hardly be higher. Recent polling has dealt the party a harsh blow, with approval ratings slipping and the opposition pulling ahead as core progressive and middle-aged voters begin to drift. Democratic leader Kim Min-seok has responded by attempting to drag his party back to disciplined policymaking—pushing for an end to chaotic, emotional crossfire and demanding that lawmakers focus on concrete issues like foreign policy coherence and the protection of national interests.

Yet the temptation for petty retaliation remains too strong on both sides. Whether it is digging into family credentials or dragging private gravesites into political crossfire, the relentless mudslinging demonstrates just how deeply polarized South Korea's political class has become. If the ruling party wants to arrest its slide in the polls, empty rhetoric about "livelihood politics" will not cut it; leadership will have to prove it can deliver tangible legislative results while keeping its own ranks from taking the bait every time the opposition throws a punch.
