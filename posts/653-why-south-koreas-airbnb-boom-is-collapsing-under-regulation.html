Why South Korea's Airbnb Boom is Collapsing Under Regulation and Burnout

September 10, 2026

  • The shift: Once romanticized as an easy side hustle, hosting on Airbnb in South Korea is increasingly being abandoned for outright quitting.
  • The drivers: A crackdown on illegal short-term rentals, grueling maintenance and marketing labor, and unpredictable, season-dependent revenues.
  • Who is affected: Part-time hosts, especially those relying on standard office-towers (officetels) where short-term rental registration is legally impossible.
  • The stakes: Unregistered operators face severe criminal penalties, including up to two years in prison or fines of up to 20 million won (about $14,000).

The glossy marketing from short-term rental platforms promises a low-friction lifestyle: monetize a spare room, chat with global travelers, and break free from a boring 9-to-5 routine. But for a growing number of South Korean hosts, the reality is a trap of endless cleaning, sky-high winter utility bills, and the constant dread of a ringing phone. As local governments step up inspections and major booking platforms tighten verification rules through 2025 and 2026, the myth of easy passive income is unraveling fast.

Structurally, much of the market was built on a legal fiction. Popular entry-level properties like officetels—multi-use urban studio buildings—cannot legally be registered as tourist lodgings or commercial accommodation under Korean public sanitation and building laws. Operating them is a criminal offense, leaving everyday investors vulnerable to sudden raids and heavy fines. Beyond the legal exposure, day-to-day hosting quickly mutates from hospitality into grueling digital marketing and janitorial labor, often leaving solitary hosts isolated and burned out.

The Reality Behind the Superhost Fantasy

The romantic image of passive cash flow shatters the moment seasonal volatility hits. Fixed overhead costs—ranging from constant property repairs to winter heating bills for outdoor amenities like jacuzzi tubs that can reach 400,000 won (about $290) a month—pile up regardless of occupancy rates. When bookings drop, hosts face severe financial and psychological stress.

Ultimately, operating an Airbnb in South Korea has evolved into a high-risk compliance game rather than a casual side gig. Without proper licenses, structural suitability, and an appetite for round-the-clock customer service, what looks like an asset quickly becomes a liability. The market is weeding out amateur operators, proving that romanticized side hustles require professional-grade risk management to survive.
