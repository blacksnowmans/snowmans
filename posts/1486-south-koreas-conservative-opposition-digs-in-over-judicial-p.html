South Korea's Conservative Opposition Digs In Over Judicial Probes and Internal Rivalries

October 26, 2026

South Korea’s conservative opposition is escalating its rhetorical assault on the judiciary and the ruling Democratic Party, turning legislative audits into ideological flashpoints while struggling to resolve its own fractured leadership.

  • Clash over the judiciary: Jeong Jeom-sig, floor leader of the main conservative People Power Party (PPP), forcefully rejected the ruling Democratic Party’s push for a parliamentary inquiry into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, branding it a malicious trial balloon aimed at intimidating judges and building momentum for impeachment.
  • War of words: Lee Jun-seok, lawmaker from the conservative splinter Reform Party, mocked senior Democratic lawmaker Choo Mi-ae after she used a dramatic "burning at the stake" metaphor to defend firefighting budget reforms, using the spectacle to attack President Lee Jae-myung’s past gubernatorial record.
  • Shifting conservative deck: The appellate court acquittal of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has unsettled the conservative power balance, sparking renewed jockeying for future influence among Oh, former PPP interim chief Han Dong-hoon, and Lee Jun-seok.
  • Entrenched party command: PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok has survived prolonged calls to step down, setting a longevity record as party chief even as critics argue his survival reflects inertia rather than a coherent plan to unify the right.

A Strategy of Aggressive Defense

The People Power Party’s fiery pushback against the proposed inquiry into Chief Justice Cho reflects deep anxiety over the ongoing legal reckoning under the Lee Jae-myung administration. By casting any scrutiny of the judiciary as an assault on the rule of law, the PPP is attempting to erect a political firewall around the institutional aftermath left by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned. With a Supreme Court justice seat sitting vacant for more than seven months, the opposition's prioritization of partisan confrontation over judicial normalcy signals that institutional reform will remain stalled.

Rather than addressing structural policy issues, prominent opposition figures are channeling their energy into high-visibility friction. Lawmaker Lee Jun-seok's clash with Choo Mi-ae is a case in point: substantive deliberations over public emergency funding were swiftly overshadowed by personal sparring crafted for short-form video clips and hyper-partisan online consumption.

Internal Feuds Trump Governance Alternatives

Mayor Oh Se-hoon's legal clearance has accelerated backstage maneuvering within the conservative camp. With the Lee administration maintaining stable control over state affairs, the right has failed to coalesce around a viable policy alternative. Instead, conservative figures are locked in a three-way contest of attrition—the so-called "Oh-Han-Lee" dynamic—where each faction gauges whether to attack rivals or seek tactical alliances.

Jang Dong-hyeok's ability to cling to the PPP leadership underlines this leadership vacuum. His tenure has endured largely because no single faction commands enough internal consensus to dislodge him. As the party drifts further toward right-wing grievance politics, its capacity to appeal to moderate voters continues to narrow, leaving South Korea’s conservative establishment absorbed in internal point-scoring rather than offering a credible agenda for the country.
