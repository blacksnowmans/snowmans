The "Illusion" Trap: Why Modern Spirituality is Making You Cynical

September 23, 2026

A growing trend in South Korean self-help and spiritual circles—often labeled as "spiritual bypassing" or "the no-self sickness"—is encouraging people to dismiss their daily struggles as mere illusions, a mindset that critics argue is fueling widespread apathy and cynicism.

  • The Paradox: Popular philosophy suggests that if life is an illusion, nothing matters. The rebuttal? If everything is an illusion, then every experience—pain, hunger, and bills—is part of that very "reality."
  • The Danger: By labeling life as "empty," people are using spirituality as a shield to avoid the responsibilities and raw emotions of daily existence.
  • The Impact: This mindset leads to a dangerous cycle of emotional detachment, where individuals ignore their physical and mental well-being under the guise of "transcending" the self.

The Physical Price of Intellectualizing Life

In modern Korean society, where high-pressure work cultures dominate, there is a temptation to embrace concepts from Eastern philosophy, like the Taoist or Buddhist idea of "no-self" (mu-a), to cope with stress. However, many are misinterpreting these teachings. When you ignore your physical needs—like hunger or financial stability—by calling them "fleeting illusions," you aren't achieving enlightenment; you are simply practicing self-deception. The body is the primary antenna through which we experience reality, and trying to "disconnect" from it creates a hollow existence.

Psychologists and philosophers are increasingly pointing out the "symptoms" of this detachment: crippling cynicism, an inability to process genuine emotions, and a false sense of intellectual superiority over those who are still "striving" in the real world. By treating life as a simulation, proponents of this mindset lose the ability to engage with the world authentically. As the adage goes, "Before enlightenment, chop wood and carry water; after enlightenment, chop wood and carry water." True wisdom isn't about escaping reality; it’s about engaging with your responsibilities even after you’ve realized the nature of existence.

Choosing Presence Over Escapism

The solution to this "no-self sickness" is grounded in the physical. It involves shifting focus from abstract thoughts back to the immediate environment: the sensation of your feet on the floor, the taste of a meal, or the physical tasks required by your job. When we treat our daily work—whether it’s a morning commute or paying bills—with the same gravity as spiritual practice, we bridge the gap between abstract philosophy and actual life.

The goal is a delicate balance. One should use the wisdom of "emptiness" to let go of overwhelming obsessions or transient grief, but use the reality of "fullness" to embrace human connections and the tangible work of daily life. Life becomes meaningful not when we declare it an illusion, but when we accept that the very "illusion" of our daily experience is the only reality we have to work with. Stop looking for meaning in the void and start looking at the ground beneath your feet.
