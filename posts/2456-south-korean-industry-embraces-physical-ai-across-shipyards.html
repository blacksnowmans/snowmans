South Korean Industry Embraces Physical AI Across Shipyards, Infrastructure, and Policy

November 18, 2026

Physical AI—artificial intelligence embodied in physical hardware to navigate and manipulate the real world—is rapidly moving from laboratory concept to core operational standard across South Korea’s heavy manufacturing, logistics, and public infrastructure sectors.

  • Heavy Industry Automates Complex Tasks: HD Korea Shipbuilding & Offshore Engineering, South Korea's largest shipbuilder, established a dedicated robotics division to automate shipyard welding and inspection, while construction equipment maker Doosan Bobcat partnered with startup Mind AI to build autonomous excavators.
  • Tech Giants Target Physical Hardware: Global semiconductor firm AMD completed its acquisition of World Labs—founded by AI pioneer Fei-Fei Li—to develop 3D "world models" and challenge Nvidia in specialized hardware for physical AI applications.
  • Infrastructure Agencies Replace Hazardous Labor: The Korea Authority of Land and Infrastructure Safety and the Korea Railroad Research Institute deployed rail-mounted LiDAR robots to conduct high-precision 3D structural inspections inside public railway tunnels.
  • Regional Governments Battle for AI Hubs: Leaders in Jeonbuk Special Self-Governing Province are lobbying the Lee Jae-myung administration for feasibility study exemptions to build a strategic "Physical AI Valley," while lawmakers in Gyeonggi Province face pushback over proposed budget cuts to local AI projects.

Why Physical AI Matters for Korea’s Industrial Base

South Korea’s industrial engine faces severe structural pressures, including persistent labor shortages in heavy manufacturing and strict safety mandates in hazardous work environments. Traditional automation relies on rigid, pre-programmed machinery built for controlled environments. In contrast, Physical AI equips robots with vision systems, real-time sensor processing, and adaptive reasoning, enabling machines to weld inside tight ship hulls, pack irregular food items, or inspect overheating power plant components without human intervention. For Korean conglomerates like HD Korea Shipbuilding and food processing leader CJ CheilJedang, adopting physical AI is no longer an experimental novelty, but a crucial strategy to preserve global margins.

Regulatory Obstacles and the Data Bottleneck

Despite strong momentum from major corporations, South Korea’s physical AI push faces significant friction from regulatory lag and data fragmentation. Domestic safety laws remain vague regarding whether autonomous inspection and delivery robots qualify as vehicles or pedestrians, creating legal uncertainty for commercial deployment. Furthermore, regional manufacturing hubs like South Gyeongsang Province are encountering data bottlenecks: disparate data formats across sub-tier suppliers prevent AI models from operating seamlessly across factory floors. Unless the National Assembly accelerates regulatory reform and establishes unified industrial data standards, technical breakthroughs risk remaining trapped in pilot programs rather than driving broad economic transformation.
