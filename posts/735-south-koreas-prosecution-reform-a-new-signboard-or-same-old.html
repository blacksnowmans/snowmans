South Korea's Prosecution Reform: A New Signboard or Same Old Power?

October 05, 2026

  • South Korea's prosecution service has officially been restructured under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, splitting investigative and indicting powers into two new bodies: the Prosecution Service (for indictments) and the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, an administrative body under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety.
  • Despite promises of complete reform, critics argue that the core powers of former prosecutors remain intact through subordinate decrees, secondary rules, and secondments to key government ministries like the Financial Services Commission.
  • The incomplete rollout—with the new investigation agency operating at roughly 60% staffing capacity—has sparked fierce public debate and deep frustration among supporters who feel genuine reform has been sidelined by political maneuvering.

When South Korea officially dissolved its traditional prosecution office, the move was billed as a monumental turning point for the nation's legal system. The historic revision aimed to permanently sever the link between criminal investigations and indictments, clipping the wings of an institution long criticized for overreach. Yet, looking past the shiny new nameplates, reality tells a far more sobering story.

Rather than dismantling the prosecution's formidable machinery, the reorganization appears to have preserved its core influence through bureaucratic loopholes. Subordinate decrees and investigative notification clauses still allow prosecutors to exert indirect control over police work. Meanwhile, former prosecutors continue to occupy key posts within the Ministry of Justice and government agencies, effectively keeping the old power networks alive under a new banner.

Political Fallout and the Search for Scapegoats

This institutional half-measure has triggered an intense political backlash. Recent reports of a four-hour private dinner between the president and former opposition party leader Jeong Cheong-rae—immediately followed by rumors of Jeong being tapped for Minister of Justice—have added fuel to the fire. Critics and disillusioned supporters view the potential appointment not as a gesture of political unity, but as a calculated trap designed to saddle an outsider with the blame for the administration's faltering reform agenda.

Placing an incoming figure into a hostile ministry filled with entrenched legal veterans, without granting them the structural leverage or institutional shields needed to take control, looks less like effective governance and more like political exhaustion. When reforms fall short of public expectations, shifting the burden onto a high-profile sacrificial lamb does little to restore public trust.

Ultimately, the saga of South Korea's prosecution overhaul serves as a sharp reminder that institutional names and legislative titles mean little without the political will to surrender entrenched privilege. As political maneuvering continues behind closed doors, it falls squarely on the shoulders of an attentive public and civil society to cut through the noise and demand genuine accountability.
