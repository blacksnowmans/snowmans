The Roots of Radicalization: Why South Korea’s Youth are Turning to the Far-Right

October 31, 2026

The radicalization and intensifying gender conflict among South Koreans in their teens and twenties are symptoms of structural economic despair and a political establishment that has failed to offer a viable future.

  • Structural Frustration: High-stakes competition for dwindling resources has fueled a sense of relative deprivation among the "1020 generation" (those in their teens and 20s).
  • Political Void: While the government focuses on high-level judicial reforms, young voters feel their immediate needs—like housing and job security—are being ignored.
  • Misdirected Anger: Without clear economic alternatives, youth frustration is often channeled into far-right ideologies or gender-based animosity.
  • Prosecutorial Reform: Calls remain for the total restructuring of the Prosecution Service to prevent the return of the "omnipotent" legal power seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure.

Economic Despair Over Ideology

The rise of far-right sentiment among young South Koreans is not a simple case of ideological deviance. Instead, it is a "structural tragedy" born from a society that demands infinite competition while providing fewer opportunities. When the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea’s ruling liberal party) focuses primarily on political victories and the success of the Lee Jae-myung administration, it risks alienating a demographic that measures political success by the cost of rent and the availability of stable jobs.

In the current political climate, "gender conflict" has become a surface-level manifestation of a deeper crisis: the failure of the social system to distribute resources fairly. For young men who feel they have been stripped of even minimal opportunities, far-right rhetoric provides an easy, if toxic, justification for their anger. Critics argue that unless the government moves beyond political slogans to address the wealth gap and job precariousness, this demographic will continue to drift toward extremism, fueled by short-form social media algorithms that thrive on outrage.

The Path to Institutional Reform

Beyond economics, the political discourse remains heavily focused on completing the overhaul of South Korea’s legal system. A key proposal mentioned is the transformation of the Prosecution Service into a "Public Prosecution Office." This would involve stripping the agency of its investigative powers and its constitutionally protected exclusive right to request warrants, limiting its role strictly to indictments and maintaining public trials. This is seen as a necessary safeguard to ensure that the "prosecutorial dictatorship" associated with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in power—who was impeached and is currently imprisoned—cannot be repeated.

However, for these reforms to lead to true social integration, they must be paired with a "livelihood vision." Figures within the reform movement, such as those inspired by former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae, emphasize that institutional change is meaningless if it does not improve the lives of individual citizens. The challenge for President Lee’s government is to prove that "people’s success" is the ultimate goal of "regime success." Without a concrete plan for housing, labor rights, and a robust social safety net, the political victory of the ruling party will remain hollow to a generation struggling to survive.
