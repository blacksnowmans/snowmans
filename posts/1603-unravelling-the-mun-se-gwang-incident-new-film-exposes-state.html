Unravelling the Mun Se-gwang Incident: New Film Exposes State Cover-Ups in 1974 Seoul

October 28, 2026

A new film titled The Assassins is reigniting scrutiny over one of South Korea's darkest historical flashpoints: the 1974 assassination attempt on authoritarian leader Park Chung-hee, exposing deep-seated state fabrication and long-buried cover-ups.

    What happened: The film investigates the Mun Se-gwang incident—the 1974 shooting targeting then-President Park Chung-hee that resulted in the tragic death of First Lady Yuk Young-su, though ballistic evidence and official records point to severe discrepancies, including an unprosecuted presidential bodyguard. What it changes: It strips away the neat North Korean-orchestrated narrative pushed by South Korea's military dictatorship at the time, highlighting botched timelines, impossible physical profiles of the shooter, and Japanese police investigations that directly contradicted Seoul's official story. Who is affected: History buffs, international film audiences, and South Korea’s truth and reconciliation bodies, which face renewed pressure to pry open sealed state archives.

The Flawed Ballistics and Missing Bullets of 1974

At the heart of The Assassins lies a stubborn physical impossibility: the bullets. Official accounts pinned the chaos entirely on Mun Se-gwang, a pro-North Korean sympathizer living in Japan. Yet, ballistics and contemporary records reveal a sloppy web of lies engineered by the Korea Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA)—South Korea's feared former spy agency—to serve a political script rather than actual justice.

Investigators discovered that a bullet fired during the melee ended up lodged in the ceiling—an impossible trajectory for a lone gunman targeting the podium, but entirely consistent with a stray round from a security detail. Even more damning, forensic files originally concluded that the high school student killed in the crossfire, Jang Bong-hwa, was struck by a bullet fired by a security guard standing right next to President Park, not Mun. Yet, while Mun was swiftly railroaded to execution under charges of treason, the guard who fired the fatal shot was reduced to a mere "reference witness" and faced zero legal consequences.

The film also highlights the sheer absurdity of the official narrative surrounding the shooter. Mun was severely nearsighted—so blind that actors portraying him required thick, Coke-bottle glasses—and lacked any tactical training suitable for a high-stakes hit. Furthermore, Tokyo police investigations completely dismantled Seoul's claim that Mun received direct orders aboard a North Korean ferry, the Mangyongbong, proving the ship wasn't even docked in Osaka on the claimed date. Japanese authorities openly rejected the South Korean government's narrative, exposing a crude fabrication built to justify authoritarian security controls.

Art, Politics, and the Fight for Historical Truth

While the film has won acclaim abroad—taking home a runner-up audience award at the Toronto International Film Festival and packing theaters in Singapore despite clumsy censorship attempts by the conservative opposition People Power Party—it strikes a raw nerve at home. The film's festival run faced obstruction when People Power Party lawmakers demanded overseas screenings be canceled, a ham-fisted bid that only amplified global interest in the psychological thriller.

This discomfort reveals a broader truth: South Korea's historical wounds remain heavily contested terrain. Past efforts by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission to probe the incident stalled out amid political interference, leaving vital ballistic evidence locked away in National Intelligence Service servers. With former conservative leader Park Geun-hye's era long past and disgraced ex-President Yoon Seok-yeol now imprisoned following his impeachment, critics argue that the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung must finally open these sealed files. As The Assassins demonstrates, letting authorities bury inconvenient history under the guise of national security means condemning the public to live inside a manipulated fiction.
