South Korea's Progressive Bloc Struggles with Internal Power and Tribalism

October 02, 2026

South Korea's liberal and progressive camp is fracturing from within as blind tribalism, defensive power hoarding, and a refusal to tolerate internal dissent paralyze its reform agenda.

    Loyal supporters feel alienated: Long-time progressive voters and regional activists—once proud to champion the liberal cause—are falling silent out of embarrassment over petty internal fights and hypocrisy.
  • Power consolidation over reform: The main opposition Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's primary liberal party led by Lee Jae-myung, is accused of using the "pro-Lee" faction to monopolize power and freeze out smaller progressive allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party.
  • Weaponized unity: Calls to protect the camp from conservative rivals are regularly misused to muzzle legitimate internal criticism and silence whistleblowers.
  • Eroding democratic checks: When political leaders treat policy debates as ego clashes rather than matters of governance, voters lose faith in the political process entirely.

The Trap of Blind Tribalism and Silenced Allies

Every political movement needs honest brokers, but South Korea's progressive camp is rapidly losing its conscience. Once-vocal supporters in tough conservative strongholds—like affluent Seoul districts or Daegu—report feeling too ashamed to defend their party anymore. They feel used. Leaders have weaponized their followers' good intentions, demanding total obedience under the banner of fighting the conservative People Power Party. Dissent is branded as treason or "internal shooting," effectively crushing the tradition of rational debate that once defined the progressive movement.

This dynamic creates a dangerous echo chamber. Rather than encouraging self-correction, top figures use loyalty as a shield. Politicians who once aligned with former factions quickly pivot to whatever camp holds power to ensure their own survival. When politicians swap principles for proximity to the leader, the entire concept of reform becomes a hollow slogan designed to protect individual careers rather than advance public good.

Blocking Allies and Sabotaging Legislative Power

The rift is not just cultural; it is institutional. The DPK's refusal to help the smaller Rebuilding Korea Party secure official negotiating bloc status in parliament exposes a deeper hypocrisy. Without bloc status, minor parties are locked out of key committee rooms where actual legislation is hammered out. By keeping a tight two-way dynamic against the conservatives rather than empowering its progressive partners, the DPK ensures it holds a monopoly on power—even if it costs the broader movement any real chance at sweeping reform.

Ultimately, the crisis facing South Korea's progressives is moral, not tactical. When political survival replaces public service, and when supporters are shamed into silence, democracy suffers on both sides of the aisle. If the progressive camp cannot learn to police its own abuses of power, it risks becoming the very thing it once fought against.
