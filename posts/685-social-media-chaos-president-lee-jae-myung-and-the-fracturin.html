Social Media Chaos: President Lee Jae-myung and the Fracturing of South Korean Politics

October 04, 2026

South Korean politics is increasingly defined by emotional polarization and fragmentation, as digital platforms turn political debate into a series of hostile, high-speed confrontations. A recent, public clash involving President Lee Jae-myung and key figures within the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal opposition party) highlights how social media is undermining the deliberative nature of democracy.

  • Presidential Intervention: On October 4, President Lee Jae-myung used X (formerly Twitter) to criticize the focus on "performative reform," arguing that political reform should prioritize people's livelihoods over grandstanding.
  • Inner-Party Infighting: The message triggered an immediate, aggressive social media spat between senior figures, including Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae and Representative Park Beom-kye.
  • Democracy at Risk: Prominent liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min has warned that the tendency to silence internal dissent and critics—"shutting mouths"—risks systemic corruption and the alienation of the public.

The Perils of "Performative Reform"

President Lee’s post was widely interpreted as a rebuke of hardline reformers within his own camp, particularly those who have publicly protested his recent nomination of Kim Ji-yong as the head of the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. By stating that "reform should not be a tool for attack or a show to gain attention," Lee effectively challenged the faction within his party that prioritizes radical signaling over incremental, practical governance.

However, instead of calming the waters, the President’s directive acted as a catalyst for deeper division. The immediate response from party heavyweights like Choo Mi-ae—who accused the administration of "cowering" before domestic enemies—and the sharp, dismissive retorts from figures like Park Beom-kye, illustrated a breakdown in internal party communication. When senior leaders engage in public bickering rather than policy discussion, they send a message to the public that the party is more interested in internal power struggles than national stability.

Rhyu Si-min’s Warning: The "Fish Market" of Power

Rhyu Si-min, a highly influential liberal intellectual, has offered a sobering critique of how the current administration and its supporters handle criticism. Using a "fish market" analogy, Rhyu argued that power, much like a fish market, inevitably produces a "fishy smell." The problem arises not from the smell itself, but from the reaction to it: when those in power try to hide the odor or silence those who point it out, the market eventually begins to rot.

Rhyu argues that the core value of democracy is not ensuring "wise leaders" make perfect choices, but building systems that prevent even the worst politicians from causing irreparable harm. By creating digital echo chambers where only praise is permitted and criticism is branded as "betrayal," the political camp risks insulating itself from reality.

As the administration approaches the midpoint of its term, the pressure to deliver results—particularly on high-stakes promises like the separation of investigation and prosecution powers—remains high. If the political establishment continues to prioritize social media "wins" over institutional processes, they risk losing the very public trust that brought them to power. The lesson remains clear: when a movement stops listening to its critics, it stops evolving and starts decaying.
