From Elite Scholar to Buddhist Monk and Back: What One Korean Thinker’s Spiritual Odyssey Teaches Us About Burnout

September 10, 2026

  • What happened: Kim Han-soo, an elite South Korean scholar with degrees from Tokyo University who was once a devout Christian missionary, shaved his head to become a Buddhist monk before returning to secular life.
  • What it changes: Her journey—chronicled in her recent book—reframes meditation not as a passive relaxation tool, but as an aggressive disruption of social conditioning and burnout.
  • Who is affected: Professionals trapped in the relentless grind of corporate or academic achievement who feel alienated from their own choices.

The Trap of Borrowed Ambitions

Modern South Korean society offers a hyper-defined script for success: top universities, prestigious employers, and relentless multi-tasking. Yet beneath the veneer of high achievement lies an epidemic of burnout and existential dread. Kim’s trajectory—moving from evangelical Christian youth to international relations elitist, then to a Zen monastery, and finally back to secular life under a pen name meaning "sharing the deer's discovery"—highlights a universal dilemma. We spend our lives chasing "fake desires" injected by parents and peers, leaving our internal houses completely empty.

Neuroscience increasingly backs up what ancient practitioners understood: the modern brain is trapped in a loop of frantic reactivity. Constant multi-tasking overactivates the amygdala, hijacking rational thought. Meditation is not about escaping reality; it is a tactical intervention to quiet the noise and reclaim cognitive sovereignty. By breaking the cycle of external validation, individuals can shift from running on society's tracks to building their own.

Rewiring the Mind Through Small Rituals

True mental rest does not require a dramatic career upheaval or a three-year stint in a monastery. Instead, Kim advocates for micro-interventions—such as three-minute breathing exercises during a red light or morning grounding routines—that leverage the brain's neuroplasticity. These tiny acts of turning inward act as an anchor against the digital noise and social comparison engines that dictate modern anxiety.

Ultimately, the core message of Kim's journey is deceptively simple: stop living like a homeless person in your own body, constantly chasing future goals or replaying past regrets. Treating yourself with the curiosity of a new romance—asking yourself how you actually feel, rather than how you are performing—is the fastest route out of burnout. In a culture obsessed with external credentials, the most radical act is simply learning to inhabit your own mind.
