The AI Cheating Crisis: South Korea Scrambles to Protect the National Exam

November 17, 2026

South Korea is introducing draconian security measures for its upcoming national university entrance exam following a sophisticated AI-assisted cheating scandal that has rocked the country’s professional licensing system.

  • High-Tech Fraud: A top scorer in a national tax accountant exam was arrested for using a modified calculator and AI to receive answers, exposing a black market for cheating gear on Telegram.
  • CSAT Lockdown: The Ministry of Education rejected calls to delay the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), opting instead for invasive checks including stickers on clothing buttons to hide pinhole cameras.
  • Market Failure: Despite a surge in pet ownership, Korea’s pet insurance market remains stalled at a 2.1% enrollment rate due to high premiums and lack of standardized veterinary fees.
  • Diplomatic Stakes: President Lee Jae-myung, currently at the ASEAN summit in Manila, is working to prevent "Korea Passing"—the fear of Seoul being sidelined in regional security talks—while securing the extradition of a major drug lord.

AI vs. The Exam State

The arrest of a suspect identified as "Mr. Han," a top scorer in the tax accountant exam, has sent shockwaves through South Korea’s education and legal sectors. Han, a graduate of a specialized robotics high school, allegedly modified a calculator to link via Bluetooth to a smartphone, displaying AI-generated answers on the calculator’s screen. The case has exposed a thriving underground market on Telegram, where Chinese vendors sell "exam support gear"—including smart glasses and pinhole cameras—for between $450 and $750 (600,000 to 1 million KRW).

This technological leap in cheating has forced the government into a defensive crouch. Prime Minister Han Seong-sook announced that anyone caught cheating will now face a 10-year ban from exams, up from the previous one-year penalty. For the upcoming CSAT—the high-stakes exam that determines a student's career trajectory—proctors will now inspect eyeglass frames, check sleeves and collars, and even place stickers over buttons to prevent the use of micro-cameras. While these measures aim to restore trust, they risk turning testing centers into high-pressure security checkpoints, further stressing a generation already defined by academic burnout.

Market Realities and Regional Power

While the domestic focus remains on education, the Lee Jae-myung administration is navigating complex market and diplomatic hurdles. The pet insurance sector, valued at about $95 million (129.1 billion KRW), remains anemic compared to Japan or the UK. Without a mandatory standardized fee system for veterinarians, insurers are hiking premiums, leaving owners to pay out of pocket for rising costs. This structural inefficiency mirrors broader issues in the Korean service sector, where rapid market growth often outpaces regulatory frameworks.

On the international stage, President Lee’s presence at the ASEAN+3 summit in Manila is a calculated move to ensure South Korea remains central to North Korean diplomacy. With major powers like the U.S. and China signaling potential direct shifts in their North Korea policies, the administration is pushing for regional support to maintain a lead role. The successful extradition of drug kingpin Park Kang-ryeol from the Philippines serves as a rare "win" for cross-border law enforcement, but the larger challenge remains: keeping Korea’s voice relevant in a rapidly shifting geopolitical landscape.

Cheat Sheet: New CSAT Security Measures

  • Button Stickers: All buttons on a student's clothing will be covered with stickers to block pinhole camera lenses.
  • Glasses Inspection: Students must place glasses on their desks before the first period for a manual inspection for "smart" technology.
  • Emergency Alerts: The government will send a synchronized emergency text alert during the exam to trigger any concealed mobile phones that were not surrendered.
  • 10-Year Ban: New legislation aims to increase the penalty for cheating from a one-year suspension to a decade-long disqualification.
