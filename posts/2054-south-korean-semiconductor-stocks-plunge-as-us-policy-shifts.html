South Korean Semiconductor Stocks Plunge as U.S. Policy Shifts and Weak Governance Fuel Uncertainty

November 06, 2026

South Korean semiconductor stocks are struggling to recover despite a global AI boom, as new U.S. environmental regulations and a watered-down domestic corporate reform bill drive away long-term foreign investors.

  • U.S. Political Shifts: New power grid regulations in states like Texas—home to 25% of U.S. data centers—have sparked fears of a slowdown in AI infrastructure spending, directly hitting demand for Korean memory chips.
  • Foreign Capital Flight: International investors have sold off roughly $143 billion (200 trillion won) this year, replacing stable, long-term funds with high-frequency "volatility traders" who profit from market instability.
  • Governance Failures: Proposed revisions to the Commercial Code aimed at protecting minority shareholders were weakened in the National Assembly, maintaining the "Korea Discount" that keeps domestic stock prices suppressed.
  • Samsung Under Pressure: Despite generating nearly $71 billion (100 trillion won) in cash flow, Samsung Electronics is facing formal demands from institutional investors for immediate share cancellations to boost its sagging valuation.

The U.S. Energy Bottleneck and the "Korea Discount"

The recent crash in Korean chip stocks, even as Wall Street hit record highs, is rooted in a massive "AI infrastructure moratorium" brewing in the United States. Roughly 35% of U.S. data center power demand is currently caught in regions facing political or environmental regulatory shifts. In Texas, a surge in power connection requests reached 474 gigawatts—nearly ten times current capacity. This has led to local freezes on new permits, causing investors to fear that the peak of the AI hardware cycle has already passed. While Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (the world's leading memory chip makers) maintain dominant market positions, the uncertainty regarding U.S. tech spending is paralyzing their short-term momentum.

Compounding these external shocks is the chronic failure of the Korean government and legislature to reform corporate governance. The Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung) and the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition) recently reached a compromise that gutted a key investor protection bill. The proposed "mandatory tender offer" rule, which would have required corporate acquirers to offer the same premium price to 100% of shareholders, was watered down to just 50%. This signals to global investors that the interests of majority owners—often the founding families of chaebol conglomerates—still take precedence over small shareholders.

Policy Divergence and Real Estate Stability

The contrast with regional rivals is stark. While China has introduced aggressive measures like low-interest loans for share buybacks and strict controls on new IPOs to support its markets, Korean authorities have been criticized for a "hands-off" approach that leaves retail investors vulnerable. This lack of institutional support is why the "Korea Discount" persists, even for a company like Samsung that holds massive cash reserves. Institutional investors are now openly petitioning the Samsung board and Chairman Lee Jae-yong to accelerate share cancellations, arguing that current management is prioritizing inheritance tax issues and group control over shareholder value.

Amid the stock market gloom, the Lee Jae-myung administration is seeing more success in the housing market. By strengthening holding taxes on high-value properties and prioritizing actual residents in tax policy, the government has managed to cool prices in the "Gangnam 3-gu" (Seoul's three wealthiest districts: Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa). This stands in contrast to the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the impeached former leader currently in prison), whose tenure was marked by volatile real estate spikes. For the current government, the challenge remains translating this domestic policy stability into a more robust and fair capital market that can withstand global political headwinds.
