Lee Jun-seok Sues Over Revived "Harvard Degree" Conspiracy Theories

November 15, 2026

Lee Jun-seok, the former leader of the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—is taking legal action against far-right influencers who have resurrected decade-old, debunked allegations questioning his academic credentials from Harvard University.

  • The Accusation: Jeon Han-gil, a prominent internet lecturer and influencer, publicly demanded Lee "reveal" his diploma and claimed Lee entered Harvard via a recommendation from the late liberal President Roh Moo-hyun.
  • The Response: Lee dismissed the claims as "pure fiction" and announced a defamation lawsuit for November 16, targeting the intentional spread of false information.
  • The History: These allegations first surfaced in 2011 when Lee entered politics; he has previously provided diplomas and transcripts, and investigative authorities cleared the matter years ago.
  • The Motive: Analysts view the revival of the controversy as a calculated "noise marketing" tactic used by far-right YouTube channels to drive views and donations from a base distrustful of Lee’s moderate stances.

A Return to "Post-Truth" Politics

The resurgence of the Harvard controversy highlights a persistent strain of "post-truth" politics within the Korean far-right. Despite Lee having provided official documentation multiple times over the last decade, conspiracy theorists continue to scrutinize minute details like font choices or the placement of university seals to claim forgery. This phenomenon is reminiscent of the infamous "Tajinyo" incident of 2010, where a massive online community obsessively harassed a famous singer over his Stanford degree despite official verification.

By linking Lee to a recommendation from the late President Roh Moo-hyun, influencers are attempting to trigger the tribal instincts of conservative voters. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party, the conservative opposition is struggling to find its footing. With Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol currently imprisoned following his impeachment, the internal friction within the right wing has turned inward, with radical influencers attacking established figures like Lee Jun-seok to gain leverage and "purity" points within their digital ecosystems.

The Economics of Political Slander

This conflict is as much about money as it is about politics. In South Korea, the far-right YouTube ecosystem has become a lucrative industry where provocative thumbnails and outlandish claims translate directly into ad revenue and "Super Chat" donations. For these creators, the truth is secondary to engagement; even a lawsuit can be framed as "persecution" to solicit further financial support from their followers. Lee’s decision to pursue criminal charges suggests he believes that only the threat of severe legal and financial penalties can break this cycle of profitable misinformation.

Ultimately, this dispute signals a degradation of political discourse. Instead of debating the policy failures or successes of the Lee Jae-myung government, the political sphere is being dragged back into a 13-year-old argument about a diploma. Unless the judiciary sets a clear precedent regarding the accountability of digital influencers, the Korean public square will likely remain vulnerable to these cyclical, manufactured scandals that prioritize clicks over facts.
