South Korea's Economic Paradox: Why Record Exports Haven't Saved Approval Ratings

October 09, 2026

While South Korea’s semiconductor industry is posting record-breaking profits, the government under President Lee Jae-myung is facing a sharp decline in public support as the average citizen's quality of life steadily deteriorates.

  • The Semiconductor Illusion: Top tech firms are seeing massive gains, but this wealth is not "trickling down" to the broader economy.
  • Wage Stagnation: Real wages for average workers have declined for two consecutive years, falling even lower than during the tenure of the previous administration.
  • Domestic Consumption Crisis: Household spending as a share of GDP has plummeted below 45%, a level rarely seen in developed nations outside of pandemic periods.
  • Small Business Collapse: Self-employed individuals are struggling with retail sales figures that remain worse than those recorded during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The Disconnect Between Macro Indicators and Reality

The core of the current political crisis lies in a widening gap between statistical "success" and the lived experience of the public. Supporters of the administration point to booming exports—particularly in semiconductors—and high GDP growth projections. However, these figures are largely fueled by a specific, high-tech industry cycle that does not reflect the health of the broader labor market.

Economist Choi Bae-geun argues that the government is trapped in an illusion of its own making. While corporations rake in billions, the average worker’s real salary has dropped to approximately $2,700 (3.6 million KRW) per month—a figure that has not improved in years. For temporary and day laborers, the situation is even more dire, with real earnings falling to levels not seen since 2013. The administration’s reliance on one-time support measures, such as small holiday vouchers, has done little to address the systemic collapse of domestic demand.

Debt, Liquidity, and the "Balance Sheet Recession"

The economic strategy of the Lee administration appears to be failing the "man on the street." Excessive liquidity from the semiconductor boom has not fueled productive investment in small businesses; instead, it has leaked into the real estate market, driving up costs for households already struggling with high debt. Financial distress is mounting, with bad debt among households and self-employed individuals hitting levels not seen since the 2008 global financial crisis.

This has led to what economists call a "balance sheet recession." Faced with high interest rates and falling incomes, households and businesses are prioritizing debt repayment over spending and investment. By focusing solely on export metrics, the government is ignoring the structural rot in domestic consumption. Unless the administration shifts its focus from macroeconomic vanity metrics to the urgent needs of small-business owners and low-wage earners, the downward trend in public support is likely to continue.
