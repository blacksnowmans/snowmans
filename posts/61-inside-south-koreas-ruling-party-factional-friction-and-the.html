Inside South Korea's Ruling Party: Factional Friction and the Battle for President Lee's Inner Circle

September 26, 2026

  • Factions in the Open: A bruising internal party congress has left South Korea's ruling Democratic Party fractured, with senior figures like lawmaker Choi Min-hee shedding weight from stress and high-profile figures clashing over control.
  • The President's Plea: President Lee Jae-myung has attempted to bridge historical divides by declaring unity across progressive factions, but critics warn the leadership is failing to heal deep wounds among core supporters.
  • High-Stakes Appointments: Scrutiny is mounting over looming changes to the presidential secretariat and key prosecutorial posts, with watchdogs warning that insider deal-making threatens the government's reform agenda.
  • Shifting Polls: Grassroots feedback shows a noticeable dip in unyielding support, forcing party strategists to weigh the risks of alienating traditional bases in pursuit of centrist voters.

South Korea's political landscape is bracing for a tense holiday period as the ruling Democratic Party—the center-left driving force behind President Lee Jae-myung—grapples with severe internal friction. Beneath the surface of shifting approval ratings lies a bitter struggle between entrenched party loyalists and newer political networks. Commentary from prominent political figures on recent digital broadcasts has laid bare the exhaustion, backstabbing, and factional posturing that threaten to derail the administration's policy goals.

At the heart of the tension is a growing anxiety over who controls access to the levers of executive power. President Lee's recent overtures calling for unity—invoking the legacies of former progressive leaders Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—aim to rally a fragmented base. Yet, party insiders argue that leadership has been sluggish in translating rhetoric into structural reconciliation, leaving room for opportunistic figures and divisive media framing to widen the cracks.

The Cost of Factional Warfare and the Personnel Crisis

The human toll of these internal battles has become a talking point in progressive circles. Lawmakers have openly discussed the physical strain of party infighting, while independent analysts point out that lazy media framing—constantly applying reductive "anti-Lee" or factional labels—serves only to manufacture division. Meanwhile, key personnel decisions within the presidential office and upcoming legislative battles risk repeating past mistakes if institutional vetting systems are bypassed for political expediency.

Commentators stress that the administration cannot afford to rely on closed-door horse-trading or reward careerists who switch loyalties based on shifting political winds. As debate swirls over contentious appointments in prosecutorial reform and the presidential secretariat, critics argue that the party needs robust internal debate—complete with a functioning "red team" to challenge groupthink—rather than enforced silence. Without systemic reform and genuine reconciliation between old guards and newer factions, the ruling bloc risks squandering its hard-won political capital.
