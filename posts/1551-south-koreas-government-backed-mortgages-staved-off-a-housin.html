South Korea’s Government-Backed Mortgages Staved Off a Housing Crash — At a Dangerous Cost

October 27, 2026

State-backed subsidized mortgage programs designed to shield South Korea’s property market have artificially halted necessary price corrections, leaving young buyers saddled with mounting debt as commercial mortgage rates climb past 5 percent.

  • What happened: After strict debt-service ratio (DSR) limits caused South Korean household debt to shrink in 2022 for the first time in modern history, state interventions—such as the 40 trillion won ($29 billion) Special Bokumjari Loan—bypassed leverage caps to prop up falling apartment prices.
  • What changed: Low-interest policy loans like the Newborn Special Loan shifted buyer demand toward mid-tier homes under 900 million won ($650,000) in outer Seoul and surrounding Gyeonggi Province, driving wage-earner mortgage debt up by 11.1 percent in 2024.
  • Who is affected: First-time buyers in their 30s and 40s who stretched their finances during the artificial liquidity boom now face severe repayment strain as the Lee Jae-myung administration reins in state lending and commercial mortgage rates top 5.5 percent.

From Market Bailouts to Household Debt Vulnerability

South Korea’s real estate market faced a natural downturn between 2022 and 2023 when rising global interest rates collided with stricter DSR regulations, which cap loan repayments relative to income. However, during Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, authorities stepped in to prevent a wider development collapse—most notably rescuing the massive Dunchon Jugong redevelopment project in eastern Seoul. By flooding the market with 40 trillion won ($29 billion) in low-interest Special Bokumjari Loans, the government offered an explicit workaround to DSR caps, effectively transferring developer strain onto individual homebuyers.

In 2024, the strategy shifted toward the Newborn Special Loan, a policy ostensibly aimed at easing housing costs for growing families. Offering 1 to 2 percent interest rates on homes valued up to 900 million won ($650,000), it exempted borrowers from DSR checks. Rather than addressing underlying housing affordability, the policy encouraged young couples to take on maximum leverage. Over 36 percent of these buyers rushed into mid-priced apartments in Gyeonggi Province, pushing home mortgage debt among salaried workers to record growth even as general credit loans contracted.

The Shift to Commercial Loans and Growing Disparities

The tide began turning as household debt approached critical levels. Under the current Lee Jae-myung administration, fiscal authorities introduced tighter curbs through the "June 27 measures," cutting caps on state-subsidized mortgages like the Didimdol loan from 500 million won to 400 million won ($290,000). Yet this created a new paradox: with policy loans scaled back, desperate buyers turned to commercial bank mortgages, which allow borrowing up to 600 million won ($435,000) for properties priced under 1.5 billion won ($1.1 million). Homeowners are now taking on private bank debt at rates above 5.5 percent without government interest subsidies.

This debt-driven floor has distorted market valuations across the Seoul Capital Region. While price gaps within Seoul proper appear to narrow because strict caps slowed sales of ultra-luxury high-rises in Gangnam, broader wealth disparity across the capital region hit a record 9.7-fold ratio between top and bottom quintiles. Outside key industrial hubs, regional real estate remains severely depressed. As interest rate pressures intensify through 2026, overleveraged buyers who bought near peak valuations find themselves exposed to severe financial stress on an artificially supported market floor.
