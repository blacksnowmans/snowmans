Global Left-Wing Failures Hold Warning for South Korea’s New Government

October 09, 2026

  • What happened: Across Europe, Canada, and Brazil, progressive and left-leaning governments are losing ground to the far-right due to perceived incompetence, economic stagnation, and over-reliance on ideology over everyday issues.
  • The Korean context: With the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party, now in power under President Lee Jae-myung following the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Seok-yeol, critics argue the administration faces the same existential test.
  • The stakes: Observers warn that if South Korea's liberals fail to enact tangible reforms—pointing to housing costs, inequality, and institutional corruption—they risk triggering the same populist backlash seen elsewhere in the democratic world.

A global wave of political discontent is sweeping established democracies, and the root cause is simple: when left-wing and progressive parties win power, they routinely fail to change ordinary people's lives. From Brazil’s struggles under President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva to the rise of the far-right across Europe and Canada, voters are turning away from traditional centrists and liberals who compromise with elites instead of delivering structural change. For South Korea, where the Democratic Party now controls the government following the downfall of Yoon Seok-yeol, this international trend is not a distant foreign spectacle—it is a flashing red warning light.

Lessons from Abroad: Brazil’s Compromises and Britain’s Public Housing Pivot

Decades ago, Brazilian leader Lula diagnosed a fundamental flaw in the modern left: once elected, politicians spend too much energy appeasing conservative media, financial systems, and corporate interests, ultimately abandoning the working-class voters who put them there. In Brazil, a failure to decisively reform corrupt judicial institutions and curb inequality has left the door wide open for far-right movements. This dynamic mirrors the risks facing South Korea’s current administration, where moderation and hesitation to take on entrenched conglomerates or conservative media risks alienating its base.

Conversely, recent moves by the UK Labour Party offer a potential playbook for survival. Shifting decisively away from decades of market deregulation, British leaders have proposed massive public housing expansions to tackle soaring rents, threats to nationalize essential utilities like water and energy, and free in-home care programs funded entirely through taxation. Whether these aggressive public-ownership policies will rescue the global left remains to be seen, but they provide a stark contrast to the technocratic paralysis that has plagued progressives in North America and Europe, where identity politics and unaddressed immigration pressures have alienated working-class voters.

The Urgency of Real Reform in Seoul

South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party can no longer afford to view itself as a permanent underdog or opposition movement; it is now the establishment, holding executive power. The public did not hand liberals victory so they could engage in polite consensus-building with conservative rivals or protect internal party factions. They voted to root out deep-seated corruption across the judiciary and media, and to ease the crushing economic pressures—particularly exorbitant housing prices—facing younger generations.

If the Lee Jae-myung administration yields to compromise and leaves the country's entrenched power structures intact, it will face the same swift and brutal electoral punishment seen across the democratic world. The lesson from Brazil, Europe, and North America is uncompromising: reform or perish. South Korea’s progressives have a narrow window to prove they are genuinely competent and responsive to the material needs of everyday citizens, or risk watching public frustration boil over into a severe conservative resurgence.
