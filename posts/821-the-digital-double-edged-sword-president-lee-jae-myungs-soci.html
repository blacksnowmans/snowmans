The Digital Double-Edged Sword: President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Strategy

October 08, 2026

President Lee Jae-myung’s reliance on social media—once his greatest political asset—is increasingly sparking internal concern within his own party, the Democratic Party of Korea, as critics argue it is trapping the administration in a bubble of performative outrage rather than broad national governance.

  • What happened: President Lee has ramped up his social media activity, using platforms to bypass traditional media and communicate directly with his supporters, despite growing discomfort from within the ruling Democratic Party.
  • The "Success" Trap: The strategy relies on the same methods Lee used as Mayor of Seongnam, where he rewarded subordinates for social media engagement and used online mobilization to bypass systemic obstacles.
  • Political Risk: Observers warn of "algorithmic distortion," where the president mistakes the amplified voices of a vocal minority for the general consensus of the public.
  • What changes: The president’s posts are increasingly interpreted by supporters as "marching orders," leading to aggressive online behavior that risks alienating the moderate voters essential for long-term stability.

The Anatomy of a Digital Strategy

For President Lee, social media is more than a communication tool; it is the infrastructure of his political identity. Having risen from a local government official to the presidency, Lee views the "direct link" to his base as the only reliable way to cut through a hostile media environment. However, this relies on a feedback loop established in the mid-2010s with his "Finger Revolutionary Army"—a loose, highly organized digital base that provided him with immense leverage. Today, that legacy is being revived as a "New Finger Revolutionary Army," where even a simple "share" button click from the President can signal his supporters to attack specific political targets.

The danger is that the President is increasingly confusing the "volume" of his digital support with the "value" of actual public opinion. While his posts receive immediate validation from his base, this creates a dangerous sense of security. Policy decisions become filtered through the binary lens of "us versus them," effectively turning political discourse into a matter of faith rather than debate. When the President uses his platform to counter criticism by rallying his base rather than engaging in dialogue, he risks delegitimizing the very mechanisms of democratic persuasion he needs to unify the nation.

A Call for Reality Beyond the Screen

Recent data suggests the President’s office is aware of the friction, with post frequency dropping significantly between June and September. Yet, the underlying strategy remains unchanged. Public sentiment, as measured by recent polling, shows a public deeply divided, with significant portions of the electorate questioning the direction of his governance. The "success story" of his time as a local official in Seongnam, where he could mobilize a base to overcome local administrative hurdles, does not scale linearly to the complex, fractured landscape of a nation that requires broad, inclusive leadership.

Ultimately, the upcoming national elections will serve as the final diagnostic test for this strategy. Voting booths represent the only true metric of public sentiment, far removed from the echo chambers of algorithmic feeds. If the President continues to prioritize the approval of his digital disciples over the quiet, unvocalized concerns of the general public, he risks falling victim to the "curse of success"—remaining tethered to the tactics that won him the office, at the expense of the unity required to govern it effectively.
