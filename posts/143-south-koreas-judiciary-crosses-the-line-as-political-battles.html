South Korea’s Judiciary Crosses the Line as Political Battles Overshadow Diplomacy

September 29, 2026

  • What happened: Critics accuse South Korea's Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae of dragging the judiciary into partisan politics, drawing comparisons to former Board of Audit and Inspection chief Choe Jae-hyeong's controversial transition into conservative politics.
  • What it changes: The politicization of the Supreme Court threatens the core of South Korea’s rule of law, while the current administration faces fierce criticism for dropping the ball on crucial regional diplomacy, such as leveraging relations with Beijing for U.S.-North Korea talks.
  • Who is affected: The South Korean public and legal system bear the brunt of rising institutional instability, which deepens national division and hurts regional security.

The Judiciary's Dangerous Political Turn

South Korea’s legal system is facing a profound crisis of credibility as the nation's top judges increasingly act as political players. Observers point out that recent controversial rulings and maneuvers surrounding the Supreme Court echo the path taken by former top auditor Choe Jae-hyeong, who eventually resigned to run as a conservative politician. Critics argue that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is utilizing judicial authority as a political weapon, turning constitutional principles into tools for partisan warfare.

This blurring of lines between the bench and political ambitions has even brought the extreme notion of impeachment into mainstream discourse. While formal impeachment requires clear and serious constitutional violations—and faces an uphill battle at the Constitutional Court—the mere fact that it is being casually tossed around reveals just how deeply distrusted the courts have become. When the head of the judiciary is suspected of building an independent political route rather than upholding neutrality, the entire foundation of legal fairness collapses.

Foreign Policy Failures and Missed Opportunities

While domestic institutions tear themselves apart in partisan bickering, the government's essential diplomatic duties lie completely neglected. Seoul holds a vital diplomatic card: the ability to encourage Beijing to play a constructive, active role in facilitating North Korea-U.S. summits and reducing peninsula risks. Yet, instead of actively shaping regional peace, the current administration has largely stood by as a passive spectator.

This diplomatic vacuum is not just a missed opportunity; it is a direct threat to national survival. By prioritizing short-term domestic political gains and partisan squabbles over pragmatic, big-picture foreign policy, the administration is inviting greater security anxieties. As long as Seoul ignores its core responsibilities on the international stage to focus on domestic political warfare, the vicious cycle of institutional chaos and public fatigue will only continue.
