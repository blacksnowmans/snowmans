Growing Internal Strife Hits South Korea’s Democratic Party Over President Lee’s Social Media Use

October 30, 2026

Internal friction inside South Korea's ruling Democratic Party is escalating as President Lee Jae-myung’s unfiltered social media posts alienate moderate voters and trigger sharp warnings from within his own party leadership.

  • Presidential dignity questioned: A recent poll reveals 62.5% of South Koreans rate President Lee’s social media activity negatively, criticizing it for lacking the gravity expected of the head of state.
  • Backlash over partisan tone: Senior Democratic Party lawmakers warn that Lee’s communication style risks echoing the hyper-partisan image of progressive writer Rhyu Si-min, shutting out opposing views.
  • Prosecutorial baggage: Lingering anger over past "cabinet investigations" by prosecutors—such as the 2020 SillaJen scandal used to target political figures—continues to fuel the party's push for judicial reform.
  • Controversial merger plans: Proposed unification with the hardline Rebuilding Korea Party is drawing strong pushback from centrist voters and party members who fear losing moderate support.

The Risk of "Unfiltered" Presidential Communication

President Lee Jae-myung’s direct communication strategy, once seen as an asset for mobilizing his core base, is increasingly becoming a political liability. Recent polling by Public Opinion Gongjeong shows that 62.5% of respondents view Lee's social media habits negatively—a disapproval rate that jumps to nearly 70% among voters under 20. Party insiders have openly cautioned that Lee’s late-night posts and use of internet slang undermine presidential dignity, drawing unflattering comparisons to the late-night drinking habits seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term before his impeachment.

Rather than fostering unity, Lee’s rhetoric is driving away crucial centrist support. Party lawmakers have urged the president to broaden his outreach by meeting opposition figures and cabinet experts instead of relying on partisan social media broadcasts. For swing voters in their 30s, effective leadership requires listening to criticism rather than broadcasting a monologue to a partisan echo chamber.

Prosecutorial Reform and the Merger Dilemma

The anxiety inside the ruling party is deeply tied to distrust toward the prosecution service. Party members recall how prosecutors previously used "cabinet investigations"—holding back indictments to deploy them as political leverage—during the 2020 SillaJen financial scandal. In that case, prosecutors squeezed executives to extract damaging testimony against progressive figure Rhyu Si-min following SillaJen's stock crash from a peak market value of 8 trillion won (about $6 billion). To prevent future abuses, Democratic Party lawmakers are now backing reforms such as a "law distortion crime" bill to punish prosecutors who manipulate evidence or abuse power.

At the same time, plans to merge with the Rebuilding Korea Party—a minor progressive party led by Cho Kuk—are exposing deep internal rifts. Polls show 41% of Democratic Party supporters oppose the merger, compared to 32% in favor, while 40% of centrists view the move negatively. Critics argue that absorbing a hardline faction will lock the Democratic Party into ideological rigidity, making it far harder to win over moderate voters ahead of future elections.
