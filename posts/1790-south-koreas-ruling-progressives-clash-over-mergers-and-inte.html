South Korea’s Ruling Progressives Clash Over Mergers and Internal Dissent

November 01, 2026

South Korea’s ruling progressive camp is facing mounting internal friction over factional dissent and a proposed merger with a minor ally, even as the conservative opposition seizes on fresh ethics scandals to mount a counteroffensive.

  • Friction inside the majority: Following sharp public criticism from influential liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min, senior Democratic Party lawmaker Jung Chung-rae urged tolerance by comparing the debate to how the Korean independence movement had multiple factions, while simultaneously setting a 100-day performance review for party leader Kim Min-seok.
  • Merger pushback: While Jung floated a deadline to merge with the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party by next January, its leader Cho Kuk rejected unconditional absorption, instead opening local branch offices to signal his party will field its own candidates.
  • Ethics battle: The main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) launched a fierce offensive against the ruling party after Democratic lawmaker Jang Kyung-tae was indicted on sexual harassment charges.
  • Fleeting cross-party cooperation: Despite bitter rhetoric, Democratic leader Kim Min-seok and PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok struck a rare bipartisan deal to ensure parliamentary support for the 2027 World Youth Day in Seoul.

Strains Inside the Progressive Camp

Power dynamics inside South Korea's governing camp are shifting into a more combative phase. With President Lee Jae-myung in office following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the ruling Democratic Party holds executive and legislative dominance, but internal cohesion is fraying. Lawmaker Jung Chung-rae’s public declaration that leader Kim Min-seok has 100 days to lift party polling and resolve simmering controversies is less a show of patience than a clear warning shot. By noting that he is considering "which banner to carry" after those 100 days, Jung is openly preserving space for an alternative leadership challenge if current poll numbers drag.

Simultaneously, the Democratic Party’s push to swallow Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party by early next year is stalling. Cho has made clear that he will not settle for a rushed union that trades away his party's reform identity for backroom convenience. His focus on grassroots campaigning—including opening a constituency base in Pyeongtaek, south of Seoul—demonstrates that smaller progressive factions intend to keep their leverage rather than be quietly folded into the ruling party machine.

Opposition Counteroffensives and Public Fatigue

For the conservative People Power Party, ethics controversies surrounding governing lawmakers offer a lifeline. Lawmaker Jang Kyung-tae's indictment gives the PPP a sharp weapon to hammer ruling-party hypocrisy and deflect attention from its own post-martial-law crisis. With its own unfavorability rating hovering around 61%, however, the PPP's attacks are primarily aimed at rallying demoralized base voters in traditional strongholds like the Chungcheong provinces rather than expanding its appeal to unaligned moderates.

The result is an uneasy political landscape where both camps appear consumed by internal maneuvering and judicial score-settling. While leaders from both parties can still share a brief smile over international youth festivals, voters face a ruling coalition entangled in premature turf wars and an opposition whose high unfavorability blunts its attempts to hold power to account.
