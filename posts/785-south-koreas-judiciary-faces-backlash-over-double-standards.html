South Korea’s Judiciary Faces Backlash Over "Double Standards" in Parliamentary Audit

October 07, 2026

South Korea’s judiciary is under intense scrutiny following a parliamentary audit that exposed a stark disparity in how the law treats ordinary citizens versus high-ranking judicial officials. Critics, including legal experts, have accused the Supreme Court of maintaining a "caste-like" system where minor offenses by the public are met with harsh penalties while systemic mismanagement and internal corruption among elites go unchecked.

  • Harsh Penalties for the Poor: Lawmakers highlighted past rulings where individuals were fired for petty incidents, such as a bus driver dismissed for misappropriating 800 won (about $0.60) to buy coffee, and logistical workers fined 500,000 won (about $370) for eating snacks worth roughly 1,060 won ($0.80).
  • Internal Misconduct: The audit uncovered that senior judicial figures have been receiving monthly "career allowances"—roughly 2 million to 3 million won ($1,500–$2,200)—without clear regulations or transparent documentation.
  • Institutional Paralysis: Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is facing accusations of "dereliction of duty" for the prolonged delay in filling vacant Supreme Court Justice positions, which critics argue is paralyzing the legal system and harming the public.
  • Accountability Gap: While the judiciary enforces strict standards on the public, the audit revealed a lack of accountability among leadership, with officials shifting blame regarding budget oversight and administrative duties.

A Crisis of Credibility and Institutional Integrity

The core of the frustration lies in the perceived hypocrisy of the South Korean judiciary. During the audit, the contrast between the rigid, unforgiving stance toward "livelihood crimes" and the lax oversight of internal slush funds sparked outrage. Lawmakers compared the undocumented "career allowance" payments to the illegal special activity fund scandals that previously plagued state agencies, noting that the judiciary, which serves as the ultimate arbiter of the law, appears to be operating its own opaque financial structure.

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s management has come under fire for what many describe as a deliberate, politically motivated delay in appointing justices. Legal advocates, including AI attorney Kim Kyung-ho, testified that this failure to fulfill constitutional obligations mirrors the obstructionism seen during the administration of the now-imprisoned former president, Yoon Suk-yeol. By leaving key benches empty, the judiciary is effectively contributing to the very "trial delays" it claims to be fighting, leaving citizens to bear the consequences.

The Path Toward Structural Reform

The atmosphere at the audit was one of profound disillusionment. While the judiciary often cites the "sanctity of law" to justify its rulings against marginalized individuals, its inability to account for its own administrative failures suggests that the institution has lost its moral compass. Many observers feel that the judiciary has become an insular fortress that prioritizes protecting its own interests over the public it is meant to serve.

The testimonies provided during the audit are not merely isolated critiques; they represent a growing demand for a total overhaul of the judicial administrative culture. As long as the system continues to apply a "double standard"—using the law as a shield for the powerful and a weapon against the vulnerable—it will struggle to regain the public trust necessary to function. The evidence brought to light in this audit serves as a sobering reminder that without radical transparency and an end to protected privileges, the judiciary will remain an obstacle to, rather than a guardian of, true justice.
