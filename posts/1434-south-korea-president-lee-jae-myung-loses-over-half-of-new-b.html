South Korea: President Lee Jae-myung Loses Over Half of New Backers in Early Approval Slump

October 24, 2026

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has seen his honeymoon period abruptly unravel just four months into his term, as more than half of the moderate, pragmatic voters who rallied behind him after his election victory have pulled their support amid policy stumbles and bitter factional friction.

  • A steep decline: A panel survey by the left-leaning daily Hankyoreh and the Korean Political Science Association showed President Lee's approval rating tumbling from 66.9% in May to 49.3%, with negative evaluations climbing to 46.5%.
  • An exodus of swing voters: While only 19.8% of traditional progressive loyalists withdrew support, an astonishing 54.8% of "New Lee" voters—moderates and pragmatists who jumped on board after the election—abandoned the administration.
  • Policy flashpoints: The primary catalyst was public blowback against property tax policy shifts and real estate instability (cited by 25.3% of dissatisfied voters), compounded by controversial cabinet appointments and a 21.5-point drop among voters in their 30s.
  • A deep ideological divide: Prosecutorial reform exposed irreconcilable differences: 77.1% of defecting new supporters opposed stripping state prosecutors of supplementary investigative powers, viewing it as disruptive, whereas 53.8% of defecting core loyalists still supported the reform.

Ideological Purists Versus Pragmatic Converts

The swift erosion reveals a fundamental fault line within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Lee entered office with an overwhelming parliamentary majority following the political vacuum left by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached, ousted from office, and subsequently imprisoned. That victory attracted millions of pragmatic, centrist voters looking for competent economic stewardship and living-cost solutions rather than ideological crusade. When the administration ran into immediate turbulence over non-residential property taxes and contentious ministerial picks, those transactional voters felt no loyalty to keep them anchored.

Nowhere is this cultural clash clearer than in the justice system. For the Democratic Party's core progressive base, dismantling prosecutorial overreach remains a sacred historic mandate forged during past political battles. For newer backers—many of whom had previously voted for the conservative People Power Party in local elections—the continued assault on prosecutorial authority looks like petty political score-settling that threatens everyday law and order.

The Danger of Rewarding Sycophancy

Prominent liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min recently sounded the alarm using an analytical framework that divides political actors into value-driven loyalists and transactional opportunists. Rhyu warned that the ruling party has become overrun by self-interested careerists loudly branding themselves as "pro-Lee" simply to secure lucrative nominations for future elections. These fair-weather loyalists silence internal dissent, brand moderate colleagues as traitors, and will inevitably be the first to cast stones the moment the political winds turn cold.

Lee's administration risks learning the hard way that blind loyalty cannot substitute for competence. If the presidential office allows opportunistic partisans to monopolize power while ignoring the pragmatic middle class that delivered its mandate, the administration will find itself increasingly isolated—defended only by a shrinking core whose enthusiasm is steadily cooling in the face of unforced errors.
