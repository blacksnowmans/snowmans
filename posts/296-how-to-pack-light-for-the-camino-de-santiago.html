How to Pack Light for the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

  • The Golden Rule: Keep your total pack weight under 6kg (about 13 lbs), including water and gear. Every extra kilogram punishes your shoulders within hours.
  • Footwear First: Ditch brand-new hiking boots. Wear broken-in shoes half a size larger to account for swelling, and pair them with double-layered socks to prevent blisters.
  • Swap Rain Covers for Ponchos: Backpack rain covers are largely useless if your body gets soaked anyway; invest in a durable, full-body poncho instead.
  • Daily Discipline: Bring only one change of clothes and do laundry every day. Soap can double as shampoo, face wash, and detergent.

Packing for the legendary 800-kilometer (approx. 500-mile) Camino de Santiago pilgrimage across Spain naturally triggers a panic to bring everything. Veteran pilgrims, however, universally agree that the secret to survival is ruthless subtraction. The journey is an exercise in shedding excess, starting with your backpack.

Beyond the physical gear, veteran advice highlights the danger of falling into a psychological trap: matching the pace of strangers. In a hyper-competitive society obsessed with constant productivity and keeping up with peers, it is easy to treat the Camino like a speed run. Yet pushing through pain to hit arbitrary daily milestones invites injury. The true lesson of the trail is learning to walk at your own rhythm—stopping when your body hurts, resting when a village feels right, and trading the anxiety of falling behind for the quiet luxury of space.

Essential Gear and Smart Adjustments

When it comes to comfort, small choices dictate success. Trekking poles are non-negotiable for distributing weight and saving your knees over a month of walking. For downtime at hostels (albergues), skip the flimsy flip-flops and bring sandals with heel straps to properly relax overworked foot muscles. Pack a 40-liter backpack equipped with a sturdy hip belt to shift the load to your pelvis, and toss in a lightweight tote bag for local errands and laundry runs.

On the medical front, pack light: a few hydrocolloid blister pads, basic painkillers, and kinesiology tape for joint stability. Keep your cash in small denominations (5, 10, and 20-euro notes) since small-town shops often cannot break large bills. Ultimately, preparing for the Camino is less about ticking off a list of survival items and more about practicing how you want to face life: traveling light, letting go of artificial pressure, and leaving room for the road to fill in the rest.
