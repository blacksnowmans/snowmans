Why South Korea’s Prosecution Reform is Stalled in a Communication Crisis

September 24, 2026

South Korea’s long-standing push to strip the state prosecution service of its immense investigative powers is failing not because of the goal itself, but because of poor execution, political amateurism, and a media environment that shields the prosecutorial status quo.

  • The Core Issue: Advocates of reform have failed to clearly explain the roadmap for separating investigative and prosecutorial functions to the public.
  • Media Manipulation: Critics argue that mainstream media outlets often frame routine internal personnel shifts as political "retaliation" or "promotions" to protect prosecutorial interests.
  • Structural Entrenchment: The current impasse highlights how deeply the "prosecutorial logic"—a system of concentrated power—is embedded in the country's governance.

A Failure of Strategy and Transparency

The movement to separate investigative and prosecutorial powers, once a flagship goal of liberal reformists, has been marred by a lack of transparency. The administration’s vision—often kept in the dark until the last minute—left the public confused and provided an easy opening for opponents to frame the reforms as politically motivated power grabs. By failing to seek broad social consensus and treating policy shifts as "surprises," reformers alienated the public and invited skepticism regarding their true intentions.

The situation is further complicated by the president’s own mixed messaging. While rhetorically championing the removal of investigative powers, his contradictory statements—citing a need to keep such powers in "small, rare cases"—have undermined the credibility of the entire project. This lack of strategic precision has turned a necessary structural reform into a political liability.

The Media’s Role in Protecting the Status Quo

A significant barrier to reform is the symbiotic relationship between the prosecution and the media. When the administration shifts personnel, the media often creates a "pro-Yoon" (loyal to President Yoon Suk-yeol) vs. "anti-Yoon" narrative. Observers note that these are often standard bureaucratic rotations, yet the press labels them as "demotions" or "purges" to generate outrage. By framing these internal movements as political theater, the media effectively distracts the public from the deeper issue: the unchecked concentration of power within the prosecution service itself.

The reality, according to reform advocates, is that the prosecution is not merely a legal body but a self-sustaining network. Because members of this organization have historically held the power to both investigate and prosecute, they have built a "prosecutorial cartel" that resists any change. As long as this network survives, the reformists' amateurish communication and lack of a coherent, long-term roadmap will keep them on the losing side of this battle. Ultimately, if the goal is to dismantle an entrenched power structure, success will require more than just technical changes—it demands a level of public engagement and strategic clarity that has so far been entirely missing.
