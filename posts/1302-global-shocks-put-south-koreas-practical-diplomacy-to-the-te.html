Global Shocks Put South Korea’s Practical Diplomacy to the Test

October 19, 2026

Rapidly shifting geopolitical currents—from political chaos in Washington and mounting war costs in Ukraine to renewed historical friction with Japan—are forcing South Korea’s Lee Jae-myung administration to double down on a pragmatic, national-interest-driven foreign policy.

  • White House friction and gas waivers: White House Press Secretary Katie Zacharias was ousted just five days into her tenure amid internal power struggles, even as Washington granted last-minute sanctions waivers on Russian diesel and suggested European allies resume Russian gas imports.
  • Escalating Ukraine war costs: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky asked the European Union for an additional €70 billion (about $76 billion) to help cover an estimated €155 billion ($168 billion) war budget for next year, while proposing a three-way summit with the U.S. and Russia at the upcoming G20.
  • Japanese historical provocations: Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi sent a ritual offering to the controversial Yasukuni Shrine, drawing sharp rebukes from South Korea and China, while North Korea pressed ahead with hypersonic missile tests.
  • Green policy rollbacks: Germany and France agreed to soften the EU’s proposed 2035 ban on internal combustion engine vehicles, signaling a broader retreat from aggressive global environmental mandates.

Navigating Washington Turbulence and Energy Shockwaves

Unpredictability in Washington poses an immediate test for South Korean foreign policy planners. Internal infighting inside the White House, punctuated by the high-profile firing of its press secretary after less than a week, threatens the consistency of U.S. diplomatic commitments. For Seoul, Washington's sudden policy shifts—such as waiving sanctions on Russian fuel to curb inflation and suggesting Europe reopen gas lines—underscore how quickly national self-interest can redefine international alliances. As global commodity markets fluctuate, South Korea must prioritize securing its own energy supply chains and shielding domestic consumers from price volatility.

At the same time, the prolonged conflict in Eastern Europe continues to ripple through global markets. President Zelensky’s massive funding requests highlight growing fatigue among European nations dealing with high inflation and domestic political pushback. With Russia launching targeted strikes against Ukrainian energy infrastructure ahead of winter, the geopolitical stalemate risks worsening global supply disruptions. For South Korea, managing these indirect economic shockwaves requires balancing international solidarity with cold-eyed economic realism.

Defending Historical Principles while Adapting to Market Shifts

In Northeast Asia, historical grievances have flared up once again. Prime Minister Takaichi’s ritual offering to Yasukuni Shrine—a site that honors Japan's war dead, including convicted Class-A war criminals—drew immediate condemnation from Seoul's Foreign Ministry. Under President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea is taking a firm, principled stance on historical matters, marking a clear departure from the submissive approach seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency. By maintaining clear boundaries on history while keeping economic channels open, the Lee administration seeks to project stability without sacrificing national dignity, even as North Korea continues to advance its military capabilities.

Meanwhile, policy shifts in Europe present both challenges and opportunities for Korean industry. The agreement by Germany and France to ease the EU’s 2035 internal combustion engine phase-out shows that economic realities are forcing major powers to adjust their green roadmaps. For South Korean automakers and battery manufacturers, this regulatory detour demands strategic flexibility. Rather than relying entirely on rapid electric vehicle adoption abroad, domestic firms will need to maintain diversified portfolios—including hybrid and hydrogen technologies—to stay competitive in a rapidly shifting regulatory environment.
